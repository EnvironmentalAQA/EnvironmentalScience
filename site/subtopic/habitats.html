Question 110 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 50-52 · HAB-01
[4 marks]01.1Describe the ecological features of temperate broadleaf woodland and explain how woodland floor plants such as bluebells are adapted to them.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why an ancient woodland (existing before 1600) usually has higher biodiversity than a recently planted woodland.
[3 marks]01.3Describe three threats to UK broadleaf woodland.
Show mark scheme
01.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1No major temperature extremes; regular water supply with no pronounced dry season; deep fertile soils built by leaf litter and root binding; strong seasonality
  • 1Deciduous canopy present spring to autumn shades the floor during the best growing temperatures
  • 1Bluebells, snowdrops, lesser celandine grow and flower early in spring before the trees leaf
  • 1Winter food shortage causes animals to hibernate (dormice), store food or migrate
01.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Long time for species to colonise; some woodland plants colonise very slowly so their presence (ancient woodland indicators) shows the wood is old
  • 1Natural woodland has trees of all ages with clearings where old trees died, giving varied light and dead wood habitats
  • 1A new plantation has a single-age dense canopy with low light and few niches until trees start to die (over 200 years)
01.3 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Clearance for farmland, plantations, urban development, roads, mineral extraction
  • 1Fragmentation isolating remaining woods so populations cannot move between them and are vulnerable
  • 1Management change: end of coppicing so woods become uniform and dark; replacement by conifer monocultures
  • 1Overgrazing by deer preventing regeneration; introduced pathogens (ash dieback)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 50-52; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Question 310 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 52-55 · HAB-03
[4 marks]03.1Explain why tropical rainforests have such high biodiversity.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why rainforest species may be particularly vulnerable to environmental change.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why rainforest plants and animals such as frogs are a rich source of medicines.
[2 marks]03.4Explain the importance of tropical rainforest for the hydrological cycle and for soils.
Show mark scheme
03.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Stable tropical climate over long periods gave time for species to evolve and adapt to local abiotic and biotic factors; many rare species with small ranges
  • 1High light, warm temperatures and high rainfall all year allow continuous photosynthesis and growth, supporting rich food webs with no seasonal food shortage (no hibernation / migration)
  • 1Stratification (emergent, canopy, understorey, shrub, ground layers) and epiphytes create many niches; intense competition for light drives specialisation
  • 1Biotic pressures (predation, inter-species relationships) drive evolution of specialised pollination / seed dispersal relationships; plants flower and fruit at different times
03.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Adapted to stable conditions so they have narrow ranges of tolerance; a small change can move conditions outside the range
  • 1Many species are rare with small ranges / small populations
03.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1No cold winter kills herbivores so plants defend themselves with toxic chemicals (alkaloids) that are medicinally active in controlled doses (quinine, diosgenin, curare)
  • 1Animals without teeth / claws (frogs, reptiles) rely on chemical defences or venoms (fer-de-lance venom - captopril)
03.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Over half the Amazon's rainfall comes from transpiration within the forest; clearance reduces downwind rainfall
  • 1Rapid decay recycles nutrients so soils are shallow / poor; canopy and roots prevent erosion of the thin soil - clearance leads to rapid erosion and infertility
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 52-55; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Question 610 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-60 · HAB-06
[4 marks]06.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows reefs to support a high biomass in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[3 marks]06.2Describe the abiotic conditions needed by reef-building corals and explain why these make reefs vulnerable.
[3 marks]06.3Explain three ways in which coral reefs are important to humans.
Show mark scheme
06.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside polyp cells (up to 30 000 per mm2)
  • 1They use the polyp's CO2 and excretory wastes (nitrogen, phosphorus) for photosynthesis and growth, so nutrients are recycled efficiently
  • 1They supply the polyp with glucose and oxygen - over 95% of its food energy; the polyp provides a protected habitat and nutrients
  • 1Polyps also trap plankton with cilia and stinging nematocysts; surplus CO2 is used to build the calcium carbonate skeleton
06.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1High light (shallow, clear water) for the algae; warm stable temperatures (about 18-30 °C); low turbidity; constant salinity; well-oxygenated water
  • 1Found only where all these coincide (about 1% of ocean area)
  • 1Small changes (warming, sediment, freshwater runoff) move conditions outside the narrow range of tolerance
06.3 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Fisheries: support over a quarter of marine species including fish, lobsters, conch
  • 1Erosion protection: complex structure dissipates wave energy protecting low coral islands; coral sand builds shores
  • 1Medicinal discoveries: AZT and Ara-C from sponges, dolastatin from sea hares, bryostatin from Bugula, trabectedin from sea squirts, secosteroids, coral for bone grafts
  • 1Climate control: carbonate skeletons sequester CO2
  • 1Tourism (diving, snorkelling)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 57-60; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Importance: cover 1% of ocean area but support over a quarter of marine species; fisheries (fish, lobsters, conch); medicines (AZT, Ara-C, dolastatin, secosteroids, bryostatin, trabectedin, bone grafts); climate control as carbon sink; erosion protection of low-lying islands; tourism income
  • Threats: physical damage (anchors, divers, litter, coral extraction), collection of ornaments (CITES Appendix II), sedimentation from rivers and coastal development, pollution (oil, nutrients causing algal shading, sewage, pesticides, sunscreen), ocean acidification, bleaching from warming, overfishing (giant triton / crown of thorns starfish), introduced species (red lionfish), loss of mangroves and seagrass nurseries
  • Conservation: marine protected areas / national parks (Great Barrier Reef, Belize, Chagos), mooring buoys, diver education, No Take Zones which can increase catches elsewhere, sustainable ecotourism and fishing, protection of mangroves and seagrass, erosion and pollution control, planning control of ports and resorts
  • Fishing controls: closed seasons, minimum and maximum catchable sizes, bans on dynamite, trawling and small-mesh nets
  • Evaluation: local measures cannot control global threats (warming, acidification) which need international action; enforcement in remote areas is difficult; conflicts with local livelihoods; large vs small NTZs
  • Justified conclusion
