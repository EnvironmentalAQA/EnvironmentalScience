Question 110 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 394-395, 401-402 · RM-05

A student used 0.5 m x 0.5 m grid quadrats to survey ground flora in two meadows, one grazed and one ungrazed. Table 1 shows the results for the grazed meadow (10 quadrats).

Table 1
SpeciesNumber of individuals
Ribwort plantain48
Perennial ryegrass120
White clover36
Daisy24
Creeping buttercup12
[2 marks]01.1Explain why a 0.5 m x 0.5 m quadrat was chosen rather than a 10 cm x 10 cm or 100 m x 100 m quadrat.
[3 marks]01.2Calculate Simpson's Index of Biodiversity (D) for the grazed meadow using D = N(N-1) / Σn(n-1). Show your working.
[2 marks]01.3The ungrazed meadow had D = 1.8 with the same number of species. Explain what this shows.
[2 marks]01.4Explain why a point quadrat would be better than a frame quadrat in tall vegetation.
[1 mark]01.5Give one limitation of using the DAFOR abundance scale.
Show mark scheme
01.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Quadrat size depends on the size / spacing of the organisms: 0.5 m x 0.5 m is typical for ground flora
  • 110 cm x 10 cm is for lichens (too small to contain a representative sample of plants); 100 m x 100 m is for trees (impractical, too much work)
01.2 3 marks
A correct final answer (with unit where asked) scores full marks. If the answer is wrong, award 1 mark for each correct step of working shown.
  • 1N = 240; N(N-1) = 240 x 239 = 57 360
  • 1Σn(n-1) = 48x47 + 120x119 + 36x35 + 24x23 + 12x11 = 2256 + 14280 + 1260 + 552 + 132 = 18 480
  • 1D = 57 360 / 18 480 = 3.10 (accept 3.1)
01.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Lower D means lower biodiversity: the ungrazed meadow is dominated by one or a few species (eg tall grass) with the others rare (less even)
  • 1Grazing (plagioclimax management) prevents dominance by vigorous species so abundance is more evenly spread
01.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A frame quadrat flattens tall plants, making percentage cover estimates inaccurate and hiding shorter plants
  • 1Point quadrat pins are lowered through the vegetation; each touch counts (10 pins = 10% each) without flattening
01.5 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Subjective judgement varies between recorders; results are not quantitative so statistical analysis is not possible (but quick for many areas)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 394-395, 401-402; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 210 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 395-396, 415-416 · RM-06

A student compared the invertebrates in a stream above and below a sewage outfall using kick sampling.

[3 marks]02.1Describe how kick sampling is carried out so that the samples from the two sites are comparable.
[3 marks]02.2Give three limitations of kick sampling.
[2 marks]02.3Explain how a Surber sampler improves on kick sampling.
[2 marks]02.4Explain why the invertebrates found give a better indication of long-term sewage pollution than a single measurement of dissolved oxygen.
Show mark scheme
02.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Net held with its straight edge on the river bed facing the current (open to the flow)
  • 1The bed in front is disturbed by kicking / scraping a boot for a standard number of kicks and time (eg 30 s)
  • 1Same substrate type, water depth and flow rate at both sites; same net mesh; repeat samples; invertebrates identified with a key and counted
02.2 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Difficult to standardise the vigour / area kicked
  • 1Buried organisms or those fixed to stones are not dislodged
  • 1Some organisms swim and avoid the net
  • 1Flow rate varies and affects capture
  • 1Fragile organisms destroyed
02.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A fixed area of bed is enclosed by a frame so the sampled area is standardised; side nets reduce escapes
  • 1But only works in fast enough flow and is hard to set on rough substrates / deep water
02.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Pollutant / oxygen levels fluctuate (eg with flow, time of day) so one reading may miss a pollution event
  • 1Sensitive taxa (stonefly, mayfly larvae) disappear and tolerant ones (midge larvae, hoglouse) remain, integrating conditions over weeks / months (biotic index)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 395-396, 415-416; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 310 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 397-399 · RM-07
[6 marks]03.1Complete Table 1 to show a suitable sampling technique for each group of organisms and one limitation of the technique.
Table 1
OrganismsTechniqueOne limitation
Invertebrates active on the ground surface at night
Invertebrates living in leaf litter
Invertebrates on tree branches
[2 marks]03.2Explain why a light trap cannot be used to prove that a moth species is absent from a site.
[2 marks]03.3Explain how a motor-driven suction sampler can give a population density, whereas a pooter cannot.
Show mark scheme
03.1 6 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Ground surface: pitfall trap (container sunk level with the surface, cover, checked after 24 h) - only mobile surface animals; trapped predators eat others; preservative attracts / repels; escapes
  • 2Leaf litter: Tullgren funnel (sample on mesh under a light; heat / light drive invertebrates down into a container for a standard time) - only species repelled by heat / light; some die or stop moving; large ones cannot pass the mesh
  • 2Branches: beating tray (branch beaten a set number of times over a sheet) - hard to standardise vigour; some species not dislodged / fly away; high branches unreachable
03.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Moths only fly as adults for part of the year and activity depends on weather / season, so a species may be present but not caught
  • 1Brightness / wavelength of the lamp affects attractiveness so catches vary between traps
03.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The area of ground covered by the suction pipe is known so invertebrates per m2 can be calculated
  • 1A pooter is only a handling / collection device for individuals already found, not a sampling method
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 397-399; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 410 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 401-402 · RM-08

A student estimated the population of ground beetles in a field using the Lincoln Index. 60 beetles were caught in pitfall traps, marked with a spot of paint and released. Three days later 45 beetles were caught, of which 9 were marked.

[2 marks]04.1Calculate the estimated population size.
[4 marks]04.2State four assumptions of the Lincoln Index method.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how the estimate would be affected if the paint made the beetles more likely to be eaten by birds.
[2 marks]04.4Suggest why the second sample was taken after three days rather than the next day or after a month.
Show mark scheme
04.1 2 marks
A correct final answer (with unit where asked) scores full marks. If the answer is wrong, award 1 mark for each correct step of working shown.
  • 1(60 x 45) / 9
  • 1= 300
04.2 4 marks
Any 4 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 4.
  • 1Marked individuals mix freely / randomly with the rest of the population
  • 1No births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples
  • 1Marking does not affect survival or behaviour (eg make them more visible to predators)
  • 1Marks do not wear off / fall off
  • 1All individuals have the same chance of being caught (traps do not attract or repel marked animals)
04.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Fewer marked beetles would survive to be recaptured
  • 1So the proportion marked in the second sample would be too low and the population would be overestimated
04.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Enough time for marked beetles to mix with the population
  • 1Not so long that births / deaths / migration or loss of marks change the population
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 401-402; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 510 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 407-410, 413 · RM-09
[3 marks]05.1Explain why electronic meters must be calibrated before use and describe how a pH meter is calibrated.
[3 marks]05.2Describe how wind velocity measurements with an anemometer should be standardised.
[2 marks]05.3Describe how a whirling hygrometer is used to measure humidity.
[2 marks]05.4Describe two methods of measuring nitrate concentration in water.
Show mark scheme
05.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Readings drift so they may not be accurate; calibration adjusts the meter to match a known standard so results are accurate and comparable between meters / occasions
  • 1pH meter placed in buffer solutions of known pH (eg 4, 7, 10) and adjusted to read correctly
  • 1If a meter cannot be adjusted, record the percentage error and correct the results
05.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Same height above the ground
  • 1No obstacles to air flow; impeller type facing into the wind
  • 1Axis of rotation horizontal (impeller) or vertical (cup); same time period / averaging
05.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Two thermometers, one with a wet bulb, are spun until the wet bulb stops cooling
  • 1Evaporation cools the wet bulb more when humidity is low; the temperature difference is compared with a table to find humidity
05.4 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Test strips with reagent bands compared with a colour chart (semi-quantitative)
  • 1Electronic colourimeter: reagent added and colour intensity measured at a specific wavelength
  • 1Ion-selective electrode measuring electrode potential (other ions can interfere)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 407-410, 413; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 610 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1-3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 410-413, 417 · RM-10
[3 marks]06.1Describe how soil texture is determined using a stack of sieves.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why a soil auger gives a more representative sample than a trowel.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why the organic matter content will be overestimated if a soil sample is heated above 550 °C.
[3 marks]06.4Describe how a turbidimeter measures water turbidity and explain how quantitative results are obtained.
Show mark scheme
06.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Sieves assembled with the coarsest mesh on top; a dried, crushed soil sample is placed in the top sieve
  • 1Stack shaken (about 2 minutes) until no more particles pass; each fraction weighed
  • 1Percentages of sand (0.02-2 mm), silt (0.002-0.02 mm) and clay (base pan, below 0.002 mm) calculated; material over 2 mm (stones, twigs) is not soil; plotted on a soil triangle
06.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Auger collects a core of known cross-sectional area and depth
  • 1So samples from different sites are directly comparable (also needed for bulk density = dry mass / volume)
06.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Minerals such as calcium carbonate break down releasing carbon dioxide
  • 1So mass is lost that is not organic matter
06.4 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A beam of light passes through the sample in a dark chamber; a light sensor at 90° measures light scattered by suspended particles - more turbid, more scatter
  • 1Standardise bulb brightness / colour, distance and container
  • 1A calibration curve is produced using samples with known amounts of suspended matter so readings convert to concentrations; Secchi discs / turbidity bottles give only semi-quantitative results
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 410-413, 417; AQA spec 3.7.2.1-3.7.2.2.
Question 710 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 416-418 · RM-11

A student investigated the effect of pH on the germination of cress seeds.

[2 marks]07.1Explain why cress is a suitable species for this investigation but the results may not apply directly to natural ecosystems.
[2 marks]07.2Describe how the student should decide the range of pH values to test.
[4 marks]07.3Give three variables that should be standardised and explain how long the experiment should continue.
[2 marks]07.4The student wants to compare the effect of nutrient concentration on the growth of duckweed. Explain why leaf area is a better measure of growth than fresh mass.
Show mark scheme
07.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Small seeds, genetically uniform, high and rapid germination rate so results are quick and reliable
  • 1Wild species have different ranges of tolerance so cress results cannot be applied directly
07.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Preliminary study with widely spaced pH values to find where germination occurs
  • 1Final range from below the lowest pH giving germination to above the highest, with closer intervals
07.3 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Temperature (constant, natural germination temperature)
  • 1Light, water volume, number of seeds per dish, seed source
  • 1Growth medium (filter paper; soil would alter pH); nutrients not needed for germination
  • 1Continue until it is clear that ungerminated seeds will not germinate, so that a delay in germination is not mistaken for failure
07.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Surface water affects fresh mass and drying damages the plants; dry mass requires killing the plants so the same plants cannot be re-measured
  • 1Leaf area is a reasonable estimate of biomass that allows repeated measurement of the same plants over time
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 416-418; AQA spec 3.7.2.2.
