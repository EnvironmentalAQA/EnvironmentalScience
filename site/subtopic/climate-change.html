[3 marks]01.1Explain the natural greenhouse effect.
[3 marks]01.2Use Table 1 to explain why carbon dioxide is considered the most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas even though methane and CFCs absorb far more infrared per molecule.

Show mark scheme
01.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Visible light passes through the atmosphere easily and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, which warms and emits infrared
  • 1Greenhouse gases (naturally water vapour and CO2) absorb infrared better than the average for the atmosphere, converting it to heat and re-emitting some back to the surface

Question 410 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.2.2.3 · Genn pp. 96-97 · CC-04
[2 marks]04.1Describe the two processes that drive the North Atlantic Conveyor.
[4 marks]04.2Explain how global climate change could weaken the North Atlantic Conveyor and describe the possible consequence for the climate of the UK.
[3 marks]04.3Explain why the effects of climate change on UK temperatures are difficult to predict.
[1 mark]04.4Describe how deep ocean currents are monitored.
Show mark scheme
04.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Friction with the prevailing south-westerly winds drags surface water north-eastwards from the tropical Atlantic
  • 1Water in the NE Atlantic cools, becomes denser (and more saline as ice forms) and sinks, drawing warm surface water in to replace it; the cold water returns southwards at depth (thermohaline circulation)
04.2 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Higher temperatures melt land ice on Greenland which flows into the North Atlantic
  • 1The fresh water dilutes the seawater, reducing its salinity and density
  • 1Less dense water sinks less easily so the flow rate of the current is reduced
  • 1Less heat is transported to NW Europe so the UK could become colder (like Moscow / Churchill at the same latitude) even while global temperatures rise
04.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Several interconnected systems act at once over different timescales: jet stream changes may raise temperatures, a slowing Conveyor may lower them, greenhouse gases raise them
  • 1Natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) can hide or exaggerate anthropogenic change; individual events do not show trends
  • 1Time delays between cause and effect (oceans take a long time to warm because of volume and heat capacity); incomplete understanding of the processes
04.4 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Argo floats sink to a set depth for about 10 days, then surface and transmit temperature, salinity and position data; the sequence of positions shows current direction and speed (surface currents also by satellites and buoys)

Question 510 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 100-102 · CC-05
[3 marks]05.1Explain why it is difficult to be certain that a particular flood or heatwave is caused by anthropogenic climate change.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why historic temperature records collected in towns may overestimate global warming.
[5 marks]05.3Describe how each of the following sources of proxy data is used to estimate past climates and give one limitation.
Show mark scheme
05.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The climate naturally fluctuates (solar output, Earth's orbit) so extreme events can occur without human influence
  • 1An individual event, or even several, does not show a trend; the change may be in the frequency of events which can only be assessed years later
  • 1Changes occur at different time and spatial scales (local storm vs global trend); interconnected systems make attribution complex
05.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Towns have a heat-island effect (heat emissions, dark surfaces absorbing light)
  • 1As the town grows around the weather station the warming recorded is partly local, not global; older equipment / sparse global coverage also reduce reliability
05.3 5 marks
Up to 5 marks in total; award marks in proportion to the completeness of each point.
  • up to 2Tree rings: width of each annual ring shows the growth rate, indicating temperature / rainfall in that year; rings easily dated by counting; limitation: growth also affected by other factors (water, pests, shading), only covers the life of the tree / limited regions
  • up to 2Ice cores: annual snow layers compact into ice; air bubbles trapped in the ice give the CO2 (and methane) concentration when trapped; oxygen isotope ratios indicate temperature; up to 800 000 years (Dome C); limitation: deep layers compressed / less distinct, dating needs radio-isotope analysis and volcanic ash layers; only polar regions
  • up to 2Others accepted: coral growth rings; pollen in lake sediments dated by radiocarbon

07.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Negative feedback: a change causes other changes that reduce the rate or size of the original change, re-establishing equilibrium
  • 1Positive feedback: a change causes other changes that increase the original change
07.2 4 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Increased low-level cloud: higher temperatures increase evaporation and condensation, producing more cloud; clouds have a high albedo so more sunlight is reflected and warming is reduced
  • 2Increased photosynthesis: higher temperatures and CO2 increase photosynthesis, removing CO2; if stored in woody tissue atmospheric CO2 rises less
07.3 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Permafrost: frozen waterlogged Arctic soils contain dead organic matter that decomposed anaerobically producing methane trapped in the frozen ground
  • 1Warming thaws the permafrost, releasing methane (a powerful greenhouse gas) and allowing further decomposition, causing more warming
  • 1Methane hydrate: methane from decomposed organic matter in deep sea sediments is held as a solid under high pressure and low temperature
  • 1If deep water warms the hydrate melts, releasing methane into the atmosphere and causing further warming
07.4 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Reduced albedo as snow / ice melts
  • 1Ocean acidification reducing coral survival and carbon sequestration
  • 1Increased forest and peat fires releasing CO2
  • 1Faster decomposition of soil organic matter releasing CO2
  • 1Increased water vapour (a greenhouse gas) in warmer air
  • 1Increased cirrus (high) cloud which traps infrared
07.5 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A point at which natural processes triggered by human-caused warming become self-sustaining / unstoppable
  • 1So that stopping the original human activity would no longer stop climate change (eg runaway permafrost melt, methane hydrate release, forest fires)

Question 810 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 90-92 · CC-08
[6 marks]08.1Explain how climate change may affect the survival of species. Use named examples.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why a species living in an interdependent community may be unable to move to a new area even if suitable conditions exist there.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why polar regions warming faster than the equator affects the weather in the UK.
Show mark scheme
08.1 6 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Direct effect of abiotic change: species near the edge of their range of tolerance (temperature, water) may die out locally, eg sundews if marshes dry, frogs if climate drier; beech (shallow roots) suffers in drought more than oak
  • 1Changes in timing: earlier flowering / emergence may separate flowers from pollinators; plant toxins building up earlier may kill caterpillars; warmer winters disturb dormouse hibernation so they use fat reserves and starve
  • 1Biotic changes: food supply, predators, disease vectors (mosquitoes / malaria spreading), pollinators and seed dispersers may change
  • 1Distribution changes: species may colonise newly suitable areas (triton molluscs around the UK) but slow-growing species (trees) may disappear from old ranges faster than they colonise; suitable areas or biological corridors may not exist; human land use blocks movement
  • 1Population fragmentation: rising seas or upward retreat to mountains isolate populations; divided gene pools increase inbreeding; small populations may not be viable; no repopulation from elsewhere
  • 1Some species benefit (bats: warmer winters, more insects) but wetter stormier weather reduces feeding time
08.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1All the species it depends on (food plants, pollinators, prey, mycorrhizae) will not move at the same speed
  • 1Less mobile species (plants, non-flying animals) cannot cross unsuitable areas without a biological corridor
08.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The temperature difference between mid-latitude and polar air drives the jet streams; a smaller difference makes the jet stream slower and more meandering (Rossby waves)
  • 1Weather systems stall over an area, giving prolonged drought or rainfall / floods; unusually cold or warm air carried north or south

Question 1010 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 89-107 · CC-10
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the success of the methods and strategies used to reduce global climate change.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by a carbon footprint.

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Control of greenhouse gases: energy conservation (transport, buildings, industry), renewable and nuclear energy replacing fossil fuels, CCS, reduced deforestation and afforestation (carbon sequestration), methane capture from landfill / digesters, HFC controls, changes in agriculture
  • Geoengineering: white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - largely untried, possible unpredictable consequences
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement - targets, but non-binding / uneven participation; carbon trading and taxes; comparison of per capita carbon footprints between countries
  • Evidence of limited success: global CO2 concentrations still rising (about 280 ppm pre-industrial to over 410 ppm); temperatures up about 1 °C; emissions in some developed countries falling but rising in developing economies

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Strategies fall into control of greenhouse gases, geoengineering, international agreements and adaptation. Emissions are cut by energy conservation in transport, buildings and industry, by replacing fossil fuels with renewables and nuclear, by carbon capture and storage, by reducing deforestation and planting forests, and by capturing methane from landfill. Geoengineering ideas - white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - remain largely untried with unpredictable side effects. Adaptation, such as flood defences and managed retreat, reduces impacts but not the cause.

The Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement set targets, but they are weak or non-binding and participation is uneven; carbon trading and taxes exist but per capita footprints still differ enormously between countries. The evidence of success is limited: atmospheric CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 410 ppm and temperature by about 1 °C, and although emissions are falling in some developed countries they are rising in developing economies.

The difficulty is that fossil fuels underpin economies, so control demands lifestyle change, cost, and political agreement between nations with different priorities, while time delays and natural fluctuations mask the effect of any action. By contrast the Montreal Protocol succeeded because ozone was a substitution problem. The judgement must be partial success: the technology exists and regional reductions prove it works, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise, and tipping points make the delay dangerous.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Methods to reduce climate change include using renewable energy such as wind and solar instead of fossil fuels, saving energy, and planting trees to absorb carbon dioxide. International agreements like the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement have set targets for countries to reduce emissions. However carbon dioxide levels are still rising, so the methods have not been very successful so far. This is because countries rely on fossil fuels and do not always keep to the agreements. More needs to be done.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 covers the main strategies and reaches a conclusion, but with no quantitative evidence (280 to 410 ppm, 1 °C) and no mention of geoengineering or adaptation.
  • Level 3 explains why success is limited (economic dependence, time lags, natural variability, political disagreement) rather than just stating that it is.
  • The Montreal comparison and the tipping-point point give Level 3 the breadth and judgement the top band requires.
