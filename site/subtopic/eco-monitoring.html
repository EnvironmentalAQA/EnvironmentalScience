Question 110 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.5 · Genn pp. 393-394, 400-402 · MON-01

A conservation team is planning to protect a population of an endangered antelope in a reserve.

[8 marks]01.1Explain why each of the following features of the population should be measured before a conservation strategy is decided.
[2 marks]01.2Describe how the population size of the antelope could be estimated.
Show mark scheme
01.1 8 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Size: shows whether the population is viable / how urgent action is; sets a baseline to judge success; needed to estimate carrying capacity and sustainable off-take
  • 2Distribution: shows which habitats / areas are used and whether the population is fragmented into non-viable isolated groups needing corridors; identifies key areas to protect
  • 2Survival rate (mortality): identifies the life stage and causes of death (predation, disease, poaching) so management targets the limiting factor
  • 2Age structure: many young indicates a growing population; few young / ageing population indicates breeding failure or high juvenile mortality even if adults are numerous; k-selected species need breeding adults protected
01.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Direct counts from vehicles / aircraft / drones along transects, or camera traps with individual recognition
  • 1Sub-sample counts in known areas scaled up to the whole reserve; mark-release-recapture (Lincoln index) for smaller species
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 393-394, 400-402; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.5.
Question 210 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.6 · Genn pp. 402-406 · MON-02
[8 marks]02.1Explain how each of the following new technologies improves the validity of ecological research.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why new technologies allow the collection of 'more representative' data.
Show mark scheme
02.1 8 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Tracking: continuous data on movements, territory size, migration routes and habitat use without disturbance, over large areas and long periods; acoustic tags in seawater; geolocators for small birds; more representative than occasional sightings
  • 2DNA: identifies individuals, gene pool size / inbreeding and regional populations (tracing ivory, timber, fish); eDNA detects rare or hidden aquatic species (great crested newt, invasive carp) from shed cells without capture
  • 2Image recognition: software identifies individuals from unique markings (tiger stripes, whale shark spots, fin damage) in camera-trap / drone / satellite images, giving population size, lifespan, social structure from large image sets quickly and objectively
  • 2Acoustic: bat detectors, hydrophones and sonograms record species that are hard to see (bats, cetaceans, insects), giving presence, abundance and activity continuously, day and night
02.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Data collected continuously / over large areas / at night rather than at a few times and places a researcher can visit
  • 1Less disturbance so animals behave naturally; larger sample sizes; objective rather than subjective identification
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 402-406; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.6.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

New technologies have transformed what can be monitored and how often. Image databases of unique markings - tiger stripes, whale-shark spots, dolphin fin scars - let individuals be recognised, giving territory size, movements, lifespan and social structure without repeated capture, though only for species with unique features. Motion-sensitive and infrared cameras and CCTV record activity day and night with far less disturbance than observers, especially at nests.

Genetics adds detection where nothing can be seen: eDNA in water reveals great crested newts or invasive carp from shed cells, and DNA databases identify which regional population timber, ivory or fish came from, supporting enforcement. Bat detectors and sonograms give presence, abundance and activity of species heard but not seen. Position monitoring uses radio, GPS and acoustic transmitters, or tiny geolocator tags for birds too small to carry a transmitter, while data recorders on drones, AUVs, balloons, aircraft, satellites or the animals themselves collect abiotic data. Satellites map land use and deforestation in visible light, vegetation density and temperature in infrared, and wind, waves, sea level and oil slicks with radar that works through cloud.

The gains are coverage, continuity and reduced disturbance. The limits are cost, battery life and weight, the need to calibrate sensors and ground-truth remote data, ethical questions about tagging, and the fact that many species still require traditional methods and indirect evidence. New technology therefore complements rather than replaces fieldwork, but for rare, remote or nocturnal species it has made effective conservation possible where it was not before.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

New technologies help monitor wildlife. Cameras can take pictures of animals at night. GPS collars can track where animals go. Satellites can see deforestation from space. DNA can tell which animals are present. Bat detectors can hear bats. These help conservation because we know where animals are and how many there are. They can be expensive though.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 lists technologies with no detail of how they work or what data they give; Level 3 links each to the information it provides (territory size, presence, activity).
  • Level 3's evaluation considers several limitations (calibration, ground-truthing, ethics, species coverage) and reaches a balanced judgement; Level 1 has one word - 'expensive'.
  • Structure: Level 3 groups by imagery, genetics, sound, position and remote sensing; the list in Level 1 has no organisation.
