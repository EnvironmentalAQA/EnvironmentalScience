Question 110 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 148-149 · BGC-01

Table 1 shows the mass of carbon in the main reservoirs of the carbon cycle.

Table 1
ReservoirMass of carbon / billion tonnes
Atmosphere (CO2)700
Living organisms470
Dead organic matter (soil)3 700
Surface ocean waters500
Deep ocean (dissolved)35 000
Fossil fuels10 000
Sedimentary carbonate rocks20 000 000
[1 mark]01.1Express the mass of carbon in the atmosphere in standard form, in tonnes.
[2 marks]01.2Calculate the percentage increase in atmospheric carbon that would result from burning 10% of the fossil fuel reservoir, assuming all the carbon remained in the atmosphere.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why the sedimentary carbonate rocks, although by far the largest reservoir, have little effect on atmospheric CO2 concentrations.
[3 marks]01.4Describe how carbon moves from the atmosphere into sedimentary carbonate rocks.
[2 marks]01.5Explain why aerobic respiration returns carbon to the atmosphere as CO2 but anaerobic respiration returns it mainly as methane.
Show mark scheme
01.1 1 mark
1 mark for the correct answer.
  • 17 x 1011 tonnes
01.2 2 marks
A correct final answer (with unit where asked) scores full marks. If the answer is wrong, award 1 mark for each correct step of working shown.
  • 110% of 10 000 = 1000 billion tonnes
  • 11000 / 700 x 100 = 143% (accept 142.9)
01.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Carbon is transferred into and out of rocks extremely slowly (fossilisation / sedimentation, weathering, volcanic release)
  • 1Residence time is millions of years so the flux to the atmosphere is tiny compared with respiration / photosynthesis / combustion
01.4 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1CO2 dissolves in the sea (forming carbonic acid / hydrogen carbonate ions) and is absorbed by phytoplankton through photosynthesis
  • 1Organisms such as molluscs, corals and planktonic organisms build calcium carbonate shells / exoskeletons
  • 1When they die the shells sink and sediment on the seabed, becoming limestone / chalk over long periods
01.5 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break down organic compounds completely to CO2 (and water), releasing much more energy
  • 1Without oxygen, anaerobic organisms only partly break down organic compounds, releasing carbon as methane (CH4), which is later oxidised to CO2 in the atmosphere
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 148-149; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.
Question 210 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 150-151 · BGC-02
[6 marks]02.1Explain how each of the following human activities changes the amount of carbon in the atmosphere.
[3 marks]02.2Give three anaerobic environments created by human activities that release methane.
[1 mark]02.3Explain how rising atmospheric CO2 causes ocean acidification.
Show mark scheme
02.1 6 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Ploughing: increases the oxygen supply to soil decomposers so aerobic decomposition of dead organic matter is faster; carbon in the soil DOM store falls and atmospheric CO2 rises
  • 2Draining: waterlogged anaerobic conditions preserved dead organic matter (peat) and produced methane slowly; drainage makes the soil aerobic so stored carbon is rapidly decomposed to CO2 (though methane release stops)
  • 2Marine pollution: kills phytoplankton so less dissolved CO2 is absorbed by photosynthesis and less carbon sinks to sediments in dead plankton; less CO2 then dissolves from the atmosphere to replace it, so atmospheric CO2 rises
02.2 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Rice paddy fields
  • 1Landfill sites
  • 1Anaerobic sediments in reservoirs
  • 1Livestock intestines (cattle)
  • 1Manure / slurry stores
02.3 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1More CO2 dissolves in the sea, forming carbonic acid which dissociates into hydrogen carbonate and hydrogen ions; more H+ lowers the pH
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 150-151; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.
Question 310 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 151-152 · BGC-03
[3 marks]03.1Explain why planting a new forest only sequesters carbon for a limited period, and how the storage can be extended.
[4 marks]03.2Compare pre-combustion and post-combustion methods of carbon capture.
[3 marks]03.3Describe two ways captured CO2 can be stored and give one concern about long-term storage.
Show mark scheme
03.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Trees absorb CO2 by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood as they grow
  • 1Once mature there is no further net storage (photosynthesis balanced by respiration / decomposition), though the standing forest remains a reservoir
  • 1Harvesting the timber and keeping it as wood (eg house construction) then replanting produces additional storage as the new trees grow
03.2 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Pre-combustion: the fuel is modified before use so no CO2 is released when burnt - eg gasification of coal to hydrogen and CO2 (CO2 stored, hydrogen distributed); oxy-fuel combustion in pure oxygen giving only CO2 and water vapour which is condensed out
  • 1Advantage: capture at a large central facility; suits small dispersed sources (vehicles) that cannot capture CO2
  • 1Post-combustion: CO2 removed from exhaust gases by solvents, membrane filtration, adsorption / desorption, cryogenic separation, graphene
  • 1Disadvantage: only about 20% of flue gas is CO2 so separation is costly / energy-intensive; none fully developed
03.3 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Injected into depleted oil / gas fields or saline aquifers
  • 1Injected into oil reservoirs to provide pressure for secondary oil recovery
  • 1Reaction with fractured basalt to form solid carbonate minerals
  • 1Concern: leakage back to the atmosphere / monitoring over centuries; limited suitable geology; cost
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 151-152; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.
Question 410 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.3 · Genn pp. 152-154 · BGC-04

Figure 1 shows a simplified nitrogen cycle with some processes labelled 1 to 6.

Figure 1
NumberProcess description
1Nitrogen gas converted to ammonium ions by Rhizobium in root nodules
2Ammonium ions oxidised to nitrite then nitrate by Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter

[3 marks]04.1Name processes 1 to 6.
[3 marks]04.2Explain why draining a waterlogged field increases the nitrate available to crops.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why nitrogen is described as a resource that is 'not very abundant' for living organisms even though the atmosphere is 78% nitrogen.
[2 marks]04.4Explain why growing legumes such as clover in a crop rotation reduces the need for artificial fertiliser.
Show mark scheme
04.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 11 nitrogen fixation; 2 nitrification
  • 13 denitrification; 4 ammonification
  • 15 ionisation; 6 leaching (3 marks for all six, 2 for four or five, 1 for two or three)
04.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Drainage makes the soil aerobic
  • 1Aerobic nitrifying bacteria (and nitrogen fixers) increase so more ammonium is converted to nitrate
  • 1Anaerobic denitrifying bacteria decline so less nitrate is lost as nitrogen gas
04.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Nitrogen gas is very unreactive so most organisms cannot use it directly
  • 1Only nitrogen fixation (bacteria, lightning, Haber process) converts it to usable ammonium / nitrate; the cycle recycles a small usable amount
04.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Rhizobium bacteria in their root nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen into ammonium / proteins
  • 1When the plants die / are ploughed in, decomposition and nitrification release nitrates for the following crop

Question 610 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.4 · Genn pp. 156-157 · BGC-06
[3 marks]06.1Explain why phosphorus is more likely than nitrogen to be a limiting factor on biological productivity.
[2 marks]06.2Explain how mycorrhizal fungi increase phosphate uptake by plants.
[3 marks]06.3Describe how human activities have altered the phosphorus cycle.
[2 marks]06.4Explain two strategies for the sustainable management of phosphorus supplies.
Show mark scheme
06.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1There is no gaseous / atmospheric reservoir of phosphorus (no equivalent of nitrogen fixation)
  • 1Most phosphorus compounds have low solubility so little is available in soil water
  • 1Phosphorus lost to marine sediments only returns through very slow processes: mountain building (uplift) and weathering of rocks
06.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Fungal hyphae form a symbiotic relationship with plant roots and spread through a large volume of soil
  • 1Greatly increasing the surface area for absorbing (poorly mobile) phosphate ions, passed to the plant in exchange for carbohydrates
06.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Mining of calcium phosphate rock (and past mining of guano deposits, eg Peru, Nauru, now largely exhausted)
  • 1Treated to make more soluble ammonium phosphate fertilisers, mobilising phosphorus into the environment
  • 1Fertiliser runoff / eroded soil / sewage effluent carry phosphates into water bodies causing eutrophication (with nitrates); harvesting removes phosphorus from farmland
06.4 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Use biological wastes (manure, sewage sludge, compost) as fertiliser to return phosphates rather than mining rock
  • 1Breed crops that absorb phosphate more efficiently
  • 1Maintain conditions for mycorrhizal fungi (less tillage, fewer fungicides, organic matter)
  • 1Recover phosphate from sewage effluent by iron(III) sulfate precipitation; reduce soil erosion
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 156-157; AQA spec 3.2.4.4.

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Carbon: combustion of fossil fuels and biomass moving carbon from long-term stores to the atmosphere; deforestation reducing photosynthesis; ploughing / drainage increasing aerobic decomposition; new anaerobic environments (paddies, landfill, reservoirs, livestock) releasing methane; marine pollution reducing phytoplankton; consequences: rising CO2 and CH4, climate change, ocean acidification, reduced soil organic matter
