Question 110 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.1 · Genn pp. 338-339 · FISH-01

Figure 1 shows the seasonal changes in light intensity, nutrient concentration and phytoplankton biomass in the surface water of a temperate ocean in the northern hemisphere.

Figure 1
[4 marks]01.1Use Figure 1 to explain the spring peak and summer decline in phytoplankton biomass.
[2 marks]01.2Explain why tropical open oceans have low productivity all year despite high light and temperature.
[3 marks]01.3Explain why areas of upwelling and coastal waters are the most productive parts of the sea.
[1 mark]01.4Explain why phosphate is often the limiting nutrient in the sea.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • In winter nutrients are high (storms disturb sediments, deep mixing) but light is too low for photosynthesis
  • In spring light rises while nutrients are still high so growth is rapid - the spring bloom (peak about May)
  • The bloom uses up nutrients (falling to 10 by July); dead plankton sink out of the photic zone carrying nutrients to the seabed
  • So nutrients become limiting in summer despite high light; a smaller autumn bloom follows when storms return nutrients while light is still adequate
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Warm surface water is stable (no seasonal mixing / storms) so nutrients lost to deep water in sinking dead organisms are not replaced
  • Nutrient (especially phosphate) concentrations in the photic zone remain very low - the limiting factor
01.3 (3 marks)
  • Upwelling (offshore winds, seamounts, currents deflected upwards) brings cold nutrient-rich deep water into the photic zone
  • Coastal waters receive nutrients washed off the land in rivers and storms disturb shallow sediments
  • Nutrients plus light in the shallow photic zone allow high algal productivity supporting rich food webs and fisheries (eg Peru anchovy)
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Phosphates have low solubility and no atmospheric reservoir; they are only added by river runoff or upwelling and are lost to sediments
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 338-339; AQA spec 3.5.2.1.
Question 210 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 340-343 · FISH-02

Table 1 shows features of some fishing methods.

[6 marks]02.1Complete Table 1.
Table 1
MethodTarget speciesOne environmental disadvantage
Demersal trawling
Purse seining
Pelagic long-lining
[2 marks]02.2Explain why by-catch is a particular problem with shrimp trawling and drift netting.
[2 marks]02.3Explain what is meant by ghost fishing and describe two ways it can be reduced.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (6 marks)
  • Demersal trawl: seabed species - cod, haddock, plaice, shrimps, scampi; chains / weights damage the seabed (mix aerobic and anaerobic layers, destroy sea fans, corals, seagrass); high by-catch in mixed fisheries; high fuel use
  • Purse seine: shoaling pelagic species - tuna, sardines, herring, anchovy; can remove whole shoals; dolphins associated with tuna shoals may be caught
  • Pelagic long-line: tuna, squid; albatrosses (long-lived, low breeding rate) drown on baited hooks; sharks and turtles caught
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Shrimp trawls use very small mesh so crabs, molluscs, sea urchins, starfish, juvenile fish cannot escape - by-catch can be 99% of the catch
  • Drift nets are non-selective curtains near the surface catching whales, dolphins, turtles, sharks
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Lost or discarded nets and traps continue to catch and kill organisms; dead animals act as bait attracting more
  • Biodegradable ropes / panels so lost traps fall apart; radio transmitters on traps so they can be recovered; net recovery schemes
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 340-343; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.

[4 marks]03.1Use Table 1 to explain why orange roughy populations collapsed within ten years of large-scale fishing beginning in the late 1970s.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why herring populations recover from overfishing much faster than cod.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why the total mass of fish landed can stay the same while a population is being overfished.
[2 marks]03.4Describe how the Greenland shark and blue (common) skate illustrate the threat of by-catch to k-selected species.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • k-selected: very slow growth, does not breed until 30, few eggs, so the population cannot replace losses
  • Fish reach catchable size (20) before they breed (30) so fishing can remove the entire future breeding population
  • Deep-water aggregations easily located and caught by trawling
  • Recovery takes decades so fisheries have had to be closed
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Herring breed at 2 years - the same age they become catchable - so there is always a breeding population and many young
  • Cod are catchable at 2 but do not mature until 4, so heavy fishing removes most fish before they breed
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Larger numbers of smaller / younger fish are caught, or bigger boats, nets and sonar increase catch effort
  • Masks the decline of the breeding population until a sudden collapse
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Neither is a target species but both are caught in demersal trawls; skate now extinct in the Baltic and critically endangered elsewhere
  • Very late maturity (skate 11 years, Greenland shark 100-150 years, living over 400) and few young mean even low by-catch mortality exceeds replacement
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 341-342, 348-349; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 410 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 349-351 · FISH-04

The Russell formula describes changes in a fish stock: S2 = S1 + (A + G) - (C + M), where S1 and S2 are the stock biomass at the start and end of the year, A = recruitment of young fish, G = growth, C = catch and M = natural mortality. Table 1 gives estimates for a cod stock.

Table 1
TermBiomass / thousand tonnes
S1600
A90
G150
C220
M80
[2 marks]04.1Calculate S2 and state whether the stock is being fished sustainably.
[1 mark]04.2Calculate the maximum catch that would have kept the stock constant.
[3 marks]04.3Explain why the catch is the only term in the formula that is easy to measure, and why the others are difficult.
[4 marks]04.4Describe how each of the following measures obtained from commercial catches can indicate overfishing.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • 600 + (90 + 150) - (220 + 80) = 540
  • Stock fell by 60 so the catch exceeds the MSY / unsustainable
04.2 (1 marks)
  • A + G - M = 90 + 150 - 80 = 160
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Catch is landed and weighed at ports so it is recorded
  • Other terms need data on fish in the sea: populations are mobile (crossing national boundaries), very unevenly distributed
  • Representative sampling of a large enough proportion of the area is impractical / expensive; survival of eggs and larvae varies greatly between years
04.4 (4 marks)
  • CPUE: if more effort (boat-days, engine power, net size) is needed to land the same catch, the population is declining even though total catch is maintained; a better measure than catch size which technology can prop up
  • Falling mean size / age (from scale or otolith growth rings) shows older adults are being removed faster than replaced and the catch depends on small young fish
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 349-351; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 510 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-05
[8 marks]05.1Explain how each of the following reduces the environmental impact of fishing.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why catch quotas work well for pelagic species such as mackerel but poorly in mixed demersal fisheries.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (8 marks)
  • Normal diagonal mesh closes as the net fills / stretches so small fish cannot escape; square-mesh panels do not close so juveniles escape to grow and breed; TEDs let turtles out; larger mesh sizes generally
  • Minimum size lets fish reach breeding age before capture (eg UK lobster minimum carapace above breeding size, cod 35 cm); maximum size / V-notching egg-carrying females protects large, highly fecund breeding adults (Hebridean lobsters over 145 mm, Nassau grouper)
  • NTZs (Lundy, Lamlash Bay, Chagos, Great Barrier Reef) protect breeding populations and habitats; surplus young spill over and increase surrounding catches; damaged communities recover
  • Closed seasons, ideally in the breeding season, let fish grow and breed; fishermen may oppose because shoaling breeders are easy to catch
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Pelagic species form single-species shoals so fishing stops when the quota is reached
  • In mixed fisheries many species are caught together; once one species' quota is reached it must be discarded (dead) while fishing for others continues
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 344-348; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 610 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-06
[3 marks]06.1Describe three methods of reducing seabird by-catch in long-line fisheries.
[3 marks]06.2Explain how restricting fishing effort and restricting fishing methods reduce overfishing.
[2 marks]06.3Describe how captive rearing and release ('population seeding') can increase wild populations of lobsters or oysters.
[2 marks]06.4Explain why collecting data on planktonic fish eggs and larvae helps to prevent population collapse.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Sinkers / weighted lines so hooks sink quickly below the reach of albatrosses
  • Setting lines at night when birds are not feeding
  • Bird-scaring streamers / decoys
  • Curved hooks that catch tuna but not birds
06.2 (3 marks)
  • Effort: limits on boat size, engine power, days at sea, number of licences; decommissioning payments to reduce fleet size where overfishing is unavoidable
  • Methods: bans or length limits on drift nets; bans on demersal trawling over sensitive seabed (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank deep-water reefs)
  • Shorter trawl tows so turtles / by-catch survive (over 99% survive under 10 minutes)
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Eggs / larvae are reared in captivity through the planktonic stage when most die (predation, failure to find habitat)
  • Juveniles are released when survival chances are good, increasing recruitment to the adult population
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Egg / larval survival varies greatly between years so adult numbers do not predict recruitment
  • Several poor years would lower the MSY; egg surveys allow catches to be reduced in advance rather than after a sudden collapse
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 344-348; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 715 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 341-345 · FISH-07

Table 1 shows the catch made by a shrimp trawler during one fishing trip.

Table 1
Component of catchMass / tonnes
Shrimps (target species)0.6
Crabs, sea urchins, molluscs and starfish5.9
Immature fish of commercial species1.9
[2 marks]07.1Calculate the percentage of the total catch that was by-catch.
[1 mark]07.2Explain why shrimp trawling produces such a high proportion of by-catch.
[3 marks]07.3Explain why k-selected species such as the common skate and the Greenland shark are especially vulnerable to overfishing.
[9 marks]07.4Evaluate the methods that can be used to reduce the environmental impacts of fishing.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • By-catch = 5.9 + 1.9 = 7.8 t; total = 8.4 t
  • 7.8 / 8.4 x 100 = 92.9% (accept 93%)
07.2 (1 marks)
  • The nets have a very small mesh size so few non-target animals can escape
07.3 (3 marks)
  • They breed at an old age (skate about 11 years; Greenland shark 100-150 years) and produce few young
  • They may reach a catchable size before they are sexually mature, so fishing can remove the whole breeding population before it has bred
  • Populations recover slowly, unlike r-selected species (herring, mackerel) which breed young and produce many young; they are caught as by-catch in demersal trawls so fishing continues even though they have no commercial value
07.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Impacts: population decline of k-selected species, by-catch (immature target fish, over-quota fish, non-commercial species; drift nets - whales, dolphins, turtles; longlines - albatrosses; pair trawls - porpoises), ghost fishing, seabed damage by demersal trawls, coral and seagrass damage, dynamite fishing, food-web effects
  • Catch quotas: limit weight landed; work well for single-species pelagic shoals but in mixed fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead so the quota fails to protect them
  • Gear design: mesh size and square-mesh escape panels let small fish escape (diagonal mesh closes under drag); turtle exclusion devices; acoustic pingers for dolphins; curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting to protect albatrosses; biodegradable / radio-tracked traps to reduce ghost fishing
  • Restricted effort: limits on boat size, engine power and days at sea; decommissioning; restricted methods - drift-net bans, demersal trawling bans on sensitive seabeds (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank)
  • Evaluation: enforcement at sea is difficult; some methods reduce catch or raise costs; quotas need reliable stock data; effectiveness depends on species (single vs mixed shoals) and on international cooperation as fish migrate; as local stocks decline fishing moves to more distant waters
  • Justified conclusion weighing effectiveness of different methods
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 341-345; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
