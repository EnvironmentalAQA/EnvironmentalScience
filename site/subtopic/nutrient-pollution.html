Figure 1 shows the dissolved oxygen concentration in a shallow lowland lake over 48 hours in late summer. The lake receives runoff from arable farmland and effluent from a sewage works.

Figure 1
[2 marks]01.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]01.2Explain the causes of the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why fish kills in eutrophic lakes usually happen at night or at the end of summer.
[2 marks]01.4Give two other harmful effects of algal blooms.
Show mark scheme
01.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Dissolved oxygen fluctuates daily / 24-hour cycle: highest in the afternoon (about 13-13.5 mg dm-3 at 16 and 40 h) and lowest at night / early morning (2.5-3.5 mg dm-3 at 4 and 28 h)
  • 1The night-time minimum is lower on the second night (2.5 vs 3.5)
01.2 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Nitrates (from fertiliser runoff) and phosphates (from sewage effluent) cause eutrophication: rapid growth of algae / cyanobacteria (algal bloom)
  • 1During the day photosynthesis by the dense algae releases oxygen so concentrations rise (above the normal saturation level)
  • 1At night there is no photosynthesis but respiration by algae / animals / bacteria continues, using oxygen
  • 1Aerobic bacterial decomposition of dead algae / shaded macrophytes uses oxygen; warm summer water holds less oxygen and decay is rapid
01.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1At night no photosynthesis replaces the oxygen used in respiration / decomposition so concentrations fall below the minimum fish can tolerate
  • 1At the end of summer large amounts of dead vegetation decay rapidly in warm water causing severe deoxygenation
01.4 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Cyanobacteria release toxins harmful to livestock, pets and people
  • 1Floating algae shade submerged / rooted macrophytes which die, disrupting food webs
  • 1Algae are an unreliable food source (die off rapidly) so food webs are poor
  • 1Blocked filters / unpleasant taste and smell in water supplies

Question 210 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 267-268 · NUT-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain why nitrates in drinking water can cause blue baby syndrome (methaemoglobinaemia).
[2 marks]02.2Explain why the link between nitrates in drinking water and stomach cancer is uncertain.
[3 marks]02.3Describe three ways farmers in a Nitrate Vulnerable Zone can reduce nitrate leaching into aquifers.
[2 marks]02.4Explain how phosphates are removed from sewage effluent and from lakes polluted in the past.
Show mark scheme
02.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Bacteria in the gut convert nitrates to nitrites
  • 1Nitrites react with haemoglobin so it cannot carry oxygen
  • 1Infants are most at risk because they have more of the gut bacteria and their haemoglobin reacts faster; usually from shallow wells receiving arable drainage
02.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Nitrites may be converted in the gut to nitrosamines which are carcinogenic
  • 1But most research has been on rats / the evidence in humans is not conclusive; nitrites are also ingested in processed food so the source cannot be isolated
02.3 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Reduce use of nitrate fertilisers / match application to crop need
  • 1Avoid ploughing / applying fertiliser at times of heavy rain (autumn / winter)
  • 1Use low-solubility / slow-release fertilisers rather than soluble rapid-release ones
  • 1Store manure / slurry where it cannot leach or wash into rivers
  • 1Grow crops with lower nitrogen requirements (eg triticale instead of wheat)
  • 1Grow legumes with nitrogen-fixing bacteria instead of relying on artificial fertiliser
02.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Iron(III) sulfate is added to effluent: phosphates are precipitated as insoluble iron phosphate (a fine sediment that can be used as fertiliser)
  • 1Lakes: dredging phosphate-rich sediments / adding iron(III) sulfate to reduce phosphate solubility (eg the Norfolk Broads)

Show mark scheme
03.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Screens (metal grills) trap floating / suspended non-biodegradable items (paper, plastic) which are landfilled or incinerated
  • 1Grit traps: the channel widens so flow slows and stones / road grit sink out because there is not enough kinetic energy to carry them
03.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The effluent stands in large tanks so the faecal / organic solids sink and are removed as sludge
  • 1This removes over 95% of the organic matter from the fluid so the risk of deoxygenation of the river is greatly reduced
03.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Aerobic bacteria digest / break down the remaining dissolved and suspended organic matter
  • 1Air (or pure oxygen) is mixed in by paddle wheels or air stones so oxygen shortage does not limit the aerobic process
  • 1The suspended bacteria collected in the secondary sedimentation tank are the 'activated sludge'; returning them maintains a large bacterial population so digestion is rapid
03.4 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Effluent from secondary treatment may still contain dissolved inorganic nutrients (phosphates, nitrates) that cause eutrophication, and some bacteria
  • 1Needed where the discharge site is ecologically sensitive or used by many people (bathing water, drinking water abstraction)
  • 1Phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate; micro-strainers (very fine sieves) then UV light or chlorine to kill remaining bacteria

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Organic nutrients (sewage, manure, silage effluent, paper mill / tannery / food processing effluent) are food for aerobic microbes whose respiration deoxygenates the water, killing fish and invertebrates; bacterial growths cover the bed, blocking light
  • Pathogens in sewage (cholera, typhoid, dysentery) spread via contaminated water; decay of organic matter releases inorganic nutrients
  • Inorganic nutrients: nitrates (fertiliser / manure runoff, leaching) and phosphates (sewage effluent, detergents, eroded soil) cause cultural eutrophication: algal blooms, shading of macrophytes, cyanobacterial toxins, night-time and end-of-summer deoxygenation
  • Human health: nitrites and methaemoglobinaemia; possible carcinogen via nitrosamines
  • Control of organic wastes: sewage treatment (pre-treatment, primary sedimentation removing >95% organic matter, aeration / activated sludge or trickling filters, anaerobic digestion of sludge with biogas)
  • Control of nutrients: tertiary phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate, denitrification in filter beds, micro-strainers / UV; Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, fertiliser timing / amount, low-solubility fertilisers, legumes, manure management; dredging sediments
  • Evaluation: sewage treatment has virtually eliminated deoxygenation and waterborne disease in developed countries but storm overflows and combined sewers still discharge untreated sewage; tertiary treatment is expensive so only used at sensitive sites; diffuse agricultural nitrate is hard to control and aquifers respond slowly; phosphate legacy in sediments
  • Judgement on point-source (sewage) control being more successful than diffuse-source (agriculture) control
Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Organic wastes - sewage, manure, silage effluent, paper-mill and food-processing effluent - are food for aerobic bacteria, whose respiration strips dissolved oxygen from the water so fish and invertebrates suffocate and bacterial growths smother the bed; sewage also carries pathogens such as cholera and typhoid. Inorganic nutrients act differently: nitrate leached from fertiliser and manure and phosphate from sewage, detergents and eroded soil cause cultural eutrophication, in which algal blooms shade macrophytes, cyanobacteria release toxins and night-time respiration and decay deoxygenate the water. Nitrate in drinking water can cause methaemoglobinaemia and may form carcinogenic nitrosamines.

Point sources are controlled by sewage treatment: screening and grit removal, primary sedimentation removing most organic solids, aeration with activated sludge or trickling filters, and anaerobic digestion of sludge yielding biogas. Nutrients need tertiary treatment - phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate, denitrifying filter beds, micro-strainers and UV. Diffuse agricultural sources are managed by Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, limits on the timing and amount of fertiliser, low-solubility fertilisers, legumes, manure management and buffer strips.

Sewage treatment has virtually eliminated river deoxygenation and waterborne disease in developed countries, but storm overflows on combined sewers still discharge untreated sewage, and tertiary treatment is expensive so it is used only where eutrophication is a risk. Diffuse nitrate from farmland is much harder to control because it comes from thousands of sources and depends on farmers' behaviour and the weather. Control of point sources has therefore been far more successful than control of diffuse ones.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

Sewage in rivers uses up the oxygen because bacteria break it down, so fish die. Fertilisers wash into rivers and cause eutrophication, where algae grow and block the light and then the water runs out of oxygen. Sewage is treated in sewage works to clean it before it goes into rivers. Farmers should use less fertiliser. Sewage treatment works well but fertiliser is still a problem.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 has the outline of both mechanisms but Level 3 explains them precisely (aerobic respiration, shading, night-time respiration, pathogens, health effects) and names the pollutant sources.
  • Level 3 describes the stages of sewage treatment and the specific nutrient controls; Level 1 says 'sewage works' and 'less fertiliser'.
  • Level 3's evaluation gives reasons (storm overflows, cost of tertiary treatment, diffuse sources) for its point-vs-diffuse judgement; Level 1 asserts it.
