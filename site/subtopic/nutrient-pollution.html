Question 110 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 266-268 · NUT-01

Figure 1 shows the dissolved oxygen concentration in a shallow lowland lake over 48 hours in late summer. The lake receives runoff from arable farmland and effluent from a sewage works.

Figure 1
[2 marks]01.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]01.2Explain the causes of the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why fish kills in eutrophic lakes usually happen at night or at the end of summer.
[2 marks]01.4Give two other harmful effects of algal blooms.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Dissolved oxygen fluctuates daily / 24-hour cycle: highest in the afternoon (about 13-13.5 mg dm-3 at 16 and 40 h) and lowest at night / early morning (2.5-3.5 mg dm-3 at 4 and 28 h)
  • The night-time minimum is lower on the second night (2.5 vs 3.5)
01.2 (4 marks)
  • Nitrates (from fertiliser runoff) and phosphates (from sewage effluent) cause eutrophication: rapid growth of algae / cyanobacteria (algal bloom)
  • During the day photosynthesis by the dense algae releases oxygen so concentrations rise (above the normal saturation level)
  • At night there is no photosynthesis but respiration by algae / animals / bacteria continues, using oxygen
  • Aerobic bacterial decomposition of dead algae / shaded macrophytes uses oxygen; warm summer water holds less oxygen and decay is rapid
01.3 (2 marks)
  • At night no photosynthesis replaces the oxygen used in respiration / decomposition so concentrations fall below the minimum fish can tolerate
  • At the end of summer large amounts of dead vegetation decay rapidly in warm water causing severe deoxygenation
01.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Cyanobacteria release toxins harmful to livestock, pets and people
  • Floating algae shade submerged / rooted macrophytes which die, disrupting food webs
  • Algae are an unreliable food source (die off rapidly) so food webs are poor
  • Blocked filters / unpleasant taste and smell in water supplies
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 266-268; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 210 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 267-268 · NUT-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain why nitrates in drinking water can cause blue baby syndrome (methaemoglobinaemia).
[2 marks]02.2Explain why the link between nitrates in drinking water and stomach cancer is uncertain.
[3 marks]02.3Describe three ways farmers in a Nitrate Vulnerable Zone can reduce nitrate leaching into aquifers.
[2 marks]02.4Explain how phosphates are removed from sewage effluent and from lakes polluted in the past.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Bacteria in the gut convert nitrates to nitrites
  • Nitrites react with haemoglobin so it cannot carry oxygen
  • Infants are most at risk because they have more of the gut bacteria and their haemoglobin reacts faster; usually from shallow wells receiving arable drainage
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Nitrites may be converted in the gut to nitrosamines which are carcinogenic
  • But most research has been on rats / the evidence in humans is not conclusive; nitrites are also ingested in processed food so the source cannot be isolated
02.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Reduce use of nitrate fertilisers / match application to crop need
  • Avoid ploughing / applying fertiliser at times of heavy rain (autumn / winter)
  • Use low-solubility / slow-release fertilisers rather than soluble rapid-release ones
  • Store manure / slurry where it cannot leach or wash into rivers
  • Grow crops with lower nitrogen requirements (eg triticale instead of wheat)
  • Grow legumes with nitrogen-fixing bacteria instead of relying on artificial fertiliser
02.4 (2 marks)
  • Iron(III) sulfate is added to effluent: phosphates are precipitated as insoluble iron phosphate (a fine sediment that can be used as fertiliser)
  • Lakes: dredging phosphate-rich sediments / adding iron(III) sulfate to reduce phosphate solubility (eg the Norfolk Broads)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 267-268; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 310 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 269-272 · NUT-03

[2 marks]03.1Explain the purpose of screens and grit traps.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why primary sedimentation is described as the most important process.
[3 marks]03.3Describe what happens in the aeration tank and explain why the sludge from the secondary sedimentation tank is returned to it.
[3 marks]03.4Explain why tertiary treatment may be needed before effluent is discharged, and describe one tertiary process.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Screens (metal grills) trap floating / suspended non-biodegradable items (paper, plastic) which are landfilled or incinerated
  • Grit traps: the channel widens so flow slows and stones / road grit sink out because there is not enough kinetic energy to carry them
03.2 (2 marks)
  • The effluent stands in large tanks so the faecal / organic solids sink and are removed as sludge
  • This removes over 95% of the organic matter from the fluid so the risk of deoxygenation of the river is greatly reduced
03.3 (3 marks)
  • Aerobic bacteria digest / break down the remaining dissolved and suspended organic matter
  • Air (or pure oxygen) is mixed in by paddle wheels or air stones so oxygen shortage does not limit the aerobic process
  • The suspended bacteria collected in the secondary sedimentation tank are the 'activated sludge'; returning them maintains a large bacterial population so digestion is rapid
03.4 (3 marks)
  • Effluent from secondary treatment may still contain dissolved inorganic nutrients (phosphates, nitrates) that cause eutrophication, and some bacteria
  • Needed where the discharge site is ecologically sensitive or used by many people (bathing water, drinking water abstraction)
  • Phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate; micro-strainers (very fine sieves) then UV light or chlorine to kill remaining bacteria
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 269-272; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 410 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 270-273 · NUT-04
[4 marks]04.1Describe the process of anaerobic sludge digestion and give two benefits of the process.
[4 marks]04.2Explain two problems of using sewage sludge as an agricultural fertiliser and how each can be reduced.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why it is more efficient to have separate collection systems for foul sewage and for surface drainage water.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Sludge from primary sedimentation is digested by anaerobic microbes in a warm tank for about four weeks
  • Kills most pathogens
  • Reduces odour and volume of the sludge
  • Produces biogas (mainly methane with some CO2) used as fuel to heat the digester / generate electricity
  • Digested sludge has a better C:N ratio and can be used as agricultural fertiliser
04.2 (4 marks)
  • Heavy metals from urban wastes / road runoff may be present and absorbed by crops - reduced by adding lime to lower metal solubility
  • Remaining pathogens - destroyed by heat treatment of the sludge before use
  • Timing: spreading in the growing season covers crops; in winter vehicles compact wet soils - apply at suitable times / when soils are dry
  • Odour / public objection - digestion reduces odour
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Foul waste is a small volume needing full treatment so smaller, cheaper treatment works can be built; surface water needs little treatment
  • The treatment works is not affected by weather-related fluctuations in flow (storms) so storm overflows of untreated sewage are avoided
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 270-273; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.

[3 marks]05.1Explain how the acidic drainage water is produced in the spoil heap.
[3 marks]05.2Use Table 1 to explain why the number of invertebrate taxa falls so sharply 50 m downstream, even though dissolved oxygen is unchanged.
[2 marks]05.3Suggest why the pH and zinc concentration have partly recovered 2 km downstream.
[2 marks]05.4Describe how acid mine drainage can be controlled.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Spoil contains residual sulfide ores (eg iron pyrites, zinc sulfide)
  • These react with oxygen in the air spaces between the waste particles (oxidation)
  • Producing metal oxides and oxides of sulfur which dissolve in drainage water to form sulfuric acid
05.2 (3 marks)
  • The low pH (3.4) directly damages exposed cells / denatures proteins and dissolves calcium-based exoskeletons
  • Low pH increases the solubility of zinc (0.02 to 6.5 mg dm-3) which is toxic (inhibits enzymes)
  • Only a few tolerant taxa survive; the sensitive mayfly larvae (indicator of clean water) are eliminated; dissolved oxygen is not the limiting factor here
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Dilution by tributaries / more water
  • Neutralisation by alkaline rocks (limestone) in the stream bed; zinc precipitates / adsorbs onto sediments as pH rises
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Collect the drainage water and pass it through crushed limestone / add lime to neutralise the acid
  • Higher pH also reduces the solubility of toxic metals so they precipitate and can be removed

Question 610 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 273-275 · NUT-06
[3 marks]06.1Explain why organic pollution of rivers is usually monitored by measuring Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) rather than the concentrations of the pollutants themselves.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why a coliform count is used to assess the health risk from sewage contamination rather than testing for pathogens such as typhoid.
[3 marks]06.3Give three characteristics of species that make them useful in a biotic index.
[2 marks]06.4Give one advantage and one disadvantage of using a biotic index rather than chemical monitoring.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Many different organic substances may be present, each with a different potential to cause deoxygenation, so it is difficult to measure or forecast them individually
  • BOD measures the effect (amount of oxygen used by microbes decomposing the organic matter) under standardised conditions (eg 5 days, 20 °C, dark)
  • It gives a single measure directly related to the risk of deoxygenation
06.2 (2 marks)
  • E. coli is a common gut bacterium so its presence confirms faecal contamination and its number indicates how serious the contamination is
  • Pathogens are not always present so their absence would not prove the water is uncontaminated
06.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Different species have different sensitivities to pollution
  • Easy to identify
  • Easy to find / normally present / usually common
  • Widely distributed
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Advantage: assesses the effect of pollution over a period of time (recent pollution events are detected even if the pollutant has gone); no expensive equipment needed
  • Disadvantage: identification needs skill; does not identify which pollutant is present so further chemical tests are required; species may be absent for other reasons
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 273-275; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 710 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 266-272 · NUT-07
[9 marks]07.1Explain how organic wastes and inorganic nutrients cause water pollution and evaluate the effectiveness of the methods used to control them.
[1 mark]07.2Give one reason why algae cause eutrophication problems more quickly than rooted plants when nutrients are added to a lake.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Organic nutrients (sewage, manure, silage effluent, paper mill / tannery / food processing effluent) are food for aerobic microbes whose respiration deoxygenates the water, killing fish and invertebrates; bacterial growths cover the bed, blocking light
  • Pathogens in sewage (cholera, typhoid, dysentery) spread via contaminated water; decay of organic matter releases inorganic nutrients
  • Inorganic nutrients: nitrates (fertiliser / manure runoff, leaching) and phosphates (sewage effluent, detergents, eroded soil) cause cultural eutrophication: algal blooms, shading of macrophytes, cyanobacterial toxins, night-time and end-of-summer deoxygenation
  • Human health: nitrites and methaemoglobinaemia; possible carcinogen via nitrosamines
  • Control of organic wastes: sewage treatment (pre-treatment, primary sedimentation removing >95% organic matter, aeration / activated sludge or trickling filters, anaerobic digestion of sludge with biogas)
  • Control of nutrients: tertiary phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate, denitrification in filter beds, micro-strainers / UV; Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, fertiliser timing / amount, low-solubility fertilisers, legumes, manure management; dredging sediments
  • Evaluation: sewage treatment has virtually eliminated deoxygenation and waterborne disease in developed countries but storm overflows and combined sewers still discharge untreated sewage; tertiary treatment is expensive so only used at sensitive sites; diffuse agricultural nitrate is hard to control and aquifers respond slowly; phosphate legacy in sediments
  • Judgement on point-source (sewage) control being more successful than diffuse-source (agriculture) control
07.2 (1 marks)
  • Algae / cyanobacteria absorb nutrients directly from the water (not the sediments) so they use dissolved nutrients more easily and grow rapidly
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 266-272; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
