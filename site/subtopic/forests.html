Question 110 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.1 · Genn pp. 360-364 · FOR-01
[5 marks]01.1Describe the resources obtained from forests. Give one example for each category.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why forests are an important carbon reservoir and how they can be used to counteract climate change.
[2 marks]01.3Explain how forests regulate the hydrological cycle and local climate.
Show mark scheme
01.1 5 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Timber: building construction, furniture, tools, telegraph poles, concrete shuttering (oak, beech, mahogany, teak, conifer softwood)
  • 1Fuel: fuelwood and charcoal - still the main energy source for most people in LEDCs
  • 1Food: fruit, nuts, bushmeat, livestock fodder; crops of forest origin (coffee, cacao, bananas, Brazil nuts); pigs and chickens were forest animals
  • 1Fibres: paper (wood pulp cellulose), cotton, viscose / rayon
  • 1Medicines: quinine (cinchona), taxol (yew), aspirin (willow); most species not yet researched
01.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Wood is mainly cellulose which is hard to digest, so carbon stored in wood has a long residence time; total forest carbon (vegetation plus soil) is about double that in the atmosphere
  • 1Growing trees sequester CO2 by photosynthesis and release oxygen; forest soils hold more carbon than the trees (especially boreal)
  • 1Planting / protecting forests removes CO2 and reduces the enhanced greenhouse effect
01.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Highest transpiration of any ecosystem increases downwind rainfall; interception increases evaporation; deep organic soils retain water and delay runoff so river flow is more even and flooding reduced
  • 1Low albedo absorbs sunlight; heat stored and released at night reduces day-night temperature extremes; canopy microclimate: lower light, wind, higher humidity
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 360-364; AQA spec 3.5.3.1.
Question 210 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.2 · Genn pp. 365-367 · FOR-02

Table 1 compares an indigenous mixed broadleaf woodland with a Sitka spruce plantation of the same area in Scotland.

Table 1
FeatureMixed indigenous woodlandSitka spruce plantation
Tree species121
Age structureAll ages, dead wood presentAll planted in the same year
Planting densityNatural, variableClose, 2 m spacing
Timber yield / m3 ha-1 yr-1416
Bird species389
Ground flora species604
[6 marks]02.1Use Table 1 to explain why the plantation has a much higher timber yield but far lower biodiversity.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why commercial plantations are felled before the trees reach full size.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why there is little demand today for coppiced or pollarded wood.
Show mark scheme
02.1 6 marks
Up to 6 marks in total; award marks in proportion to the completeness of each point.
  • up to 2Non-indigenous species (Sitka spruce from North America) chosen for fast growth and pest resistance; indigenous wildlife's needs for food, pollination, seed dispersal and habitat are not met
  • up to 2Single species: easy planting, thinning and felling over large areas; but each tree species supports its own community so few species overall
  • up to 2Close planting makes trees compete for light so they grow tall and straight (good timber) but shades out ground flora and the species that depend on them
  • up to 2Simple age structure: uniform size makes management easy but gives little variety of light, temperature, wind; no old trees or dead wood for invertebrates and woodpeckers
  • up to 2Mixed woodland: varied niches, stratification, dead wood, indigenous trees each with their own dependent species
02.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Trees are felled when income will be greatest - approaching full size when the growth rate starts to decline
  • 1Continuing to grow them ties up land and capital for little extra timber; natural forests keep old and dead trees
02.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Industrial society replaced species-specific uses (baskets, fencing, charcoal, tool handles) with large-scale uses: chipboard, plywood, pulp
  • 1Wood fuel replaced by fossil fuels / electricity; so traditional management declined and woods became uniform
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 365-367; AQA spec 3.5.3.2.

[4 marks]03.2Explain the main causes of forest loss in South America and Africa.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why roads and railways cause more forest loss than the area they occupy.
[1 mark]03.4Explain why softwood from Scandinavia and Canada is generally produced more sustainably than tropical hardwood.
Show mark scheme
03.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1South America: high loss (5.1-5.5 Mha yr-1) falling by about half by 2020
  • 1Africa: loss steady at about 3.4 then rising to 3.9 by 2020 - now the highest
  • 1Europe: negative values - forest area increasing (afforestation exceeds loss), though the gain is shrinking
03.2 4 marks
Up to 4 marks in total; award marks in proportion to the completeness of each point.
  • up to 2South America: clearance for commercial cash crops (sugar cane, soya) and cattle ranching for export; HEP reservoirs; roads improving access; mining
  • up to 2Africa: growing rural populations clearing land for subsistence farming; fuelwood and charcoal collection above regrowth (charcoal wastes much of the wood's energy); fodder cutting in dry areas
  • up to 2Both: hardwood timber (teak, mahogany) from clear-felled mixed forest often not replanted; urban expansion
03.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1They create barriers fragmenting wildlife populations
  • 1Improved access makes it easier to remove timber and farm produce, so clearance spreads along them
03.4 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Conifers grow quickly so plantations are replanted after harvest and yield a saleable crop within decades; slow-growing hardwoods (teak, mahogany) take too long for commercial plantations so they are taken from natural forest that is clear-felled and not replanted

Question 410 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 368-369 · FOR-04
[4 marks]04.1Explain the effects of deforestation on soil.
[4 marks]04.2Explain how deforestation affects hydrology and climate.
[2 marks]04.3Give two ecological impacts of deforestation other than the direct loss of species.
Show mark scheme
04.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Less dead organic matter / leaf litter so less humus and slower soil formation; fewer nutrients returned
  • 1Loss of canopy and litter exposes soil to rain splash and wind
  • 1Decomposing roots no longer bind soil so erosion, gullying and landslides increase (especially on slopes)
  • 1Reduced organic matter and biota lower infiltration and water retention
04.2 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Reduced interception and transpiration so less water returned to the atmosphere and less rainfall downwind
  • 1Increased runoff: more flooding after rain, lower flows in dry periods; sedimentation of rivers
  • 1Increased albedo of cleared land (reflects more sunlight)
  • 1Reduced carbon sequestration and loss of the carbon reservoir (CO2 released by burning / decay) contributing to climate change
04.3 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Fragmentation of remaining forest into isolated patches with non-viable populations / edge effects
  • 1Loss of inter-species relationships (pollinators, seed dispersers) causing further declines
  • 1Loss of genetic resources / potential medicines before they are discovered
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 368-369; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.
Question 510 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 369, 386-387 · FOR-05

The Rio Bravo Conservation and Management Area in Belize exploits mahogany under a sustainable management plan while conserving a rainforest of over 200 tree, 400 bird and 70 mammal species.

[5 marks]05.1Describe five features of the timber harvesting at Rio Bravo that make it sustainable.
[3 marks]05.2Explain why involving local communities is essential to the success of the reserve.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why selective logging is better for wildlife than clear-felling, and give one disadvantage.
Show mark scheme
05.1 5 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Harvest rate below the maximum sustainable yield; only economically valuable species (mahogany) selectively logged, not clear-felled
  • 1Trees felled only where other mahoganies stand upwind so seeds recolonise the clearing; large seed trees and small trees left to grow
  • 1Buffer zones with no harvesting or machinery near rivers / wetlands
  • 1Restricted machinery and planned track routes re-used to avoid damaging roots of retained / rare / fruit trees
  • 1Each felled tree tagged and tracked to the retailer for FSC accreditation; rangers control illegal logging; tree nurseries replant over-exploited and ecologically important species
05.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The reserve removed traditional uses (fuelwood, timber, farming clearings, hunting, medicines) so people would otherwise resent / ignore it
  • 1Employment as rangers, forestry and tourism workers; ecotourists use local restaurants, produce and souvenirs; offcuts given to local carvers
  • 1Benefits give the community an incentive to protect the forest; carbon sequestration payments from companies fund management
05.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Removing individual trees leaves small clearings so remaining populations survive nearby and the forest structure / microclimate is kept; timber resource used over a longer period
  • 1Disadvantage: more labour-intensive and expensive; more tracks needed per tree
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 369, 386-387; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.

  • C  Clear-felling on a 40-year rotation
  • D  Removal of dead wood

06.1 1 mark
Correct answer only.
  • 1  B
06.2 4 marks
Any 4 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 4.

  • 1Mixed species plantations - more food choices, relationships and niches
  • 1Indigenous species - support indigenous insects, birds
