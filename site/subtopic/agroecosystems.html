Question 110 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 306-309 · AGR-01

Table 1 shows climate data for two sites where a farmer is considering growing maize, a frost-sensitive crop that needs a thermal growing season of at least 120 days.

Table 1
FeatureSite A: valley floorSite B: south-facing slope
Frost-free period / days110150
Days with mean temperature above 5 °C160175
Mean summer rainfall / mm180150
Gradient / %18
[3 marks]01.1Use Table 1 to explain which site is more suitable for maize.
[3 marks]01.2Describe three ways in which temperature can be controlled to increase crop productivity.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why keeping livestock warm increases the amount of food converted to growth.
[2 marks]01.4Explain how day length affects livestock production and how it can be manipulated.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • B: frost-free period (150 days) exceeds the 120 needed whereas A (110) is too short - late frosts would kill seedlings
  • B is warmer / longer thermal growing season because south-facing slopes receive more insolation; valley floors are frost pockets where cold dense air collects
  • But B has lower rainfall and a steeper slope (erosion / machinery difficulties) so irrigation / contour cultivation may be needed
01.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Greenhouses / polytunnels heated by solar gain or burning gas / paraffin (ventilated to prevent overheating)
  • Woven plastic cloches / fleece over rows to retain warm air near the ground
  • Burning oil / gas or using large fans in orchards to prevent frost damage to blossom
  • Site selection (aspect, avoiding frost pockets); heated buildings and shelter for livestock
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Mammals and birds use food energy to maintain a constant body temperature (thermoregulation)
  • Reducing heat loss (shelter, heated buildings) means less food energy is used for heat and more is available for growth
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Long days increase milk production and egg laying; poultry grow best with short days; sheep mate as days shorten in autumn
  • Artificial lighting extends the growing season for greenhouse crops or simulates autumn in spring to give sheep a second mating / lambing season
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 306-309; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.
Question 210 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 308-310, 313 · AGR-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain why a moderate water shortage reduces crop productivity even though the plants survive.
[4 marks]02.2Explain the problems caused by waterlogged soils and describe two ways farmers reduce soil water levels.
[2 marks]02.3Explain how irrigating with groundwater can cause soil salinisation and how this harms crops.
[1 mark]02.4State how a farmer could raise the pH of an acidic soil and explain why a low pH reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Plants close their stomata to reduce transpiration losses
  • Closed stomata stop the absorption of carbon dioxide so photosynthesis and growth stop
  • Less water for nutrient absorption / transport and cell turgidity; severe shortage dehydrates cells and inhibits biochemical reactions
02.2 (4 marks)
  • Anaerobic soil favours denitrifying bacteria and inhibits nitrifying bacteria so nitrate is lost faster than it forms; higher risk of fungal disease; root respiration inhibited
  • Drainage ditches or pipes
  • Deep ploughing; avoiding compaction by machinery / livestock; adding organic matter to encourage worms
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Groundwater often contains dissolved salts; when the water evaporates from the soil the salts are left behind and accumulate
  • High soil salinity causes osmotic dehydration of roots / crop death; washing out with extra water uses more water and salinises rivers
02.4 (1 marks)
  • Add crushed lime (calcium carbonate / hydroxide); low pH increases leaching, inhibits nutrient uptake and mobilises toxic aluminium and lead
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 308-310, 313; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.
Question 310 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 310-312 · AGR-03

[6 marks]03.1Complete Table 1 by giving two advantages and two disadvantages of each type of fertiliser.
Table 1

Organic (manure, sewage sludge, compost)

[4 marks]03.2Explain how hydroponic cultivation maximises productivity and give two disadvantages.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (6 marks)
  • Organic advantages: often waste products, locally available and cheap; add humus improving structure and water retention; increase soil biota; slow release reduces leaching
  • Organic disadvantages: nutrient composition cannot be controlled; released slowly so needs long-term planning; bulky with high water content so expensive to transport; cannot be applied to a growing crop; odour / pathogens
  • Inorganic advantages: composition controlled to match crop needs; nutrients released rapidly; concentrated and easy to apply
  • Inorganic disadvantages: high energy (fossil fuel) manufacture (Haber process); no organic matter so humus and biota decline; some toxic to worms; high solubility so leached causing eutrophication; raw materials (phosphate rock) finite
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Crops grown in a circulating nutrient solution (inert medium) in greenhouses; nutrient concentrations monitored and adjusted so nutrients are never limiting; abiotic factors controlled
  • Roots are small (all in contact with nutrients) so more growth goes into the harvested crop; no soil-borne pathogens or weeds; crop stays fresh with roots
  • Disadvantages: high inputs of nutrients and energy (heating, lighting, pumping); high level of technical knowledge; only economic for high-value crops
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 310-312; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.
Question 410 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 314-318 · AGR-04

Table 1 shows the yield of a vegetable crop under different pest control methods over three years on one farm.

Table 1
MethodMean yield / t ha-1Pesticide applications per yearNon-target insect species recorded in field margins
Chemical insecticide only18.568
Crop rotation + beetle banks + hedgerows16.2031
Integrated control18.11-226
[4 marks]04.1Explain how crop rotation and beetle banks reduce pest damage.
[3 marks]04.2Use Table 1 to evaluate integrated control compared with the other two methods.
[3 marks]04.3Describe how the sterile male technique controls insect pests and explain why it is species-specific.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Rotation: each crop has its own pests; growing a different crop each year (4-5 year cycle) means pests left at the end of a season die before that crop returns, so populations cannot build up early
  • Beetle banks / hedgerows provide habitat and overwintering sites for natural predators (ground beetles, ladybirds) that eat aphids
  • Rotation also evens out nutrient demands and includes legumes
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Integrated control gives almost the same yield as chemical control (18.1 vs 18.5) with far fewer applications (1-2 vs 6)
  • It retains most of the biodiversity of cultural control (26 vs 31 species) whereas chemical control alone leaves only 8
  • Cultural control alone loses about 12% yield; integrated control combines cultural methods, resistant varieties and carefully timed specific non-persistent pesticides only when essential - best balance of yield, cost and impact
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Large numbers of males are sterilised with gamma radiation and released
  • Females of many insects mate only once and store sperm, so mating with a sterile male produces no offspring; enough sterile males reduce fertile matings below replacement level (screw worm fly, Mediterranean fruit fly)
  • Only the target species' females mate with them so non-target species are unaffected; fails if sterilised males behave abnormally or re-colonisation occurs
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 314-318; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.
Question 510 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 316-320 · AGR-05
[4 marks]05.1Describe two case studies of biological control, one successful and one that caused problems.
[2 marks]05.2Explain how pheromone traps can be used in pest control.
[3 marks]05.3Explain why the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in livestock is a concern for human health.
[1 mark]05.4Give one way farmers can support crop pollinators.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • Success: Cactoblastis moth introduced from South America controlled prickly pear cactus in Australia; Encarsia parasitic wasps control greenhouse whitefly
  • Problems: cane toads introduced to Australia to control sugar cane beetles did not control them but ate many other species and are toxic to predators; rosy wolfsnail introduced to Polynesia to control African land snails ate endemic Partulid snails (48 species extinct); harlequin ladybirds introduced to Europe eat native ladybirds
  • Lesson: control agents should be specialist feeders on the pest
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Release an artificial sex scent attracting males (or females) of the pest species
  • Used to monitor when the pest arrives so pesticide is timed correctly, or (in enclosed greenhouses) to trap enough of one sex to prevent breeding
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Regular low doses kill only the most sensitive bacteria so resistant strains survive and multiply
  • Agricultural use exceeds medical use, so resistance spreads
  • Resistant zoonotic bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter) can transfer to humans and are harder to treat; hence reduced use of antibiotics is a sustainability goal
05.4 (1 marks)
  • Grow nectar-rich flowering plants alongside crops; restrict pesticides harmful to bees / spray when not in flower; introduce bee hives
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 316-320; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.
