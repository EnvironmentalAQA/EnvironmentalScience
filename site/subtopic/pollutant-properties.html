Question 210 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 239-240 · PPRO-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain the difference between a mutagen, a carcinogen and a teratogen.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why a birth abnormality caused by a teratogen cannot be inherited by future generations, whereas one caused by a mutagen may be.
[2 marks]02.3Distinguish between somatic and gonadic effects of a mutagen.
[3 marks]02.4Explain what is meant by synergism and give an example.
Show mark scheme
02.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Mutagen: an agent that changes the chemical structure / base sequence of DNA (causes mutations), eg ionising radiation, UV, PCBs, cadmium, asbestos
  • 1Carcinogen: a mutagen that causes cancer / uncontrolled cell division producing a tumour
  • 1Teratogen: an agent that causes birth abnormalities by preventing normal gene expression (inhibiting proteins / enzymes) without changing DNA structure, eg mercury, 2,4,5-T
02.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Teratogens do not change the DNA structure, so the abnormality is not in the genes and cannot be passed on
  • 1Mutagens change DNA; if the mutation is in a gamete / egg / sperm (gonadic effect) it can be inherited
02.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Somatic: mutation in a general body cell, affecting the individual only (eg cancer / tumour)
  • 1Gonadic: mutation in cells of the ovaries or testes / gametes / embryo, which may cause abnormalities in offspring
02.4 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Two or more pollutants whose effects interact to produce a different (usually more serious) effect
  • 1The pollutants themselves do not react to form a new pollutant (that would be a secondary pollutant)
  • 1Example: ozone damages leaf cuticles so sulfur dioxide can cause more damage to the exposed cells; smoke and sulfur dioxide in smogs; neonicotinoids with fungicides

Question 710 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 236-240 · PPRO-07
[9 marks]07.1Discuss how an understanding of the properties of pollutants can be used to predict their behaviour in the environment and to plan strategies to control them.
[1 mark]07.2State what is meant by bioaccumulation.

LevelMarksWhat the answer must show

 0No relevant content.
Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • State of matter: solids deposit near the source, gases disperse widely in the atmosphere; density (lead dust, hydrogen cyanide at Bhopal) controls how far particles/gases travel
  • Persistence / half-life: persistent pollutants (organochlorines, CFCs, heavy metals, radioactive wastes with long half-lives) travel further, accumulate and need containment rather than dilution; non-persistent pollutants (sewage, organophosphates) can be allowed to degrade
  • Toxicity, specificity, mode of action (enzyme inhibition, neurotoxins) determine acceptable concentrations and which species are at risk; LD50 / MDAF / MDNF used to set doses
  • Solubility in water (nitrates disperse and leach - control by nitrate vulnerable zones) vs liposolubility (DDT, mercury bioaccumulate and biomagnify - control by bans / restrictions)
  • Reactivity: reactive pollutants may degrade quickly or form secondary pollutants (ozone, acid rain, PANs) - controlling primary pollutants (catalytic converters, FGD) prevents secondary pollutants
  • Adsorption onto clay / sediments immobilises metals and caesium; can be used deliberately (activated carbon filters, polymer adsorption)
  • Mutagenic / carcinogenic / teratogenic action means no safe dose; exposure kept As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA)
  • Synergism (ozone + SO2; smoke + SO2) means pollutants must be considered together
  • Environmental features (temperature inversions, currents, pH, oxygen) interact with properties: Critical Pathway Analysis uses properties + environment to predict where pollutants go and to focus monitoring
  • Evaluation: properties of new chemicals can be predicted from similar ones (precautionary principle); understanding allows prevention rather than clean-up
Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

A pollutant's properties largely determine where it goes and how much harm it does, so they can be used both to predict its behaviour and to choose a control. State of matter and density govern dispersal: solids deposit near the source, gases spread widely, and dense gases such as hydrogen cyanide at Bhopal hug the ground. Persistence decides whether dilution is enough: non-persistent wastes like sewage can be discharged where they break down, but organochlorines, CFCs, heavy metals and long-lived radioisotopes travel far and accumulate, so they need containment, bans or restrictions instead. Toxicity, specificity and mode of action, quantified by LD50 and used to set acceptable doses, identify which species are at risk. Solubility explains why nitrate leaches and is controlled by nitrate vulnerable zones, while liposolubility explains why DDT and methyl mercury bioaccumulate and biomagnify along food chains. Reactive pollutants may degrade quickly or form secondary pollutants - ozone, acid rain, PANs - so controlling the primary emissions with catalytic converters or flue-gas desulfurisation prevents the secondary ones. Adsorption on clay immobilises metals and caesium and is exploited deliberately in activated-carbon and polymer filters. Mutagens and carcinogens have no safe dose, so exposure is kept As Low As Reasonably Achievable, and synergism between ozone and sulfur dioxide, or smoke and sulfur dioxide, means pollutants must be assessed together.

Properties interact with the environment - inversions, currents, pH, oxygen - which is why Critical Pathway Analysis combines both to predict where pollutants concentrate and to focus monitoring. The greatest value of this understanding is preventive: the properties of a new chemical can be predicted from similar ones under the precautionary principle, allowing control before release rather than clean-up afterwards.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (2 marks)

Pollutants have properties like toxicity, persistence and solubility. Toxic pollutants are dangerous. Persistent pollutants like DDT last a long time in the environment and build up in food chains. Soluble pollutants dissolve in water and get into rivers. Knowing these properties helps scientists to predict what pollutants will do and to control them, for example by banning DDT.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 defines three properties in isolation; Level 3 pairs every property with a prediction and a control strategy, which is what the question asks.
  • Level 3 uses a wide range of accurate examples (Bhopal, nitrate zones, methyl mercury, PANs, caesium adsorption); Level 1 uses DDT twice.
  • The final paragraph links properties to environmental conditions (CPA) and to the precautionary principle - synoptic evaluation that distinguishes the top band.
