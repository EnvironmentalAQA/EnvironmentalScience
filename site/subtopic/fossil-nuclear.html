Question 110 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 187-188 · FN-01

Figure 1 shows the percentage of the oil in a reservoir that can be recovered using different recovery techniques.

Figure 1
[2 marks]01.1Describe how primary oil recovery works and explain why it recovers only about 20% of the oil.
[2 marks]01.2Describe secondary recovery and explain how it can be linked to carbon capture and storage.
[3 marks]01.3Describe three tertiary (enhanced) recovery techniques.
[3 marks]01.4Explain three advantages of directional drilling.
Show mark scheme
01.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Natural pressure of gas above the oil (or dissolved in it) or water beneath forces oil up the production well; pump-jacks ('nodding donkeys') increase flow
  • 1Pressure falls as oil is removed so the remaining oil no longer flows to the well
01.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Water or natural gas is pumped down an injection well to maintain the pressure and flow of oil (recovery to about 40%)
  • 1Captured CO2 can be pumped down instead, increasing oil recovery while storing the CO2 underground
01.3 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Steam injection (from combustion or solar parabolic concentrators) to heat the oil and reduce its viscosity
  • 1Controlled underground combustion to heat the oil
  • 1Detergents / solvents to reduce surface tension so oil flows more easily
  • 1Bacteria that partially digest heavy oil into lighter oils and produce CO2 to maintain pressure
01.4 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Many wells can be drilled from a single platform, reducing land take / number of rigs
  • 1Deposits under locations where rigs cannot be placed (urban areas, sensitive habitats) can be reached
  • 1Drilling can follow softer strata (quicker) and target multiple small reservoirs up to 10 km away, increasing total recovery

Question 210 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 184, 189-190 · FN-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain why oil and gas trapped in shale cannot be extracted by conventional drilling and describe how hydraulic fracturing (fracking) allows extraction.
[5 marks]02.2Describe four environmental concerns about fracking and suggest how they may be reduced.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why tar sands and oil shales are not included in most estimates of oil reserves despite containing more oil than conventional crude oil.
Show mark scheme
02.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Shale has very low permeability so the 'tight' oil / gas cannot flow through the rock to a well
  • 1Fluid is pumped down at high pressure to open fissures in the shale along which oil / gas can flow to the recovery well
  • 1Sand grains (to hold fissures open) and solvents are pumped in to increase the recovery rate
02.2 5 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Natural gas may enter aquifer water
  • 1Injected chemicals may reach aquifers or the surface; toxic metals in the rock may be mobilised
  • 1Large volumes of water needed
  • 1Earth tremors as fracking releases natural tensions in the crust (though not tremors that could not occur naturally)
  • 1Reduced by: collection, treatment and re-use of waste water; well casing standards; restricting sites away from aquifers / sensitive areas; monitoring
02.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Extraction is expensive and energy-intensive (quarrying and hot water treatment, steam / solvent / combustion in-situ; heating shale to release oil) so it is not economically viable at current prices
  • 1Reserves only include what is economically exploitable now; high environmental impacts (land take, water, CO2) also limit exploitation
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 184, 189-190; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 310 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 187, 190 · FN-03
[4 marks]03.1Describe coal gasification and coal liquefaction and explain how each increases the usefulness of coal.
[2 marks]03.2Describe what methane hydrate is and where it is found.
[4 marks]03.3Describe two proposed methods of extracting methane from hydrate deposits and explain two environmental risks.
Show mark scheme
03.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Gasification: coal (including deposits too deep to mine) is burnt underground under controlled conditions to produce fuel gases (hydrogen, carbon monoxide, methane)
  • 1Allows deep coal to be exploited and gas can be piped / used in gas turbines or for hydrogen with CCS
  • 1Liquefaction: coal is converted to liquid hydrocarbons directly with solvents or via gasification and chemical conversion
  • 1Provides liquid fuels for vehicles / aircraft that solid coal cannot supply
03.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A solid, ice-like crystalline structure in which methane is trapped within water ice
  • 1Found at low temperatures (polar regions / permafrost) or high pressure in ocean sediments around continental margins
03.3 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Water heating: hot water pumped into sediments melts the hydrate releasing methane
  • 1Depressurisation: drilling reduces pressure so methane dissociates from the crystals
  • 1CO2 injection: CO2 bonds more strongly to the ice and displaces methane (could double as CCS)
  • 1Risks: methane not collected escapes to the atmosphere - powerful greenhouse gas / positive feedback; seabed ecology poorly understood; sediment instability / landslides
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 187, 190; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.

04.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Nuclei of large atoms (uranium-235, plutonium-239) split when hit by a neutron
  • 1A small amount of mass is converted to a large amount of energy (E = mc2) and more neutrons are released, causing a chain reaction (controlled by control rods / moderator)

  • 1Very high construction cost but low fuel cost means it is most economic running continuously at full output

  • 1Still need condenser cooling water (coast / large lake) and a stable site

1 mark.

Question 510 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 192-194 · FN-05
[3 marks]05.1Describe three factors that have restricted the growth of nuclear power.
[3 marks]05.2Explain how a plutonium 'breeder' reactor can produce more fissile fuel than it uses.
[4 marks]05.3Give two advantages and two disadvantages of thorium reactors compared with uranium reactors.
Show mark scheme
05.1 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Public opposition following accidents (Three Mile Island 1979, Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011)
  • 1Very complex, expensive technology needing an advanced industrial infrastructure
  • 1Concerns about links between civil nuclear materials and weapons / terrorism
  • 1Uncertainty over permanent disposal of radioactive waste
  • 1Uncertain decommissioning costs
05.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Only 0.7% of natural uranium is fissile U-235; the remaining 99.3% is non-fissile U-238 (a 'fertile' fuel)
  • 1Neutron bombardment in the reactor converts U-238 to fissile plutonium-239 (via beta decays)
  • 1So much more energy is harnessed from the mined uranium; fast reactors need no moderator but are complex and costly
05.3 4 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Advantages: thorium is three times more abundant; much less radioactive waste with shorter half-lives; harder to make weapons material; no fuel enrichment needed
  • 2Disadvantages: thorium-232 is not fissile so must be bred into uranium-233 (slow breeding rate); U-233 is an alpha emitter so very hazardous to handle; less developed technology with high development costs

Question 610 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 195-196 · FN-06
[2 marks]06.1Explain the difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.
[2 marks]06.2State the sources of the two fuels used in fusion research.
[3 marks]06.3Explain why very high temperatures, a vacuum and a magnetic field are needed in a toroidal (torus) fusion reactor.
[3 marks]06.4Give two potential advantages of fusion over fission and one reason why it is not yet a commercial energy source.
Show mark scheme
06.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Fission: splitting the nuclei of large atoms (U-235, Pu-239) when hit by neutrons
  • 1Fusion: joining the nuclei of small atoms (deuterium and tritium - isotopes of hydrogen) to form helium, releasing a neutron and energy - the energy source of stars
06.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Deuterium (hydrogen-2) extracted from (sea)water
  • 1Tritium (hydrogen-3) produced by neutron bombardment of lithium (a lithium blanket around the reactor)
06.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Very high temperature turns hydrogen into a plasma (electrons stripped away) and gives nuclei enough kinetic energy to overcome the repulsion between positive nuclei and collide
  • 1Vacuum so the plasma is not cooled by air
  • 1Magnetic field holds the plasma centrally so it does not touch the container walls and cool
06.4 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Fuel (deuterium / lithium) is abundant / effectively unlimited
  • 1Much less radioactive waste (no long-lived fission products); no chain reaction / meltdown risk; no weapons material
  • 1Not yet achieved sustained net energy output: reactors (JET) use more energy than they release; ITER aims for 500 MW out from 50 MW in; laser fusion also experimental; commercial viability decades away

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Fossil fuel technologies: secondary / tertiary recovery raising recovery from 20% to 60%; directional drilling and subsea wells; ROVs; fracking for tight oil and gas; tar sands / oil shales; coal gasification / liquefaction; enhanced gas recovery; methane hydrates; CCS (pre / post combustion, storage)

  • Limits: cost overruns, decommissioning, waste, public opposition, proliferation, embodied energy of low-grade uranium; fusion not commercial

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Technology has repeatedly extended fossil-fuel supplies. Secondary and tertiary recovery raise the proportion of oil recovered from about 20% to 60%; directional drilling, subsea wells and ROVs open deep-water fields; fracking releases tight oil and gas; tar sands and oil shales, coal gasification and liquefaction convert lower-grade resources; and carbon capture and storage could reduce the CO2 impact. Nuclear fission can be stretched even further: uranium can be adsorbed from seawater with polymers or recovered from phosphate mining and coal ash, molten-salt reactors run more safely, and plutonium breeder and thorium reactors multiply the energy obtainable from each tonne of fuel, with fusion a long-term prospect.

None of this makes the resources renewable. Discoveries of conventional oil have declined since the 1980s, unconventional sources cost more energy and money per unit and damage aquifers, use huge volumes of water, cause tremors and take land, and CCS is unproven at scale. Nuclear projects overrun their budgets, decommissioning and long-lived waste remain unsolved, public opposition and proliferation constrain expansion, low-grade uranium has a high embodied energy, and fusion is not commercial.

Sustainability means maintaining supply without unacceptable environmental, economic or social impact. New technology can extend supplies for decades and cut some impacts, which matters for closing the energy gap while renewables and conservation scale up, but it cannot make non-renewable resources sustainable indefinitely. Their proper role is as a transition and base-load source alongside renewables, not a permanent solution.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

New technologies mean fossil fuels can last longer. Fracking gets gas out of shale rock and directional drilling reaches oil under the sea. Carbon capture and storage can stop the carbon dioxide going into the atmosphere. For nuclear power, breeder reactors and thorium reactors can use fuel more efficiently and fusion might work in the future. However fossil fuels are still non-renewable so they will run out eventually, and fracking can pollute groundwater. Nuclear waste is still a problem. So new technologies help but they cannot make fossil fuels and nuclear power fully sustainable.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 reaches a sensible conclusion but supports it thinly; Level 3 gives specific evidence (20% to 60% recovery, declining discoveries, embodied energy of low-grade uranium).
  • Level 3 sets out a criterion for sustainability and judges against it - the hallmark of 'evaluate'.
  • Level 3 places the technologies in context (transition and base load alongside renewables) rather than treating them in isolation.
