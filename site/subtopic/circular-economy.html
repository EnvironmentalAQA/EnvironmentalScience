Question 110 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.1 · Genn pp. 374-375 · SUS-05
[6 marks]01.1Describe the main principles of the circular economy.
[2 marks]01.2Explain what is meant by 'optimum production rather than maximum production', using a comparison between a natural ecosystem and an agroecosystem.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why international cooperation (eg through the EU or UN) is needed to implement circular economy initiatives.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (6 marks)
  • Cycling of materials (like biogeochemical cycles) - recycling, re-use
  • Energy from renewable sources
  • Human activities should support ecosystems / conserve biodiversity
  • Separation of technical and biological materials; connected systems where the waste of one process feeds another (industrial symbiosis)
  • Diverse systems are more resistant to change
  • Design of products for extended use and end-of-life re-use; optimum rather than maximum production; technologies that improve efficiency and enable dismantling
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Natural systems over-produce so the surplus supports the processes the system relies on: plant products feed pollinators, seed dispersers, decomposers, mycorrhizae
  • Agroecosystems aim for maximum harvested yield, removing the surplus that would maintain soil biota, pollinators and pest predators, so they need artificial inputs
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Costs of environmental measures could put companies in one country at a disadvantage against competitors in countries without them
  • A common approach gives a level playing field, coordinated monitoring and support for less successful countries
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 374-375; AQA spec 3.6.4.1.
Question 210 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.1 · Genn pp. 375-379 · SUS-06
[9 marks]02.1Evaluate the extent to which the principles of the circular economy can be applied to three of the following: water supplies, mineral supplies, waste management, pollution control, energy supplies, food production.
[1 mark]02.2State what is meant by industrial symbiosis.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Water: catchment management, conservation, metering, greywater, re-use of treated effluent, aquifer recharge, sustainable urban drainage - largely cyclical already; limits in arid regions
  • Minerals: exploiting low-grade ores with low-temperature processes (bioleaching), extending product lifespans, repair / re-use / repurpose / recycle, design for disassembly, reduced packaging; limits: dispersal, alloys, energy of collection
  • Waste management: repair, re-use, refurbish, recycle, composting and anaerobic digestion returning nutrients, minimal landfill, energy from residual waste; EU targets on packaging, batteries, WEEE
  • Pollution control: shift from post-production treatment to non-release - fuel desulfurisation not FGD, fuel cells / electric vehicles not combustion, industrial symbiosis (Kalundborg: waste heat, gypsum, fly ash, yeast used by neighbours); limits for legacy pollution
  • Energy: renewables, storage, energy density technologies, low-temperature manufacturing, CHP; limits of intermittency, transport fuels
  • Food: natural processes for nutrients (legumes, organic matter), pest control (IPM), soil biota, agri-environment schemes, IMTA aquaculture; tension with maximum yield for a growing population
  • Land uses supporting ecosystems: urban green infrastructure, gardens, permeable surfaces, habitat in redeveloped docks / lagoons
  • Judgement: high applicability in some sectors (water, waste), partial in others (minerals, energy for transport); needs policy support
02.2 (1 marks)
  • Locating industries so that the wastes of one (heat, gases, by-products) become the raw materials of another, mimicking natural cycles (eg Kalundborg eco-industrial park)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 375-379; AQA spec 3.6.4.1.
Question 310 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.2 · Genn pp. 379-380 · SUS-07

Table 1 shows the ecological footprint and biocapacity per person for four countries and the world in 2016.

Table 1
Ecological footprint / gha per personBiocapacity / gha per personHuman Development Index
USA8.13.60.92
UK4.41.40.92
China3.61.00.75
Bangladesh0.80.40.61
World2.81.70.72
[2 marks]03.1Define ecological footprint and biocapacity.
[2 marks]03.2Explain what is meant by a global hectare and why it is used.
[1 mark]03.3Use Table 1 to calculate how many Earths would be needed if everyone lived like the average US citizen.
[3 marks]03.4Use Table 1 to describe the relationship between development and ecological footprint, and explain the concept of Earth Overshoot Day.
[2 marks]03.5Suggest two ways a country such as the UK could reduce its footprint without reducing its HDI.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Ecological footprint: the area of the Earth's surface (global hectares) needed to provide the resources used by a person / population / activity and to absorb the wastes produced
  • Biocapacity: the biological productivity of an area (forests, cropland, grazing, fisheries, built land) - its ability to supply resources and absorb wastes, expressed in global hectares
03.2 (2 marks)
  • A hectare of land / sea with the world-average biological productivity
  • Biocapacities of real areas vary widely so a standard unit allows footprints and biocapacities of different places to be compared
03.3 (1 marks)
  • 8.1 / 1.7 = 4.8 (accept 4.7-4.8)
03.4 (3 marks)
  • Higher HDI is associated with a larger footprint (USA 8.1 vs Bangladesh 0.8), though the UK achieves the same HDI as the USA with about half the footprint
  • Global footprint (2.8) exceeds global biocapacity (1.7) so humanity uses resources faster than they regenerate and CO2 accumulates (overshoot)
  • Earth Overshoot Day is the date each year when a year's worth of resources / waste absorption has been used up; it falls earlier each year (8 August in 2016)
03.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Renewable energy and energy conservation (carbon footprint is the largest component)
  • Reduced meat consumption / food waste; local seasonal food
  • Circular economy: repair, re-use, recycling; longer product lives
  • Public transport, efficient buildings
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 379-380; AQA spec 3.6.4.2.
Question 410 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.2 · Genn pp. 380-383 · SUS-08
[3 marks]04.1Explain what a carbon footprint should include when calculated for a car.
[2 marks]04.2Describe what the WWF Living Planet Index measures and how it is used.
[5 marks]04.3Describe how the Ol Pejeta reserve in Kenya combines commercial cattle ranching with wildlife conservation.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • All greenhouse gases expressed as CO2 equivalent (allowing for different warming strengths)
  • Direct emissions from fuel combustion plus indirect: manufacture of components, maintenance items, final disposal (life-cycle analysis)
  • A fair share of the infrastructure: roads, car parks, signals, street lighting
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Compares the populations of over 18 000 populations of 3700 vertebrate species (terrestrial, freshwater, marine) with 1970 levels
  • Published in the Living Planet Report as a measure of the state of the planet's ecosystems / biodiversity loss over time
04.3 (5 marks)
  • Hardy, disease-resistant Boran cattle; regular tick dipping reduces ticks for wild herbivores too
  • Mobile bomas (night enclosures) protect cattle from lions and leave dung that enriches soil and plant growth
  • Electric fences keep game in and poachers out; biological corridors let animals (except rhinos) move to other reserves; problem elephants relocated
  • Monitoring: radio-collared lions, drones, camera traps, track surveys; endangered species (Grevy's zebra, northern white rhino) protected
  • Community links: ecotourism income funds health, education and water projects; local employment; cattle breeding help for local farmers
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 380-383; AQA spec 3.6.4.2.
Question 510 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4 · Genn pp. 384-389 · SUS-09

The Isle of Eigg (area 31 km2, population about 100) is not connected to the national grid. Table 1 shows its electricity sources.

Table 1
SourceCapacity / kW
Hydroelectric (3 generators)110
Solar PV50
Wind (4 turbines)24
Diesel generators (back-up)160
[3 marks]05.1Explain why Eigg uses a mix of three renewable resources rather than one, and why diesel back-up is still needed.
[3 marks]05.2Describe three other ways in which the Eigg community has increased its sustainability.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why small self-contained communities often find it easier to adopt sustainable lifestyles than large cities.
[2 marks]05.4Explain how large-scale DC electricity grids such as Desertec could increase the use of renewable energy.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Diversity: HEP (wet winters), solar (summer) and wind peak at different times so supply is more reliable - diverse systems resist change
  • No single source could meet demand all year; battery storage on the island grid smooths fluctuations
  • Calm, dry, dull periods still leave gaps so diesel generators provide security (fossil fuel use minimised, not yet eliminated)
05.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Community ownership (Isle of Eigg Trust with Scottish Wildlife Trust) managing the island for people and wildlife
  • Wood biomass heating; insulation such as straw-bale housing; some electric vehicles
  • Habitat protection and creation: fencing rare plants from grazing, planting indigenous woodland (rowan, birch, willow); eagles and hen harriers nesting
  • Recyclables shipped off; garden / food waste composted; low-impact food production; beach litter collection
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Small groups can organise, agree and act quickly; decisions benefit people who see the results directly
  • Resources and wastes are local and visible so feedback is immediate, unlike cities supplied from far away
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Renewable energy is abundant but geographically and temporally variable; a continental grid moves electricity from areas of surplus (desert solar, offshore wind) to areas of demand
  • DC grids do not need every generator synchronised in phase as AC grids do, so coordination across many countries is easier
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 384-389; AQA spec 3.6.4.
