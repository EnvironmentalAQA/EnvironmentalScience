Question 110 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 132-134 · MIN-01
[4 marks]01.1Describe how hydrothermal deposition produces exploitable concentrations of metal ores.
[2 marks]01.2Explain how Proterozoic marine sediments produced the world's major iron ore deposits.
[2 marks]01.3Explain how alluvial (physical sediment) processes separate and concentrate minerals such as gold and tin ore.
[2 marks]01.4Give two examples of mineral deposits formed by biological sedimentation and state their origin.
Show mark scheme
01.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Igneous intrusions (batholiths) of magma cool within the crust, heating groundwater to produce superheated, pressurised water
  • 1The hot water dissolves minerals from the surrounding rock and the mineral-rich solutions travel along fissures / cracks away from the batholith
  • 1As the solutions cool the dissolved minerals crystallise in order of solubility (least soluble first) - fractional crystallisation
  • 1So different metals are separated and concentrated in veins, eg tin, copper, lead, silver, gold, arsenic (SW England batholith)
01.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Dissolved iron compounds in ancient seas were oxidised by the oxygen released by early photosynthetic organisms (2.5-1.8 billion years ago)
  • 1Iron oxides are insoluble so they were deposited as sediments (haematite, magnetite)
01.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Flowing water carries solid particles; the ability to carry them depends on the water velocity and the density of the solids
  • 1As velocity falls, dense minerals (gold, tin ore) are deposited first while lighter material is carried further, concentrating the heavy minerals in particular places
01.4 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Limestone / chalk from the shells of marine organisms
  • 1Coal from terrestrial vegetation
  • 1Crude oil and natural gas from marine organisms (plankton)

02.1 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1As the (linear) purity / grade of a mineral deposit decreases, the amount of the mineral present increases exponentially / logarithmically
02.2 1 mark
1 mark for the correct answer.
  • 12400 / 40 = 60 times
02.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Reserves: the amount that can be exploited now, economically, with existing technology (deposits above the cut-off ore grade)
  • 1Resource: all the material that could be exploited technically and economically now or in the future (with realistic price rises or new technology), including deep, low-grade or protected deposits
  • 1The resource is finite but the reserves change: they rise if prices rise or technology improves, and fall if prices fall
02.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Lowering the cut-off ore grade includes deposits of lower purity in the reserves
  • 1Because the amount of material rises exponentially as purity falls, a small drop in COOG adds a very large mass (eg 0.5-1.0% has 4x the copper of 1.0-2.0%)
02.5 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The total quantity (stock / resource base) is far greater than could ever be used
  • 1But most is at purities too low or in locations too inaccessible for current economic exploitation
  • 1The challenge is developing technologies (low-energy extraction such as bioleaching, phytomining; better exploration) that make low-grade deposits exploitable; energy needs rise rapidly as ore grade falls

03.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Gravimetry: gravimeters detect variations in gravity caused by differences in rock density / mass (metal ores usually denser than sedimentary rock)
  • 1Magnetometry
  • 1Seismic survey: sound / shock waves from controlled explosions or a seismic vibrator; echoes recorded by geophones give the depth, density and shape of rock strata

1 mark.
  • 1Results are available immediately in the field, saving time waiting for laboratory analysis

Question 710 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.7 · Genn pp. 143-145 · MIN-07
[5 marks]07.1Describe how bioleaching is used to extract copper from low-grade ores or spoil heaps, and explain why it may be more sustainable than smelting.
[3 marks]07.2Describe the process of phytomining.
[2 marks]07.3Explain how polymer adsorption may provide a low-energy source of uranium in the future.
Show mark scheme
07.1 5 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Acidophilic bacteria (eg Acidithiobacillus) oxidise sulfide ores, producing sulfuric acid which dissolves the metal ions
  • 1Leachate (rainwater / recirculated solution) percolating through the spoil heap is collected; recirculation increases the metal concentration
  • 1Copper is recovered from solution by electrolysis or by displacement with scrap iron (iron is more reactive so copper is deposited as solid metal)
  • 1Smelting needs chemical reduction at high temperatures; energy needed rises rapidly as ore grade falls, so low-grade ores are uneconomic
  • 1Bioleaching works at low temperatures with low energy inputs, so it can exploit low-grade deposits and wastes (increasing reserves) - mimics natural low-temperature processes
07.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Plants (hyperaccumulators) absorb metal ions from soil / water and concentrate them in their leaves
  • 1The vegetation is harvested and incinerated
  • 1The concentrated metal in the ash is dissolved in acid and separated by electrolysis; also decontaminates polluted sites
07.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Uranium ions dissolved in seawater adsorb onto the surface of synthetic or natural polymers (lignin from wood, chitin from shrimp shells)
  • 1The polymers are collected and the uranium recovered, avoiding mining / high-energy extraction

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Context: Lasky's principle - vast quantities exist at low grade; reserves vs resource; COOG affected by price, technology, energy costs
  • Improved exploration: higher resolution multispectral satellite sensors, IR spectroscopy, gravimetry, magnetometry, seismic, resistivity, portable field analysis, targeted trial drilling

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Lasky's principle means that as grade falls, the quantity of metal available rises enormously, so depletion of high-grade deposits does not mean running out - it means the cut-off ore grade must fall, which depends on price, technology and energy costs. Better exploration finds what remains: high-resolution multispectral and infrared satellite sensors, gravimetry, magnetometry, seismic and resistivity surveys and portable field analysers narrow the search so expensive trial drilling is targeted. Mechanisation allows deeper underground mines and larger open-cast pits, and previously inaccessible deposits - polymetallic nodules and deep-sea sediments, and Antarctica if its protection were ever lifted - become possible, with serious environmental and legal problems.

The most sustainable route to low-grade ores is low-energy extraction: bioleaching with acidophilic bacteria and fungi, phytomining, iron displacement of copper, bacterial adsorption of rare earths and polymer adsorption from seawater all avoid the energy of crushing and smelting vast tonnages. Equally important is extending the use of metal already mined: recycling, Cradle-to-Cradle design so products can be disassembled, longer product lifetimes and substitution of scarce metals - the circular economy approach.

Each strategy has costs: deep-sea mining and Antarctica risk unique habitats, deeper mines need more energy, recycling is limited by dispersal and mixed alloys. The strategies that mimic natural low-temperature processes and keep metals in use are the most sustainable, and exploration and new mining should be the last resort rather than the first.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

When high grade ores run out we can mine lower grade ores instead, but this uses more energy. Better technology like satellites can find new deposits. We could mine the deep sea or Antarctica. Bacteria can be used to extract metals from low grade ore. Recycling metals means we do not need to mine as much. These strategies will help secure supplies of metals in the future.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 names most strategy types but Level 3 explains each (what the surveys measure, why low-temperature extraction matters, what Lasky's principle implies).
  • Level 3 weighs environmental and energy costs and ranks the strategies; Level 1 offers no evaluation.
  • Framing the answer with the concept of cut-off ore grade shows understanding rather than recall.
