[3 marks]01.1State three roles of the IUCN.
[3 marks]01.2A species of lemur has declined from 12 000 to 2 100 mature adults in three generations and occupies 400 km2 of forest. Use Table 1 to determine its Red List category. Explain your answer.
[3 marks]01.3Explain why only a small proportion of species have been assessed for the Red List, and give two criteria used to decide which species to assess.
[1 mark]01.4Give one example of a species whose Red List category improved because of conservation and state the actions responsible.
Show mark scheme
01.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Coordinating global data on biodiversity conservation (Red List)
  • 1Increasing understanding of the importance of biodiversity
  • 1Deploying nature-based solutions to global challenges in climate, food and sustainable development
01.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Decline = (12 000 - 2100) / 12 000 x 100 = 82.5%
  • 1Decline over 80% meets the Critically Endangered criterion even though habitat area (400 km2) and adults (2100) only meet Endangered
  • 1A species is placed in the highest category for which it meets any one criterion, so Critically Endangered
01.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Categorising a species requires a great deal of research (population, range, trends) and resources; about 85 000 of millions of species assessed
  • 1Key species in habitats under particular threat (eg lemurs indicating the state of Madagascar's forests)
  • 1Evolutionarily unique species / EDGE species; endemic species; keystone species; flagship species; species with fragmented populations
01.4 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Iberian lynx: Critically Endangered to Endangered after captive breeding and protection of two habitat areas (population trebled in 15 years); Fregate Island beetle after rat eradication

Question 210 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.1 · Genn pp. 28-29 · PRI-02
[8 marks]02.1Explain the meaning of each of the following terms and explain why such species are given conservation priority.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why the degree of population dispersal is considered when setting priorities.
Show mark scheme
02.1 8 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2EDGE: Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered - few close relatives, often the last species of their genus / family (Bactrian camel, pygmy hippo, northern bald ibis); loss would remove a unique branch of evolution / genes
  • 2Endemic: found in only one area (often islands - Galapagos, Madagascar, Seychelles: Aldabra giant tortoise, red ruffed lemur); if lost locally there is no population elsewhere to recolonise
  • 2Keystone: disproportionately important for the structure of the community relative to its abundance - predation of dominant species, seed dispersal, habitat creation (jaguar, forest elephants keeping paths and water holes open, wolves, beavers); losing them causes loss of many other species
  • 2Flagship: high public profile (tiger, panda, elephant, orang-utan) raising funds and support that also protects the whole habitat and less charismatic species (fungi, bats, moths)
02.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A fragmented population may consist of isolated groups each too small to be viable (resources, inbreeding) even if total numbers are large
  • 1A species confined to a single area (eg bonobos in one country) is vulnerable to any local change / conflict, unlike chimpanzees spread across many countries
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 28-29; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.1.
Question 310 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 30-31 · PRI-03
[4 marks]03.1Describe four ways in which the Wildlife and Countryside Act (1981) protects wildlife in the UK.
[4 marks]03.2Explain the difference between CITES Appendix I and Appendix II, giving an example species for each.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why controlling international trade can be more effective than trying to stop collection of a species in its own country.
Show mark scheme
03.1 4 marks
Any 4 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 4.
  • 1Designation of protected areas (SSSIs, MCZs)
  • 1Protection of wild birds and their nests (except some pests / game birds)
  • 1Protection of named mammals such as otters, dormice, red squirrels, badgers and their setts
  • 1Uprooting wild plants generally illegal
  • 1Bats must not be disturbed; roof timber treatments must be non-toxic to bats
03.2 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Appendix I: species threatened with extinction - all international (commercial) trade banned, except movement for conservation breeding
  • 1Examples: great apes, big cats, rhinos, blue whale
  • 1Appendix II: species that may become threatened if trade is not closely controlled - trade permitted under licence from countries where the species is well protected
  • 1Examples: Honduras mahogany, hippopotamus, green iguana, great white / basking shark, Venus fly trap
03.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Collection may be hard to police in remote areas / poor countries, but if the animals or products cannot be exported / imported there is no market
  • 1Removing the profit removes the incentive to collect
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 30-31; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.2.
Question 410 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 30, 38-39 · PRI-04

Table 1 shows some designated protected areas in the UK.

[4 marks]04.1Complete Table 1 to show the legislation under which each area is designated and its key feature.
Table 1
DesignationDesignated underKey feature
Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI)
Special Protection Area (SPA)
Ramsar site
Marine Conservation Zone (MCZ)
[3 marks]04.2Explain how designating a protected area protects habitats and species.
[3 marks]04.3Explain why voluntary agri-environmental schemes are needed in addition to legislation.
Show mark scheme
04.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1SSSI: UK legislation (Wildlife and Countryside Act); best examples of UK flora, fauna, geological features; management plan agreed with owner who must notify Operations Likely to Damage (ploughing, drainage, pesticides, tree planting / removal)
  • 1SPA: EU Birds Directive; places of international importance for birds (with SACs under the Habitats Directive form the Natura 2000 network; may restrict activities in surrounding areas)
  • 1Ramsar: Ramsar Convention (intergovernmental agreement); internationally important wetlands, originally for waterfowl, now wetland conservation and wise use generally
  • 1MCZ: UK marine legislation (Marine and Coastal Access Act) under the W&CA framework; protects marine habitats / species by restricting activities such as fishing and dredging; NNR: best SSSIs with complete communities
04.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Legal status: restrictions on damaging activities within the area (and sometimes in surrounding areas, eg drainage)
  • 1Management agreements between owners and statutory bodies to maintain suitable conditions
  • 1But it does not protect against external influences such as air pollution or climate change; very few sites are state-owned so protection depends on owner cooperation
04.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Much of the UK's wildlife lives in habitats created by farming (hay meadows, hedgerows) which only survive if the farming continues
  • 1Managing land for wildlife costs money / reduces income so farmers need payments (points for beetle banks, hedgerows, buffer strips, wild bird seed, low-input grassland)
  • 1Benefits everyone so society pays through central funding; legislation cannot compel positive management
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 30, 38-39; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.2.

[1 mark]05.1Calculate the percentage decline in the blue whale population between 1900 and 1986.
[3 marks]05.2Explain why whale populations were so easily over-exploited.
[4 marks]05.3Describe how the International Whaling Commission regulates whaling.
[2 marks]05.4Explain why the ITTO has been less successful than the IWC in achieving sustainable exploitation.
Show mark scheme
05.1 1 mark
1 mark for the correct answer.
  • 1(240 000 - 2 000) / 240 000 x 100 = 99.2%
05.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Whales are k-selected: slow growing, late maturing, one calf every few years, so populations recover very slowly
  • 1Technology (harpoon cannons, steam / factory ships) allowed catches far above the sustainable yield within decades
  • 1No regulation / open access to the ocean; whales were valuable (oil, spermaceti) and easily found
05.3 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Moratorium on commercial whaling since 1986 until populations recover
  • 1Total protection for certain species; whale sanctuaries
  • 1Limits on numbers and sizes taken; protection of suckling mothers and calves
  • 1Research into whale biology and threats (ship strikes, net entanglement); exceptions: aboriginal subsistence quotas (eg Inuit bowheads), 'scientific' permits (Japan - ruled not research in 2014), Norway / Iceland set their own quotas
05.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1ITTO's 1990 target of trade only from sustainably managed forests by 2000 was not met and little progress since
  • 1Timber comes from many countries / private land with weak enforcement, high demand and alternative land uses; whaling involves few nations and a single treaty body
