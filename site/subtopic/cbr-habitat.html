Question 110 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 32-34 · CBR-01
[2 marks]01.1Explain the difference between in-situ and ex-situ conservation and state when ex-situ conservation is needed.
[4 marks]01.2Give four criteria used to select species for captive breeding and release programmes.
[4 marks]01.3Explain why each of the following species would be difficult to keep or breed in captivity.
Show mark scheme
01.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1In-situ: conserving species in their natural habitat with the whole community; ex-situ: away from the natural habitat, eg zoos, botanic gardens, seed banks, captive breeding
  • 1Needed when conservation in the wild is impossible or insufficient to prevent extinction (habitat gone, population too small, poaching)
01.2 4 marks
Any 4 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 4.
  • 1Is the wild population threatened?
  • 1Is there a genetically diverse captive population?
  • 1Is in-situ conservation failing?
  • 1Is keeping a captive population realistic (habitat size, food, relationships, cost)?
  • 1Is release likely to be successful now or in future (suitable habitat, threats removed)?
01.3 4 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Blue whale: habitat size far too large to provide; food (huge quantities of krill / plankton) cannot be supplied; cost
  • 2Large blue butterfly: complex inter-species relationship - larvae are taken into ant nests where they overwinter feeding on ant grubs; requires specific ant species and thyme food plant

Question 210 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 33-35 · CBR-02

The Hawaiian goose (nene) was reduced by hunting, habitat loss and introduced predators to about 30 birds. A captive breeding programme started with just seven individuals and has increased the wild population to over 2000.

[4 marks]02.1Explain why starting the programme with seven individuals caused problems, and describe how these problems are managed.
[3 marks]02.2Explain three reasons, other than gene pool size, why species kept in captivity may fail to breed.
[3 marks]02.3Describe how artificial insemination and embryo transfer increase breeding success in captive mammals.
Show mark scheme
02.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Very small gene pool so individuals are closely related and inbreeding is likely
  • 1Harmful recessive genes are expressed when both parents carry them: some goslings had thin hair-like feathers with poor insulation so survival in cold weather was low
  • 1A stud book records family trees so breeding is arranged between the least related individuals (often coordinated by a specialist zoo)
  • 1Exchange of individuals / semen between zoos; avoid hybridisation with related species / subspecies
02.2 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Breeding is triggered by stimuli (day length, light, temperature, food, body fat) which may be unknown / not provided
  • 1Population interactions: mixing breeding and non-breeding individuals causes conflict; some need a choice of partners, others pair for life
  • 1Breeding habitat needed: courtship display sites, nest sites, social groups (flamingos only breed in large groups - mirrors used)
  • 1Stress / disturbance
02.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1AI: semen collected (and can be stored / cryopreserved for years) and inserted into the female, so animals need not be transported or meet; avoids dangerous mating (elephants, rhinos) and partner rejection
  • 1Embryo transfer: a female of the endangered species (eg bongo) is treated with hormones to release many eggs, which are fertilised and implanted into surrogate females of a common related species (eland)
  • 1So many offspring are produced per cycle instead of one calf per year, overcoming the shortage of breeding females
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 33-35; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 310 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 36-37 · CBR-03
[3 marks]03.1Explain the difference between hard and soft release and state the types of species for which each is suitable.
[5 marks]03.2Describe five features of a suitable release site.
[2 marks]03.3Explain the importance of post-release monitoring.
Show mark scheme
03.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Hard release: individuals released with no post-release support - suitable for species whose behaviour is instinctive and need no learned skills (insects, fish, amphibians, reptiles)
  • 1Soft release: gradual release into larger areas with food provided while they learn to find food, avoid predators - needed for mammals and birds
  • 1Problems faced: finding / recognising food and poisonous foods, hunting skills (captive-born predators never learned), avoiding predators, acceptance into wild social groups
03.2 5 marks
Any 5 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 5.
  • 1Large enough suitable habitat for a viable population
  • 1Reliable food supply
  • 1Low predation risk / original threat removed
  • 1Suitable breeding sites
  • 1Water
  • 1Support of the local human population
  • 1Official / legal protection of habitat and species
03.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Tracking (radio / GPS tags, rings) shows survival, dispersal and breeding of released animals so the success of the project can be assessed
  • 1Identifies causes of failure so future releases can be improved / extra support given
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 36-37; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 410 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 35-37 · CBR-04
[3 marks]04.1Describe how cloning could be used to conserve the northern white rhinoceros, and explain why success has been limited.
[3 marks]04.2Explain how micro-propagation and cloning are used to conserve rare plants such as the Atlantic black poplar.
[4 marks]04.3Describe the purpose and methods of the Millennium Seed Bank.
Show mark scheme
04.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1The nucleus of a stem cell from the endangered species is transferred into an empty egg of a closely related species (southern white rhino)
  • 1The embryo is implanted into a surrogate female of the related species which gives birth to a northern white rhino
  • 1Very low success rates with wildlife so far; deep-frozen cells could allow clones even after the last individuals die
04.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Micro-propagation: tissue culture producing many cell clusters from one plant, each grown into a genetically identical plant
  • 1Black poplar: cuttings / pollarded stems of pure native trees are rooted to produce many identical trees
  • 1Needed because the flowers are pollinated by introduced subspecies producing hybrids, so cloning maintains the pure gene pool (but no genetic diversity)
04.3 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Stores seeds of wild plants (especially threatened species and crop wild relatives) so species are not lost if extinct in the wild
  • 1Seeds collected worldwide; target of 10 000 seeds per species to maintain a diverse gene pool
  • 1Stored dry and refrigerated / frozen in an underground reinforced vault to reduce risk of accident
  • 1Seeds germinated periodically to check viability and replenish stocks; can be used for reintroduction / breeding
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 35-37; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 510 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 40-42 · CBR-05

A conservation organisation is converting 700 ha of arable farmland on the coast into a wetland reserve for breeding and wintering birds (as at Wallasea Island).

[4 marks]05.1Explain why habitat area and shape are important in the design of the reserve.
[3 marks]05.2Explain how the reserve could be designed to provide a range of niches.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why some species may need to be deliberately introduced while others colonise naturally.
[1 mark]05.4Explain the value of a biological corridor linking the reserve to other wetlands.
Show mark scheme
05.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Area must be large enough to support viable populations (breeding numbers high enough, gene pool large enough to avoid inbreeding); species higher up food chains need larger areas (lower density)
  • 1Large areas contain a full range of resources (eg different fruiting trees / feeding areas through the year)
  • 1Shape: the perimeter has edge conditions (a mix of neighbouring habitats); a compact shape maximises core habitat for species needing core conditions
  • 1Some species benefit from edges (higher light) or small isolated habitats (ponds too small for predatory fish suit amphibians)
05.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Varied topography: banks, lagoons, islands, ditches created with machinery give a range of water depths (emergent, floating-leaved, submerged plant zones)
  • 1Islands provide roosting / nesting sites safe from terrestrial predators (foxes)
  • 1Control of salinity (eg intertidal conditions for opossum shrimps feeding dunlin and avocet); scrapes for skylarks; different water depths for waders and ducks
05.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Mobile species (birds, flying insects) colonise easily
  • 1Less mobile species (many plants, amphibians, invertebrates) may not reach an isolated site so must be introduced; keystone species especially
05.4 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Allows dispersal of surplus young, mixing of gene pools (less inbreeding) and recolonisation if a population dies out; avoids hazards (eg rope bridges for howler monkeys over roads)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 40-42; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 610 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.4, 3.1.3.1 · Genn pp. 43-46 · CBR-06
[6 marks]06.1Describe how each of the following abiotic factors can be managed to increase biodiversity in a nature reserve.
[4 marks]06.2Explain how biotic factors can be managed to increase the survival of a species. Give four examples.
Show mark scheme
06.1 6 marks
2 marks for each point (partial credit for a partly correct point).
  • 2Light: selective felling to create clearings for plants needing high light while retaining dense canopy for shade species; clearings also give warm sand / soil for lizard eggs; leave dead wood; woodland floor plants (bluebells) grow before the canopy leafs
  • 2Dissolved oxygen: narrowing a slow river increases flow and turbulence so more oxygen dissolves, suiting trout and mayfly larvae; shallow water warms quickly for egg development
  • 2Nutrients: removing fertile topsoil / not fertilising allows plants of nutrient-poor soils (eg sundew on acidic nitrate-deficient soils) that cannot compete on fertile ground; dung patches add local nutrients
06.2 4 marks
Any 4 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 4.
  • 1Food: wildflower grasslands for seed-eating birds; providing food species
  • 1Control of predation: island nest sites unreachable by foxes; trapping / culling / eradicating introduced rats (Scilly Isles - Manx shearwater and storm petrel breeding success increased)
  • 1Control of competitors: removing rhododendron so wildflowers get light
  • 1Pollination: a range of flowering plants with different flowering times so pollinators have food all season
  • 1Seed dispersal species (hippos, elephants, monkeys) conserved; control of introduced pathogens

Question 810 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 47-49 · CBR-08
[4 marks]08.1Describe coppicing and explain how it increases the biodiversity of a woodland.
[4 marks]08.2Explain how the reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park increased biodiversity.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why culling or removing species may be necessary in habitat management.
Show mark scheme
08.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Trees (hazel, oak) are cut to ground level every 3-30 years and allowed to regrow from the stump
  • 1About 0.5 ha cut each year on a rotation (eg 8 years) so the wood is a patchwork of areas of different ages
  • 1Each age has its own community: newly cut areas get high light for wildflowers and butterflies; older areas give dense cover for nesting birds / dormice
  • 1Pollarding cuts above browsing height so deer / livestock cannot eat the regrowth
08.2 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Wolves (top predator, exterminated by 1926) reduced deer populations and changed their behaviour
  • 1Fewer young trees eaten so trees and riverside vegetation recovered
  • 1More trees allowed beaver populations to increase; beaver dams increased wetland area
  • 1Wetland species (birds, fish, invertebrates) became more common; overall biodiversity rose, though wolves cause problems for livestock farmers
08.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Undesirable species (introduced predators / competitors, or natural succession species such as scrub, conifers in the New Forest heathland) reduce survival of the target species
  • 1Removal restores suitable conditions (eg heathland for sand lizards and smooth snakes) or reduces predation / competition

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • In-situ: species remain in natural conditions with the whole community and inter-species relationships; protected areas (SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Ramsar, MCZ), land ownership, agri-environment schemes, habitat creation and design (area, shape, corridors, diversity), management of abiotic and biotic factors, plagioclimax maintenance, population control, reintroductions and rewilding (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish beavers)
  • Limits of in-situ: external threats (pollution, climate change), enforcement, cost, conflicts with land use, small fragmented populations
  • Ex-situ: captive breeding when in-situ is impossible or insufficient; selection criteria; difficulties keeping and breeding (habitat size, food, relationships, breeding triggers, group dynamics, gene pool / inbreeding, hybridisation); techniques (stud books, cryopreservation, AI, embryo transfer, micropropagation, cloning); seed banks; successes (Hawaiian goose, Iberian lynx, red kite, large blue)
  • Limits of ex-situ: expensive, few species, loss of wild behaviour, small gene pools, release requires suitable habitat and post-release support (hard vs soft release, monitoring)
  • Judgement: ex-situ is a temporary safeguard / support that only succeeds if in-situ habitat exists to return to; both needed, in-situ fundamental
Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

In-situ conservation keeps a species in its natural habitat with the whole community it depends on - food species, pollinators, predators and the abiotic conditions it is adapted to. It is delivered through protected areas such as SSSIs, NNRs, SACs, SPAs, Ramsar sites and Marine Conservation Zones, land ownership by conservation bodies, agri-environment schemes and habitat creation and management. Its weaknesses are that external threats such as pollution and climate change cross reserve boundaries, enforcement and cost, conflicts with other land uses, and the fact that many populations are already small and fragmented.

Ex-situ captive breeding is used when in-situ protection is impossible or insufficient. It is hard: animals need enough space, the right food, social groupings and breeding triggers, and small founder populations risk inbreeding and hybridisation, which studbooks, artificial insemination and embryo transfer help to manage. It is expensive, works for few species, and captive animals may lose wild behaviour; release succeeds only where suitable habitat exists and with soft release and post-release monitoring.

The two are not alternatives. Ex-situ methods are a temporary safeguard that can rescue a species from extinction, as with the Arabian oryx, but they only succeed if there is protected habitat to return to. In-situ conservation is therefore fundamental and ex-situ a support for it; the relative importance depends on how close to extinction the species is.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

In-situ conservation means protecting species in their natural habitat, for example in nature reserves and national parks. This is good because the species stays in the wild with the other species it needs. Ex-situ conservation means captive breeding in zoos and then releasing the animals back into the wild. This can save species that are nearly extinct but it is expensive and animals may not survive when released. Both methods are important and are often used together.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 defines both approaches correctly but Level 3 gives specific mechanisms (types of protected area, breeding difficulties, release methods) and named examples.
  • Level 3 weighs the limitations of each approach explicitly; Level 2 gives one limitation for ex-situ only.
  • 'Both are important' is not a judgement about relative importance; Level 3 explains why in-situ is fundamental and when ex-situ matters most.
09.2 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Restoring natural processes / communities to a habitat changed by humans, often by reintroducing missing (keystone) species and removing human management such as grazing
