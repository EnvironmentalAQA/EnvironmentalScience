Table 1 shows the textural composition of four soil samples.

Table 1

[3 marks]01.1Identify the sample that is a loam and explain why loams are ideal for cultivating most crops.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why sample A is likely to have the highest permeability but the lowest nutrient retention.
[2 marks]01.3Compare the thermal capacity and root penetration of soils A and C.
[2 marks]01.4Describe how the texture of a soil sample could be determined by sedimentation.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • B (about 40:40:20 sand:silt:clay)
  • Balanced mix gives good drainage and aeration from the sand fraction
  • Water retention and nutrient (ion) retention from the clay fraction; easy root penetration and cultivation
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Large sand particles leave large pore spaces so water drains rapidly (high permeability / aeration)
  • Nutrient ions adsorb onto the (charged) surfaces of clay particles but not onto sand
  • With only 5% clay there is little surface to hold ions so nutrients are leached; low water retention too
01.3 (2 marks)
  • C (clay) holds much water so has a high thermal capacity: warms and cools slowly; A warms rapidly after cold weather
  • Clay particles held together by capillary water so roots find it hard to push between them; penetration is easy in sandy A
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Shake a known mass of soil with water in a measuring cylinder and leave to settle; particles settle in order of size (sand first, then silt, then clay; organic matter floats)
  • Measure the depth / volume of each layer to calculate the percentage of each fraction, then use the soil triangle to name the textural class

Question 210 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.1 · Genn pp. 158-161 · SOIL-02
[6 marks]02.1Explain the roles of three named groups of soil organisms in maintaining soil fertility.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why a soil pH between 5.5 and 7.0 is best for fertility.
[2 marks]02.3Explain how soil compaction by heavy machinery reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (6 marks)
  • Detritivores (worms, beetle larvae, millipedes, woodlice) physically break up dead organic matter, increasing its surface area, and release nutrients; worms create tunnels improving drainage and aeration
  • Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) secrete enzymes that break down dead organic matter, releasing nutrient ions (ammonification) and forming humus which binds particles into crumb peds
  • Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas to ammonium ions; nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite then nitrate for root absorption
  • Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with roots increasing phosphate uptake
02.2 (2 marks)
  • This is the range of tolerance of most plants and soil biota; acidic soils leach nutrients and mobilise toxic aluminium, damaging root membranes
  • In alkaline soils phosphates become insoluble / unavailable
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Interstitial spaces are reduced so aeration falls (aerobic processes such as nitrification and decomposition slow) and roots cannot penetrate
  • Infiltration falls so waterlogging is more likely and runoff / erosion increase
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 158-161; AQA spec 3.2.5.1.
Question 310 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.2 · Genn pp. 162-164 · SOIL-03

Figure 1 shows the annual soil loss measured on experimental plots with different vegetation cover on a 10% slope in a region with heavy seasonal rain.

Figure 1
[2 marks]03.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]03.2Explain four ways in which vegetation reduces the rate of soil erosion.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why ploughing up and down a slope causes more erosion than contour ploughing.
[2 marks]03.4Explain why the region's climate makes the soil vulnerable to both wind and water erosion.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Soil loss increases as vegetation cover decreases: forest lowest (0.5), bare soil highest (60 t ha-1 yr-1) - over 100 times more
  • Ploughing downslope loses nearly three times as much as contour ploughing for the same crop
03.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Leaves / leaf litter intercept raindrops so soil particles are not dislodged by rain splash
  • Roots bind soil particles together
  • Vegetation acts as a windbreak reducing wind velocity and the kinetic energy available to carry particles
  • Plants increase infiltration (root channels, organic matter) so less surface runoff
  • Organic matter / humus from vegetation binds particles into stable peds
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Furrows running downslope act as channels / gulleys that increase the velocity (kinetic energy) of runoff
  • Furrows along the contour trap the water so it loses energy, deposits particles and infiltrates
03.4 (2 marks)
  • In the dry season the soil (especially low-clay soils) is loose and dry with little cohesion so wind blows it away
  • In the wet season heavy rain causes rain splash and surface runoff when infiltration capacity is exceeded
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 162-164; AQA spec 3.2.5.2.
Question 410 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.2 · Genn pp. 162-164 · SOIL-04
[4 marks]04.1Explain how each of the following human activities increases soil erosion.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why landslides become more likely on steep slopes some years after deforestation.
[4 marks]04.3Describe four environmental impacts of accelerated soil erosion away from the field where it occurs.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Overgrazing: vegetation is eaten faster than it grows so soil is exposed; hooves disturb the soil, damage roots and compact it, reducing infiltration and increasing runoff
  • Reduced biota (ploughing, agrochemicals, less organic matter): less humus so less adhesion between particles; fewer worms so less aeration and lower infiltration capacity; fewer nutrients released so less vegetation cover
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Tree roots that held the soil together decompose so the soil is bound less strongly
  • Heavy rain adds mass and lubricates particles; without interception more water enters the soil, making slumping more likely
04.3 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Sedimentation in rivers reduces flow capacity so flooding downstream is more likely
  • Sedimentation in reservoirs reduces their storage volume
  • Coastal sedimentation covering reefs / seagrass; turbidity reducing light for aquatic plants
  • Increased atmospheric particulates from wind erosion worsening asthma; dust covering crops / urban areas
  • Desertification as vegetation cannot re-establish; reduced rainfall
  • Nutrients (phosphates) carried on soil particles causing eutrophication
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 162-164; AQA spec 3.2.5.2.

[2 marks]05.1Calculate the annual soil loss for beans with spring ploughing, ploughed up and down the slope. Give your answer to one decimal place.
[2 marks]05.2Calculate the annual soil loss for wheat with mulch tillage and strip cropping with contour ploughing.
[3 marks]05.3The natural rate of soil formation on the farm is 1 t ha-1 yr-1. Use your answers and Table 2 to evaluate the sustainability of the two farming systems.
[2 marks]05.4Explain why the USLE gives only an estimate of soil loss.
[1 mark]05.5Suggest which single factor in the USLE a farmer can change most easily.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • 110 x 0.67 x 1.35 x (0.50 x 0.90) x 1.0
  • = 44.8 t ha-1 yr-1 (accept 44.7-44.8)
05.2 (2 marks)
  • 110 x 0.67 x 1.35 x (0.35 x 0.60) x 0.25
  • = 5.2 t ha-1 yr-1
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Beans / up-and-down ploughing: severe class (over 30) and about 45 times the formation rate - clearly unsustainable, soil depth will fall
  • Wheat / mulch / strip cropping: moderate class, reduced by about 88% but still 5 times the formation rate so not fully sustainable
  • Further measures (long-term crops, terracing, windbreaks, more organic matter) or grazing land (C = 0.02) needed to bring loss below 1 t ha-1 yr-1
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Factors are averages / arbitrary values for typical conditions; rainfall erosivity varies between years / storms
  • Does not include gully erosion, landslides, wind erosion or local variation in soil; based on data from particular regions
05.5 (1 marks)
  • C (crop / tillage) or P (erosion control practice) - R, K and LS are fixed by climate, soil and topography

Question 610 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.3 · Genn pp. 164-166 · SOIL-06
[5 marks]06.1Complete Table 1 to explain how each method reduces soil erosion.
Table 1

Terracing
Tied ridging

Mulching

[2 marks]06.2Explain why long-term crops such as tea, coffee or orchards reduce erosion compared with annual arable crops.
[3 marks]06.3Describe three ways livestock can be managed to reduce soil erosion.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (5 marks)
  • Terracing: sloping land divided into narrow level fields held by retaining walls along contours; water slows as it crosses each terrace and infiltrates instead of running off
  • Tied ridging: on nearly flat land a criss-cross of intersecting ridges retains rainwater, increasing infiltration and preventing runoff
  • Windbreaks: hedgerows / tree rows reduce wind velocity (for up to 30 times their height) so the wind has less kinetic energy to carry particles; protect bare fields after harvest
  • Mulching: organic matter on the surface protects soil from raindrop impact and wind; decomposes to humus which binds particles; retains moisture
  • Strip cropping: strips of different crops harvested at different times (at 90° to the wind) so there is always a crop protecting part of the field; slows runoff
06.2 (2 marks)
  • No frequent replanting / soil disturbance (ploughing) that breaks up soil structure and exposes particles
  • Permanent root systems and canopy cover protect the soil all year
06.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Control stocking density so vegetation regrows faster than it is eaten
  • Restrict access to vulnerable areas such as river banks (fencing)
  • Move / remove livestock at high-risk times, eg after heavy rain when soil is wet
  • Rotational grazing to rest pastures
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 164-166; AQA spec 3.2.5.3.
Question 710 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.1, 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 158-159, 412 · SOIL-07

A student determined the water and organic matter content of a soil sample. Table 1 shows the results.

Table 1
MeasurementMass / g
Empty crucible25.0
Crucible + fresh soil65.0
Crucible + soil after drying at 105 °C to constant mass55.0
Crucible + soil after heating in a furnace at 550 °C52.6
[2 marks]07.1Calculate the percentage water content of the fresh soil.
[2 marks]07.2Calculate the percentage organic matter content of the dry soil.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why the soil is dried to constant mass before the organic matter is burnt off.
[3 marks]07.4Explain how the organic matter content affects the fertility and erosion risk of this soil.
[1 mark]07.5Suggest one reason why two students measuring the same field might get different organic matter results.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Water = 65.0 - 55.0 = 10.0 g; fresh soil = 40.0 g
  • 10.0 / 40.0 x 100 = 25.0%
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Organic matter = 55.0 - 52.6 = 2.4 g; dry soil = 30.0 g
  • 2.4 / 30.0 x 100 = 8.0%
07.3 (2 marks)
  • So all water is removed and the mass loss on burning is due to organic matter only
  • Heating until mass no longer changes shows all water has evaporated
07.4 (3 marks)
  • Organic matter releases nutrients as it decomposes and provides food for soil biota
  • Increases water retention and, as humus, binds particles into crumb peds improving structure / aeration
  • Surface organic matter / humus adhesion reduce rain splash and wind erosion
07.5 (1 marks)
  • Samples taken from different depths / locations (organic matter is concentrated near the surface); incomplete burning; different heating times

Question 85 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5 · Genn pp. 158-169 · SOIL-08
[1 mark]08.1Which soil property is most directly responsible for capillary rise of water from below? Tick one box.
  • A  High sand content
  • B  High clay content
  • C  Crumb peds
  • D  High pH
[3 marks]08.2Distinguish between hygroscopic, capillary and gravitational water in soil and state which are available to plants.
[1 mark]08.3Explain why crumb peds produce more fertile soil than platy peds.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (1 marks)
  • B - tiny pores between clay particles allow capillary rise; none in sand
08.2 (3 marks)
  • Hygroscopic: held tightly by adhesive forces on particle surfaces; cannot drain and cannot be absorbed by plants
  • Capillary: held in pores by surface tension; does not drain but can be absorbed by plants
  • Gravitational: drains freely through large pores; available to plants only briefly as it passes
08.3 (1 marks)
  • Small round crumbs leave spaces giving good drainage, aeration and easy root penetration; large flat platy peds pack together reducing all three
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 158-169; AQA spec 3.2.5.
