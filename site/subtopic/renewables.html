Question 110 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 199-203 · REN-01

Table 1 shows the efficiency and cost of four types of solar photovoltaic (PV) cell.

Table 1
PV cell typeEfficiency / %Relative cost per m2
Amorphous silicon (thin film)81
Polycrystalline silicon162
Monocrystalline silicon with anti-reflective surface223
Multi-junction cell4025
[2 marks]01.1Describe how a photovoltaic cell generates electricity.
[3 marks]01.2Explain how anti-reflective surfaces and multi-junction cells increase efficiency.
[3 marks]01.3Use Table 1 to suggest which cell type would be chosen for a large solar farm on cheap desert land and which for a satellite. Justify your choices.
[2 marks]01.4Explain why solar PV output is intermittent and unpredictable.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Photons of light absorbed by the (n-type silicon) upper layer dislodge electrons, making it negatively charged
  • Electrons flow through an external conductor / circuit to the relatively positive lower layer; the current powers appliances
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Smooth cells reflect about 30% of the light; grooved / textured surfaces (mimicking moth eyes) reflect light into the cell rather than away
  • Multi-junction cells have several layers of different materials, each absorbing different wavelengths
  • So a greater proportion of the available light is absorbed and converted to electricity
01.3 (3 marks)
  • Solar farm: polycrystalline / thin film - lowest cost per unit electricity where land area is not limiting (efficiency per m2 matters less)
  • Satellite: multi-junction - highest efficiency where mass / area is critical and cost is less important
  • Cost per watt = cost / efficiency: thin film 0.125, poly 0.125, mono 0.136, multi-junction 0.625 per unit
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Intermittent: no output at night and low in winter (daily and seasonal cycles) - predictable
  • Unpredictable: cloud cover, dust and smoke reduce intensity and scatter light and cannot be forecast accurately
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 199-203; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 210 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 200-202 · REN-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain how a ground-source heat pump can deliver more heat energy to a building than the electrical energy used to run it.
[3 marks]02.2Explain how Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) with thermal storage overcomes the intermittency of solar power.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why CSP can only be used in areas with little cloud, whereas PV can be used anywhere.
[2 marks]02.4Describe two environmental impacts of solar PV.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • A fluid in a buried ground loop absorbs low-temperature heat from the ground; at the expansion valve the fluid boils at low pressure absorbing this heat
  • A compressor raises the pressure so the gas condenses, releasing heat at a higher temperature inside the building
  • The electricity only moves heat rather than creating it, so up to four times as much heat is delivered as electricity used (less if the source is colder)
02.2 (3 marks)
  • Parabolic reflectors focus sunlight onto tubes of oil, increasing the energy density and reaching high temperatures
  • The heat is transferred to molten salt (potassium nitrate) stored in large insulated tanks at up to 550 °C
  • The stored heat boils water to drive steam turbines whenever electricity is required, including at night
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Reflectors can only focus parallel rays of direct sunlight; cloud, dust and smoke scatter light so it cannot be concentrated
  • PV cells convert diffuse as well as direct light, though output is lower
02.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Manufacture: extraction / processing of silicon, metals and plastics; toxic wastes (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium)
  • Land take by large solar farms competing with other uses / affecting desert flora and fauna
  • Water for cleaning panels in arid areas
  • Disposal of old panels
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 200-202; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 310 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 203-205 · REN-03

A rural community in Pakistan is considering a low-head helical (Archimedes screw) turbine on a river rather than joining a high-head dam scheme.

[5 marks]03.1Explain why low-head turbines are more suitable than high-head HEP schemes for many rural areas.
[1 mark]03.2Suggest one reason why such schemes may not be installed even at suitable sites.
[2 marks]03.3The maximum flow rate at the site is 6.5 m3 s-1 giving an output of 100 kW at 90% turbine efficiency. At 10% of maximum flow the efficiency falls to 40%. Calculate the output at 10% of maximum flow.
[2 marks]03.4Outline two ways a high-head reservoir can affect the river downstream.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (5 marks)
  • No large dam / reservoir needed so no flooding of farmland, homes or habitats and no barrier across the whole river (run-of-river / micro-hydro diverts part of the flow)
  • Suitable for rivers with a small drop in height and lower flow rates that are common in lowland rural areas
  • Small scale matches local demand; electricity used locally without long transmission lines / grid connection
  • Low construction cost and simple technology maintained by the community
  • Helical turbines tolerate high turbidity / debris without damage and fish pass through unharmed; Kaplan turbines with adjustable blades cope with variable flow
03.2 (1 marks)
  • Lack of capital / finance, technical knowledge or grid access; seasonal drying of rivers; conflicts with irrigation water; planning / land ownership
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Output is proportional to flow x efficiency: 100 x 0.10 x (40 / 90)
  • = 4.4 kW (accept 4.4-4.5)
03.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Constant regulated flow removes seasonal extremes: dry-season sandbanks used by nesting birds / turtles lost; high flows that clear gravel spawning beds lost; floodplain no longer flooded
  • Sudden flow increases for peak generation raise turbidity, wash away species, reduce light for photosynthesis
  • Sediment trapped so less nutrient replenishment downstream; barrier to migrating fish (salmon, sturgeon) and isolation of populations
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 203-205; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 410 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 206-209 · REN-04

The kinetic energy available to a wind turbine is proportional to the cube of the wind velocity. Table 1 shows mean wind velocities at four possible wind farm sites.

Table 1
SiteMean wind velocity / m s-1Distance to grid / kmOther features
A: upland ridge912Migrating bird route; peat bog
B: coastal farmland83Near village
C: offshore shallow sea1015Sandbanks
D: inland plain62Arable farmland
[2 marks]04.1Calculate how many times more kinetic energy is available at site C than at site D.
[5 marks]04.2Use Table 1 to evaluate the four sites for a large wind farm.
[2 marks]04.3Give two advantages of vertical axis wind turbines (VAWTs) over horizontal axis turbines (HAWTs).
[1 mark]04.4Explain how direct-drive turbines improve on turbines with a gearbox.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • 103 / 63 = 1000 / 216
  • = 4.6 times (accept 4.63)
04.2 (5 marks)
  • C has the highest velocity (most energy, and energy rises with the cube of velocity), strong reliable winds over water, few land-use conflicts, but long expensive cable and offshore installation / maintenance costs
  • A is windy but on a migration route (bird strikes) and peat bog (foundations / tracks damage hydrology; carbon release); far from grid; scenic objections
  • B is close to the grid with good wind but near a village (noise, visual objection, shadow flicker) - possible with careful siting
  • D is cheap to connect and land can still be farmed between turbines, but low velocity gives little energy
  • Judgement, eg C or B; turbines spaced 3-5 blade diameters apart to avoid wind shadow
04.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Driven by wind from any direction so no motor to turn them; suitable for turbulent urban sites
  • Generator can be at ground level so a lighter tower
  • Quieter
  • Operate at lower wind velocities
  • Blades more visible so fewer bird deaths
04.4 (1 marks)
  • No gearbox: fewer breakdowns, less friction so start generating at lower wind speeds, quieter, lighter nacelle (DC converted to AC by an inverter)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 206-209; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 510 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 210-211, 214-217 · REN-05
[2 marks]05.1Explain the locational requirements for harnessing wave power.
[2 marks]05.2Describe how one named wave power device generates electricity.
[2 marks]05.3Give two design problems that have delayed the development of wave power.
[4 marks]05.4Compare the environmental impacts of tidal barrages, tidal lagoons and in-stream tidal turbines.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • High mean wind velocities that are consistent in strength and direction
  • A long fetch (distance of open water) over which waves build up, eg Atlantic coasts of the UK
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Oscillating water column (eg Islay Limpet): waves force water up and down in a submerged chamber; the air forced in and out drives a turbine
  • Or point absorber (floating buoy moving relative to a fixed base turns a generator); surface attenuator (Pelamis - hinged sections push pistons pumping fluid over a turbine); overtopping device (Wave Dragon - waves fill a reservoir above sea level which drains through turbines); oscillating wave surge converter (Oyster)
05.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Equipment must withstand storms and corrosion
  • Difficult to anchor off stormy coasts / in deep water
  • Expensive to transmit electricity from isolated coasts to consumers
  • Competition with established technologies
05.4 (4 marks)
  • Barrage: huge quantities of construction materials; reduces tidal range so intertidal mudflats used by wading birds are lost; changes sedimentation (erosion near turbines, deposition elsewhere), lower turbidity; pollutants concentrate in the lagoon; blocks migratory fish and shipping
  • Lagoon: similar but smaller scale; only part of the estuary enclosed so the most sensitive areas and main channel can be avoided
  • In-stream turbines: do not block the tidal flow so little impact on range or sediment; but noise affects cetaceans and many dispersed turbines could have a larger total impact; harness less energy
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 210-211, 214-217; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 610 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 214-215 · REN-06

Figure 1 shows the water level inside and outside a tidal barrage over one 12.5-hour tidal cycle and the periods when electricity is generated.

Figure 1
[2 marks]06.1Use Figure 1 to explain when electricity is generated by the barrage.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why tidal power is intermittent but predictable.
[2 marks]06.3Explain the difference between spring and neap tides and why this affects output.
[2 marks]06.4Explain how a tidal lagoon can be used to generate electricity when there is no tidal flow.
[2 marks]06.5Give two coastal features that make a site suitable for tidal power.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (2 marks)
  • When there is a difference in level between the sea and the basin: on the ebb (about 6-8 hours) water flows out through the turbines
  • Also possible on the flood when the sea is higher than the basin; no generation when levels are equal (slack water)
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Two periods of slack water in each 12.5-hour cycle (twice in about 25 hours) give no flow; the timing does not match demand
  • Tides depend on the positions of the Moon and Sun which are known, so output can be forecast years ahead and alternative supplies planned
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Spring tides: Sun and Moon aligned so their gravitational effects combine giving a large tidal range; neap: effects oppose giving a small range
  • Energy depends on the mass of water and height difference, so output is much greater at springs
06.4 (2 marks)
  • When demand is low, surplus electricity pumps water into the lagoon above sea level
  • The stored water is released through the turbines later when demand is high (pumped storage)
06.5 (2 marks)
  • Large tidal range (eg Severn Estuary)
  • Estuaries, bays or headlands / channels that focus the flow and increase velocity
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 214-215; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 710 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 212-214 · REN-07
[3 marks]07.1Explain three advantages of biofuels over other renewable energy resources.
[3 marks]07.2Explain three disadvantages of biofuel crops.
[2 marks]07.3Describe two new biofuel technologies.
[2 marks]07.4Explain how new low-temperature turbine technology could allow geothermal electricity generation in the UK.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Supply rate of energy crops can be controlled and fuels stored so supply matches demand (not intermittent)
  • Liquid biofuels (alcohol, biodiesel) can replace petrol / diesel in vehicles
  • Carbon neutral in principle: CO2 released equals CO2 absorbed in photosynthesis
  • Energy density of alcohol / biodiesel nearly as high as fossil fuels; wastes (straw, sewage, landfill gas) are cheap
07.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Large areas of farmland compete with food production or cause clearance of wildlife habitats (eg palm oil)
  • Intensive farming inputs (fertiliser, pesticide, machinery fuel) may release nearly as much CO2 as using fossil fuels directly
  • Low energy density of straw, wood, Miscanthus makes transport uneconomic - must be used locally
  • Supply from wastes limited to the amount produced
07.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Hydrogen from algae: some algae deprived of sulfur produce hydrogen during photosynthesis
  • Anaerobic digestion extended to crop wastes and marine algae; small-scale digesters (eg one cow's manure supplying a family's cooking gas)
  • Biofuels from microorganisms (eg oil-producing microalgae)
07.4 (2 marks)
  • Conventional geothermal steam turbines need rocks above 150 °C (recent volcanic activity), too deep to reach economically in the UK
  • New turbines use fluids such as butane / pentane that boil at low temperatures, so water as cool as 60 °C from deep boreholes / hot dry rock systems can drive them
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 212-214; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 810 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 196-217 · REN-08
[9 marks]08.1Evaluate the potential contribution of renewable energy resources to future energy supplies in a country such as the UK.
[1 mark]08.2State the factor by which the kinetic energy of wind increases when wind velocity doubles.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Properties of renewables: intermittency, predictability, low energy density, storage, applicability, locational constraints, impacts, technology level, economics
  • Wind: large offshore resource in shallow seas, falling costs, HAWT / VAWT / direct drive developments; intermittent, needs storage and grid; land-use conflicts
  • Solar: PV costs falling rapidly, roof space, but low winter insolation in the UK; CSP unsuitable; heat pumps and photothermal for heating
  • HEP: most large sites used; low-head, helical and micro-hydro extend use; pumped storage vital
  • Tidal: large range (Severn, Mersey), predictable; barrages costly with high impact, lagoons and in-stream turbines lower impact
  • Wave: long Atlantic fetch, but technology immature
  • Biofuels: wastes, anaerobic digestion, energy crops vs food / habitat
  • Geothermal: limited to low-temperature schemes / heat pumps
  • Systems issues: storage (pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen, P2G, V2G), grid management, diversity of sources, energy conservation reducing demand, electrification of transport
  • Judgement: renewables could supply most electricity with storage and a diverse mix, but heat and transport are harder; transition needs investment and lifestyle change
08.2 (1 marks)
  • Eight times (energy proportional to velocity cubed)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 196-217; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.

Question 915 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 199-203 · REN-09

Table 1 shows the mean daily solar energy received on a horizontal surface at three locations.

Table 1
LocationLatitudeMean solar energy in June / kWh m-2 day-1Mean solar energy in December / kWh m-2 day-1
Sahara desert25°N7.84.2
Southern England51°N5.00.7
Northern Norway69°N4.60.0
[1 mark]09.1Use Table 1 to calculate how many times more solar energy is received in December in the Sahara than in southern England.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why northern Norway receives more solar energy in June than in December.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why concentrating solar power systems using parabolic reflectors only work where there is no cloud.
[1 mark]09.4Explain why the low energy density of solar power is a problem for large-scale generation.
[9 marks]09.5Evaluate the extent to which new technologies could overcome the problems of using solar power as a large-scale energy resource.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (1 marks)
  • 4.2 / 0.7 = 6
09.2 (2 marks)
  • Long summer days (almost 24 hours of daylight) so the total energy received is high, even though the Sun is low
  • In winter days are very short / the Sun does not rise, so light levels are extremely low
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Reflectors can only focus parallel rays of direct sunlight onto the absorber
  • Clouds, smoke and dust scatter the light so it cannot be focused and intensified
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Very large areas of collectors are needed to harness significant amounts of energy (and the changing angle of incidence reduces it further)
09.5 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Problems: intermittency (daily and seasonal cycles), unpredictable cloud cover, low energy density, changing angle of incidence, land requirements, water for cleaning in deserts, toxic wastes in manufacture (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium), efficiency loss at high temperature
  • Photothermal: evacuated tubes reduce conduction / convection losses; thermal stores (water, sand, concrete, molten salt) allow later use; passive solar architecture, brise soleil, light tubes; heat pumps deliver up to four times the energy used
  • Photovoltaic developments: multijunction cells absorbing more wavelengths, anti-reflective / moth-eye textured surfaces (smooth cells reflect 30%), PV-thermal hybrids cooling the cell, transparent PV in windows, heliostats keeping the optimum angle, self-cleaning nanohydrophobic surfaces
  • Concentrating solar power with molten-salt storage to 550 °C so steam turbines run when needed - overcomes intermittency
  • Integration: grid-connected solar farms, rooftop space avoiding land conflicts, batteries, pumped storage, links to energy storage and demand management
  • Evaluation: strong in sunny deserts / low latitudes, weaker in high latitudes in winter; cost falling; manufacturing impacts; no single technology solves all problems; justified conclusion
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 199-203; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
