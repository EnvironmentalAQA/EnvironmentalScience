01.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Photons of light absorbed by the (n-type silicon) upper layer dislodge electrons, making it negatively charged
  • 1Electrons flow through an external conductor / circuit to the relatively positive lower layer; the current powers appliances

1 mark for each point.

01.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.

  • 1Unpredictable: cloud cover, dust and smoke reduce intensity and scatter light and cannot be forecast accurately

Question 210 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 200-202 · REN-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain how a ground-source heat pump can deliver more heat energy to a building than the electrical energy used to run it.
[3 marks]02.2Explain how Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) with thermal storage overcomes the intermittency of solar power.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why CSP can only be used in areas with little cloud, whereas PV can be used anywhere.
[2 marks]02.4Describe two environmental impacts of solar PV.
Show mark scheme
02.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A fluid in a buried ground loop absorbs low-temperature heat from the ground; at the expansion valve the fluid boils at low pressure absorbing this heat
  • 1A compressor raises the pressure so the gas condenses, releasing heat at a higher temperature inside the building
  • 1The electricity only moves heat rather than creating it, so up to four times as much heat is delivered as electricity used (less if the source is colder)
02.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Parabolic reflectors focus sunlight onto tubes of oil, increasing the energy density and reaching high temperatures
  • 1The heat is transferred to molten salt (potassium nitrate) stored in large insulated tanks at up to 550 °C
  • 1The stored heat boils water to drive steam turbines whenever electricity is required, including at night
02.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Reflectors can only focus parallel rays of direct sunlight; cloud, dust and smoke scatter light so it cannot be concentrated
  • 1PV cells convert diffuse as well as direct light, though output is lower
02.4 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Manufacture: extraction / processing of silicon, metals and plastics; toxic wastes (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium)
  • 1Land take by large solar farms competing with other uses / affecting desert flora and fauna
  • 1Water for cleaning panels in arid areas
  • 1Disposal of old panels

Question 510 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 210-211, 214-217 · REN-05
[2 marks]05.1Explain the locational requirements for harnessing wave power.
[2 marks]05.2Describe how one named wave power device generates electricity.
[2 marks]05.3Give two design problems that have delayed the development of wave power.
[4 marks]05.4Compare the environmental impacts of tidal barrages, tidal lagoons and in-stream tidal turbines.
Show mark scheme
05.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1High mean wind velocities that are consistent in strength and direction
  • 1A long fetch (distance of open water) over which waves build up, eg Atlantic coasts of the UK
05.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Oscillating water column (eg Islay Limpet): waves force water up and down in a submerged chamber; the air forced in and out drives a turbine
  • 1Or point absorber (floating buoy moving relative to a fixed base turns a generator); surface attenuator (Pelamis - hinged sections push pistons pumping fluid over a turbine); overtopping device (Wave Dragon - waves fill a reservoir above sea level which drains through turbines); oscillating wave surge converter (Oyster)
05.3 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Equipment must withstand storms and corrosion
  • 1Difficult to anchor off stormy coasts / in deep water
  • 1Expensive to transmit electricity from isolated coasts to consumers
  • 1Competition with established technologies
05.4 4 marks
Up to 4 marks in total; award marks in proportion to the completeness of each point.
  • up to 2Barrage: huge quantities of construction materials; reduces tidal range so intertidal mudflats used by wading birds are lost; changes sedimentation (erosion near turbines, deposition elsewhere), lower turbidity; pollutants concentrate in the lagoon; blocks migratory fish and shipping
  • up to 2Lagoon: similar but smaller scale; only part of the estuary enclosed so the most sensitive areas and main channel can be avoided
  • up to 2In-stream turbines: do not block the tidal flow so little impact on range or sediment; but noise affects cetaceans and many dispersed turbines could have a larger total impact; harness less energy

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

What renewables can contribute depends on their properties - intermittency, predictability, energy density, storage, applicability, location, impacts, technological maturity and cost - set against the UK's resources. Wind is the largest: shallow seas give a huge offshore resource, costs have fallen and direct-drive and vertical-axis designs keep improving, but it is intermittent, needs storage and grid connections and provokes land-use conflicts onshore. Solar PV costs are falling rapidly and roof space is plentiful, but winter insolation is low and concentrating solar is unsuitable; photothermal panels and heat pumps are better for heating. Most large HEP sites are used, though low-head, helical and micro-hydro extend it and pumped storage is vital for balancing. The Severn and Mersey have large, predictable tidal ranges, but barrages are costly and damaging while lagoons and in-stream turbines have lower impact; the long Atlantic fetch gives wave energy that the technology cannot yet harness reliably. Biofuels from wastes and anaerobic digestion are useful, but energy crops compete with food and habitat, and geothermal is limited to low-temperature schemes.

The system matters as much as the sources: storage in pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen and power-to-gas, vehicle-to-grid, grid management, a diverse mix that smooths supply, and conservation to reduce demand. With these, renewables could supply most of the UK's electricity. Heat and transport are harder, because they need high temperatures or liquid fuels, so electrification and hydrogen would be required. The potential is large, but realising it needs sustained investment and changes in how energy is used.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

The UK could use lots of renewable energy. It is windy so wind turbines could be built, especially offshore. Solar panels can be put on roofs but it is not very sunny in winter. Tidal power could be used in the Severn estuary because it has big tides. Hydroelectric power can be used in Scotland. Renewables are intermittent so batteries would be needed. Renewables could provide a lot of the UK's energy in the future.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 mentions the main resources with one fact each; Level 3 assesses each against its properties, constraints and the state of the technology.
  • Level 3 treats storage, grid and demand as a system and distinguishes electricity from heat and transport - essential for judging 'future energy supplies'.
  • The conclusion in Level 3 is qualified (most electricity; heat and transport harder; needs investment); Level 1's is unqualified.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Solar power's problems are intrinsic: it is intermittent with daily and seasonal cycles, cloud makes it unpredictable and scatters light so it cannot be focused, its low energy density demands large collector areas, the angle of incidence keeps changing, cleaning in deserts uses scarce water, manufacturing PV produces toxic wastes such as silicon tetrachloride and cadmium, and PV efficiency falls as cells heat up.

New technology addresses most of these. Evacuated-tube photothermal panels cut conduction and convection losses, and thermal stores of water, sand, concrete or molten salt hold heat for later; passive solar design, brise soleil and light tubes reduce demand; heat pumps deliver up to four times the energy used to run them. Multijunction PV cells absorb more wavelengths, moth-eye anti-reflective surfaces stop the 30% of light a smooth cell reflects, PV-thermal hybrids cool the cell and use the heat, transparent PV turns windows into generators, heliostats keep panels at the optimum angle and nanohydrophobic surfaces self-clean. Concentrating solar power with molten salt stored at 550 °C runs steam turbines when electricity is wanted, directly overcoming intermittency, and grid-connected farms, rooftops that avoid land conflicts, batteries and pumped storage integrate the output.

Even so, no technology changes the fact that winter insolation at high latitudes is tiny, that CSP needs cloudless skies, or that manufacturing has impacts. Solar can dominate supply in sunny, low-latitude regions and contribute strongly elsewhere in summer, but overcoming its problems fully depends on storage and a diverse energy mix rather than on solar technology alone.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (2 marks)

Solar power can be stored in batteries for use at night. Mirrors can concentrate the sunlight to make it hotter. So new technologies can overcome most of the problems with solar power.
