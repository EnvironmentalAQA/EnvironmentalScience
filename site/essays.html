Discuss the extent to which the study of ozone depletion illustrates how scientific investigation can lead to the successful solution of an environmental problem.spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 108-113 · ESS-P1-OZONE
Indicative content
  • Stages of investigation: identifying the issue (Halley Station UV data, Dobson unit measurements), hypothesis formation (Rowland-Molina 1974: persistence, UV dissociation, chlorine reactions), data collection (ground, satellite, balloon and aircraft air sampling detecting ClO), analysis of spatial, temporal and altitude variability, proposal of solutions, enactment
  • Why Antarctica: low stratospheric temperatures, ice crystals, stratospheric clouds, polar vortex, spring sunlight
  • Effects of UVB: skin cancer, cataracts, DNA damage, plant and marine damage - motivating action
  • Solutions: Vienna Convention, Montreal Protocol - phase-out and bans, alternative processes (pump sprays), alternative materials (HCFCs, HFCs, hydrocarbons, HFAs), collection and incineration of CFCs, fund for developing countries
  • Evaluation of success: ozone hole area / depth stabilising, CFC levels falling; limitations - persistence, remaining ODSs, HFCs as greenhouse gases, decades to full recovery
  • Comparison with other atmospheric problems (climate change, acid rain) - why ozone was easier; role of peer review, international cooperation, availability of substitutes
  • Conclusion on the extent to which it is a model for other issues
Discuss the difficulties in monitoring and predicting global climate change and evaluate the methods used to overcome them.spec 3.2.1.2, 3.1.1.3 · Genn pp. 89-90, 100-104, 10 · ESS-P1-CLIMATE
Indicative content
  • Nature of the problem: interconnected atmosphere-hydrosphere-biosphere systems; changes over different time and spatial scales; natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) masking or exaggerating anthropogenic change; time delay between cause and effect (ocean heat capacity)
  • Limitations of historic data: sparse global coverage, unsophisticated equipment, heat island bias, no atmospheric composition records
  • Proxy data and their reliability: dendrochronology, coral growth rings, pollen in dated sediments, ice cores (gas bubbles, isotope ratios, volcanic ash layers, compaction problems)
  • Modern monitoring: long-term data sets (Mauna Loa), electronic equipment, satellites (LEO vs geostationary), Argo floats, remote sensing of ice thickness / area / sea level
  • Feedback mechanisms and tipping points adding uncertainty; ecological uncertainty (species survival, distribution, fragmentation)
  • Computer models: how they are tested and refined; importance of accurate representative data; scenario-based predictions and ranges
  • Uncertainty over ENSO / Conveyor / jet stream responses
  • Evaluation and conclusion: confidence in the overall trend and human cause despite detailed uncertainty; role of peer review and international bodies (IPCC)

Indicative content
  • Negative feedback: increased low-level cloud (albedo), increased photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle evaporation-precipitation

  • Tipping points: self-sustaining processes; why control must precede them; examples
  • Implications: urgency and precautionary principle; need for deep cuts in greenhouse gases (energy, transport, buildings, agriculture, forestry, CCS) rather than only adaptation; geoengineering risks
  • Difficulty of prediction because feedbacks interact at different scales; models; international agreements and per capita footprints

Discuss how knowledge of the properties of pollutants can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.spec 3.4.1-3.4.3 · Genn pp. 236-245, 262-266, 275-285, 295-304 · ESS-P1-CONTROL
Indicative content

  • Direct exploitation: food (fisheries, bushmeat), fashion, entertainment / pets, furniture and ornaments, traditional medicines, other products (whale oil); deliberate eradication of predators, competitors, vectors, pests

  • Why monitor: species present, population size, density, distribution, survival, age structure, breeding rates, movements; baseline and evaluation; Red List categorisation and quotas rely on it
  • Standard techniques and their limitations: quadrats, transects, nets, traps, Lincoln index, Simpson's index, DAFOR; sampling design (random / systematic, size, number, timing, standardisation)
