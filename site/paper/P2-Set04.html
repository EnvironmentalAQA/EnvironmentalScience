Question 310 marks · Energy & material cycles: natural vs human systems · spec 3.6.3 · Genn pp. 373-374 · SUS-04
[4 marks]03.1Explain how natural material cycles differ from linear human systems of material use.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why the use of toxic metals in electronic products is a particular problem for sustainability.
[4 marks]03.3Describe how the design of a product can move it from a linear to a cyclical system.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Natural cycles use a small number of abundant elements (C, H, O, N, P) built into monomers and polymers (carbohydrates, proteins); wastes of one process are raw materials for another so nothing is depleted or accumulates
  • Natural wastes are biodegradable / non-toxic and are broken down by decomposers into raw materials
  • Human systems are linear: extract - process - manufacture - use - discard; minerals are dispersed after use or mixed so recovery is difficult
  • Human wastes are often persistent and toxic (organochlorines, PCBs, heavy metals in electronics) and build up; fossil fuel use is inherently linear and unsustainable
/ 4
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Metals are dispersed in small amounts within complex mixed products so they cannot easily be separated / recovered at end of life
  • They leach from landfill or are released by incineration; persistent, bioaccumulating
/ 2
03.3 (4 marks)
  • Design for durability, repair and re-use (replaceable parts, no built-in batteries, spare parts and support, no planned obsolescence)
  • Easy identification of materials (coding) and separation of components so they can be recycled
  • Separation of technical materials (metals, plastics) from biological materials (which can be composted / digested)
  • Use of recycled and recyclable / biodegradable materials; reduced packaging and material use (Cradle to Cradle)
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 373-374; AQA spec 3.6.3.
Question 410 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 308-310, 313 · AGR-02
[3 marks]04.1Explain why a moderate water shortage reduces crop productivity even though the plants survive.
[4 marks]04.2Explain the problems caused by waterlogged soils and describe two ways farmers reduce soil water levels.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how irrigating with groundwater can cause soil salinisation and how this harms crops.
[1 mark]04.4State how a farmer could raise the pH of an acidic soil and explain why a low pH reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Plants close their stomata to reduce transpiration losses
  • Closed stomata stop the absorption of carbon dioxide so photosynthesis and growth stop
  • Less water for nutrient absorption / transport and cell turgidity; severe shortage dehydrates cells and inhibits biochemical reactions
/ 3
04.2 (4 marks)
  • Anaerobic soil favours denitrifying bacteria and inhibits nitrifying bacteria so nitrate is lost faster than it forms; higher risk of fungal disease; root respiration inhibited
  • Drainage ditches or pipes
  • Deep ploughing; avoiding compaction by machinery / livestock; adding organic matter to encourage worms
/ 4
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Groundwater often contains dissolved salts; when the water evaporates from the soil the salts are left behind and accumulate
  • High soil salinity causes osmotic dehydration of roots / crop death; washing out with extra water uses more water and salinises rivers
/ 2
04.4 (1 marks)
  • Add crushed lime (calcium carbonate / hydroxide); low pH increases leaching, inhibits nutrient uptake and mobilises toxic aluminium and lead
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 308-310, 313; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.
Question 55 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.1 · Genn pp. 5-7 · LIFE-04
[1 mark]05.1Which feature of the Earth prevents most atmospheric gases from escaping into space? Tick one box.
  • A  Its mass
  • B  Its magnetic field
  • C  Its distance from the Sun
  • D  Its speed of rotation
[2 marks]05.2Explain how the speed of rotation of the Earth and the tilt of its axis affect the conditions for life.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why the presence of liquid water on Earth depends on both the distance from the Sun and atmospheric pressure.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • Its mass
/ 1
05.2 (2 marks)
  • The 24-hour rotation period means the surface is not exposed to sunlight for long, so temperature extremes / excessive heating and cooling are reduced
  • The axis is tilted relative to the orbit so the amount of insolation at each latitude changes through the year, producing seasonal variations in conditions
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • The distance from the Sun gives surface temperatures between 0 and 35 °C in most areas: too close and water would boil / evaporate, too far and it would freeze
  • Atmospheric pressure (produced by the mass of gases held by gravity) is high enough to stop the water boiling / evaporating away; at much lower pressure there would be no liquid water
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 5-7; AQA spec 3.1.1.1.

07.1 (2 marks)
  • Ecological footprint: the area of the Earth's surface (global hectares) needed to provide the resources used by a person / population / activity and to absorb the wastes produced
  • Biocapacity: the biological productivity of an area (forests, cropland, grazing, fisheries, built land) - its ability to supply resources and absorb wastes, expressed in global hectares
/ 2
07.2 (2 marks)
  • A hectare of land / sea with the world-average biological productivity
  • Biocapacities of real areas vary widely so a standard unit allows footprints and biocapacities of different places to be compared
/ 2

  • 8.1 / 1.7 = 4.8 (accept 4.7-4.8)
/ 1
07.4 (3 marks)
  • Higher HDI is associated with a larger footprint (USA 8.1 vs Bangladesh 0.8), though the UK achieves the same HDI as the USA with about half the footprint
  • Global footprint (2.8) exceeds global biocapacity (1.7) so humanity uses resources faster than they regenerate and CO2 accumulates (overshoot)
  • Earth Overshoot Day is the date each year when a year's worth of resources / waste absorption has been used up; it falls earlier each year (8 August in 2016)
/ 3
07.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Renewable energy and energy conservation (carbon footprint is the largest component)
  • Reduced meat consumption / food waste; local seasonal food
  • Circular economy: repair, re-use, recycling; longer product lives

Question 1015 marks · Impacts of agriculture, social/political factors & sustainable agriculture · spec 3.5.1.5 · Genn pp. 335-337 · AGI-07
[2 marks]10.1Explain why the potential to increase food supplies by cultivating new land is limited.
[2 marks]10.2Explain why importing food may reduce the availability of food in the area where it is produced.
[2 marks]10.3Explain why organic agriculture is more than simply 'not using pesticides and artificial fertilisers'.
[9 marks]10.4Evaluate the strategies that could be used to make agriculture more sustainable.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (2 marks)
  • About 10% of land is already cultivated and 25% grazed
  • Most remaining land is too dry, wet, cold, hot or steep, is covered in ice, or has no soil
/ 2
10.2 (2 marks)
  • Land becomes too expensive for local people to rent / is used to grow export crops for more affluent societies
  • So people with less money lose access to the land and food they used to produce
/ 2
10.3 (2 marks)
  • It uses natural processes to supply nutrients (legumes, recycled organic matter, soil biota), control pests and provide ecological services
  • So that pesticides and artificial fertilisers become unnecessary rather than just being banned
/ 2
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Unsustainable features: reliance on chemical pesticides (resistance, non-target impacts, bans), non-renewable rock phosphate, fossil-fuel-intensive nitrate manufacture, over-exploitation of rivers / groundwater, salinisation, loss of soil organic matter, greenhouse gases from fuel, rice and livestock, loss of species providing ecosystem services
  • Pest control: cultural methods (weeding, mulching, crop rotation, barrier crops, culling), biological control, predator habitats, polyculture / companion crops, integrated control, reduced antibiotic use
  • Nutrients: recycling organic matter, crop rotation, cultivation of legumes, conservation of soil biota, reduced artificial fertiliser especially nitrate, low tillage
  • Water: low water-use crops, reservoirs and aquifer recharge, drip irrigation rather than sprays
  • Soil and carbon: low-tillage farming to maintain organic matter, reduced machinery, renewable energy, drought-tolerant rice varieties so fields are drained earlier, high-carbohydrate ground feed for cattle reduces methane
  • Biodiversity: conserve habitats of crop wild relatives, seed banks, retain hedgerows, ditches, ponds, woodland; agri-environment schemes
  • Evaluation: growing population (perhaps 12 billion) needs more food; lower trophic level diets; obstacles - cost, need for support, gaining acceptance of new techniques; learning from past land degradation (fertile crescent, US mid-west)
  • Judgement: no single strategy sufficient; combination of ecological principles and new technology; justified conclusion
/ 9

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Explain how the physical features of the Earth and the activities of living organisms have combined to create and maintain conditions suitable for life.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Physical features: mass / gravity retaining an atmosphere and giving pressure for liquid water; distance from the Sun and the Goldilocks zone; speed of rotation reducing temperature extremes; tilt producing seasons; magnetic field deflecting the solar wind; the Moon stabilising the orbit and producing tides
  • Importance of liquid water: solvent, transport, temperature control, anomalous expansion, high specific heat capacity, habitats, absorption of UV before the ozone layer
  • Development of life: Archaea at vents; photosynthesis; oxygen reacting with iron then accumulating; ozone layer allowing colonisation of land; aerobic organisms
  • Life changing conditions: carbon sequestration offsetting solar brightening, biogeochemical cycles, transpiration and rainfall, regulation of atmospheric gases
  • Methods of studying past conditions: proxy data, isotope ratios, ice cores, Mg:Ca ratios; understanding of continental drift and ocean currents
  • Links to current threats (climate change, ozone depletion) and a justified conclusion about the interdependence of physical and biological factors
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
