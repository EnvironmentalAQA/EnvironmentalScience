Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Fishing's impacts go beyond depleting target species: k-selected species that breed late and produce few young collapse and recover slowly; by-catch of immature target fish, over-quota fish and non-commercial species is thrown back dead, with drift nets killing whales, dolphins and turtles, longlines drowning albatrosses and pair trawls porpoises; lost gear keeps ghost fishing; demersal trawls destroy sea fans, corals and seagrass; and removing any species alters its competitors, prey and predators.

Catch quotas limit the weight landed and work well for single-species pelagic shoals where fishing stops when the quota is reached, but in mixed demersal fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead, so the quota does not protect them. Gear design tackles by-catch directly: mesh sizes and square-mesh escape panels let small fish out because, unlike diagonal mesh, they do not close under drag; turtle exclusion devices, acoustic pingers for dolphins, curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting protect turtles, cetaceans and albatrosses; biodegradable and radio-tracked traps limit ghost fishing. Restricting effort - boat size, engine power, days at sea, decommissioning - reduces total pressure, and banning drift nets or demersal trawling on sensitive seabeds such as Rockall Bank protects habitats.

Effectiveness depends on enforcement at sea, which is difficult, on reliable stock data for quotas, on the species and fishery type, and on international cooperation because fish migrate; some measures cut catches or raise costs, and as local stocks fall fleets simply move further away. Gear modifications are the most effective per unit cost because they reduce damage without reducing legal catch; quotas and effort limits only work where they are enforced.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Fishing damages the environment by overfishing, catching by-catch such as dolphins and damaging the seabed with trawling. Quotas limit how many fish can be caught so stocks can recover. Bigger mesh sizes let small fish escape so they can grow and breed. Turtle exclusion devices let turtles escape from nets. Some fishing methods can be banned in sensitive areas. No-take zones let fish breed. These methods help but they are hard to enforce at sea and some fishermen ignore them.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 covers impacts and methods soundly but without the mechanisms (why square mesh works, why quotas fail in mixed fisheries).
  • Level 3 evaluates each method on its own terms and then compares them; Level 2's evaluation is a single general sentence.
  • Named examples (Rockall Bank, albatross measures, ghost-fishing controls) give Level 3 the range the top band expects.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Other species supply resources we use directly: wood, fibres such as cotton and wool, oils, fuels and food. Because most farmed species were domesticated long ago and are often kept where they are poorly adapted, wild species such as the drought-resistant morama bean, the yeheb tree that grows on poor soils, perennial maize that needs no ploughing, and bison that graze grassland too poor for cattle offer new, better-adapted food sources. Wild species also carry genetic resources: crop wild relatives in Vavilov centres hold genes for disease resistance and salt tolerance that inbred commercial varieties lack.

Biomimetics copies adaptations into technology - humpback flipper tubercles in turbine blades, shark skin in friction-reducing coatings, termite-mound convection in naturally ventilated buildings, hollow bird bones in lightweight roofs. Medicines such as taxol, quinine, AZT and diosgenin came from wild species, and physiological research on marsupials, hippo skin and sponge proteins is helping with foetal development, burns and transplant rejection. Since only a tiny fraction of species has been studied, the unknown future value argues for conserving whole habitats.

Beyond resources, other species provide services we could not replace: regulation of atmospheric gases, evapotranspiration driving rainfall, the carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles run by microbes, soil formation by decomposers, and pollination, seed dispersal and habitat provision. These are unnoticed precisely because they are in dynamic equilibrium, so their economic value is underestimated. On balance the resource and service arguments alone justify conservation, but they are strongest when combined with the intrinsic and aesthetic case, because many species with no known use still sustain the systems we depend on.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

Biodiversity is important because we get lots of things from other species. We get wood, cotton and food from plants and animals. Some medicines come from plants, like aspirin from willow trees. Animals can help design things, like Velcro was copied from seeds. Other species also do things for us like bees pollinating crops. If we lose species we might lose things that could be useful in the future. So we should conserve biodiversity.

What makes the difference
  • Both answers cover resources, medicines, biomimetics and services, but Level 3 develops each with specific, accurate detail (which species, what property, why it matters).
  • The question asks 'to what extent'. Level 3 weighs resource arguments against intrinsic value and reaches a reasoned conclusion; Level 1 asserts 'so we should conserve'.
  • Level 3 explains why services are undervalued (dynamic equilibrium) - an explanation, not just a list.
