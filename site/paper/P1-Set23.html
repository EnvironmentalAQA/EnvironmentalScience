Question 110 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 99-100, 105-108 · CC-09

A low-lying coastal town in eastern England is at risk of flooding from rising sea level and more intense rainfall.

[3 marks]01.1Describe three ways the town could adapt to the increased flood risk.
[2 marks]01.2Explain the circumstances in which managed retreat may be chosen instead of coastal defences.
[2 marks]01.3Describe two impacts of higher temperatures on transport infrastructure.
[3 marks]01.4Explain how carbon capture and storage (CCS) could reduce CO2 emissions from a fossil fuel power station, and give one limitation.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Raise river banks / build sea walls, flood barriers or wave screens
  • Permeable urban surfaces (gravel, soil) instead of concrete / tarmac to slow runoff
  • River flow management: upstream dams / low soil dams / afforestation / maintained flood plains to delay runoff
  • Raised buildings on stilts or floating houses
  • Pumping rainwater from land below river level
  • Managed retreat - abandoning land where protection costs exceed value (eg Happisburgh)
/ 3
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Where the cost of flood prevention is greater than the value of what is protected, or defences would be ineffective
  • Land is abandoned as water levels rise / coast erodes; defences used only where land is very important
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Road surfaces deform as tar melts - need tarmac with a higher melting point
  • Rail track expands and buckles above the temperature it was pre-stretched for (27 °C in UK) - relay with higher pre-stress
  • Bridge damage from high river flows / debris; landslides on deforested slopes
/ 2
01.4 (3 marks)
  • CO2 is captured from the flue gases (or carbon removed from the fuel before combustion)
  • Transported by pipeline / ship / tanker and stored in depleted oil or gas fields, aquifers, or used for secondary oil recovery
  • Limitation: developmental / expensive, uses energy (reducing efficiency), risk of leakage, limited storage sites
/ 3

Question 210 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.3-3.4.3.2.4 · Genn pp. 248-249, 253-254 · AIR-05

Figure 1 shows the concentrations of three pollutants in the air of a large city in a valley during one sunny day.

Figure 1
[4 marks]02.1Use Figure 1 to describe how the ozone concentration changes during the day and explain the pattern.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why the early-morning peak in NO is followed by a peak in NO2.
[2 marks]02.3Name one other toxic secondary pollutant produced in photochemical smogs and give one of its effects on humans.
[2 marks]02.4Explain why cities such as Los Angeles and Mexico City are particularly prone to photochemical smogs.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
  • Ozone is very low overnight / early morning, rises from about 08:00, peaks at about 80 units at 14:00 and falls in the evening
  • Ozone is a secondary pollutant formed by photochemical reactions requiring sunlight (UV), so it peaks in the afternoon after several hours of sunlight
  • NO2 (from morning traffic) is broken down by UV to NO and monatomic oxygen; monatomic oxygen reacts with O2 to form ozone
  • Ozone lags behind the NO / NO2 peaks (which are produced by the morning rush-hour traffic) because the reactions take time; falls at night when there is no sunlight
/ 4
02.2 (2 marks)
  • NO is the primary pollutant produced in engines when nitrogen and oxygen react at high temperature / pressure
  • NO is oxidised in the atmosphere (by oxygen / ozone) to NO2, a secondary pollutant
/ 2
02.3 (2 marks)
  • PANs (peroxyacetyl nitrates)
  • Eye irritation / breathing difficulty / asthma / emphysema / increased risk of heart attacks (toxic at much lower concentrations than NOx)
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Heavy vehicle use giving high NOx and hydrocarbon emissions
  • Sunny climate providing the UV for photochemical reactions
  • Valley / basin topography and frequent temperature inversions trapping pollutants
/ 2

Question 310 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.2 · Genn pp. 232-233 · ECON-06
[2 marks]03.1Explain how occupancy sensors and programmable thermostats reduce energy waste.

03.1 (2 marks)

  • Programmable thermostats set different temperatures for different rooms and times so unused rooms / periods are not heated

  • Heating as needed (or just before) avoids these standing losses; if storage is needed the tank should be well insulated

Question 410 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 110-111 · OZ-02

Figure 1 shows the minimum ozone concentration recorded over Antarctica each spring between 1979 and 2015.

Figure 1
[2 marks]04.1Describe the trend shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]04.2Calculate the percentage decrease in minimum ozone between 1979 and 1995.
[4 marks]04.3Explain why ozone depletion is most severe over Antarctica in spring.
[2 marks]04.4Explain why the data in Figure 1 alone could not confirm that CFCs were the cause of the depletion, and describe how the cause was confirmed.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • Minimum ozone fell steeply from about 225 DU in 1979 to about 95 DU in the mid 1990s
  • Since then it has fluctuated around 100-115 DU with a slight recovery / no further decline
/ 2
04.2 (2 marks)
  • (225 - 95) / 225 x 100
  • = 57.8% (accept 58%)
/ 2
04.3 (4 marks)
  • Stratospheric temperatures over Antarctica are the lowest on Earth so stratospheric clouds / ice crystals form
  • Ice crystal surfaces allow reactions that convert the chlorine released from CFCs into chlorine molecules (Cl2) during the dark winter
  • The polar vortex (rotating winds) prevents mixing with the rest of the atmosphere, keeping temperatures low and chlorine concentrated
  • When sunlight returns in spring (September) it splits the chlorine molecules into free radicals that destroy ozone; levels recover by December as the vortex breaks down
/ 4
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Ground and satellite UV / ozone measurements show depletion but not the chemistry / correlation is not causation
  • Air samples collected from the stratosphere by helium balloons and high-flying research aircraft detected chlorine and chlorine monoxide, confirming the Rowland-Molina hypothesis
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 110-111; AQA spec 3.2.1.3.

Question 610 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 267-268 · NUT-02
[3 marks]06.1Explain why nitrates in drinking water can cause blue baby syndrome (methaemoglobinaemia).
[2 marks]06.2Explain why the link between nitrates in drinking water and stomach cancer is uncertain.
[3 marks]06.3Describe three ways farmers in a Nitrate Vulnerable Zone can reduce nitrate leaching into aquifers.
[2 marks]06.4Explain how phosphates are removed from sewage effluent and from lakes polluted in the past.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Bacteria in the gut convert nitrates to nitrites
  • Nitrites react with haemoglobin so it cannot carry oxygen
  • Infants are most at risk because they have more of the gut bacteria and their haemoglobin reacts faster; usually from shallow wells receiving arable drainage
/ 3
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Nitrites may be converted in the gut to nitrosamines which are carcinogenic
  • But most research has been on rats / the evidence in humans is not conclusive; nitrites are also ingested in processed food so the source cannot be isolated
/ 2
06.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Reduce use of nitrate fertilisers / match application to crop need
  • Avoid ploughing / applying fertiliser at times of heavy rain (autumn / winter)
  • Use low-solubility / slow-release fertilisers rather than soluble rapid-release ones
  • Store manure / slurry where it cannot leach or wash into rivers
  • Grow crops with lower nitrogen requirements (eg triticale instead of wheat)
  • Grow legumes with nitrogen-fixing bacteria instead of relying on artificial fertiliser
/ 3
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Iron(III) sulfate is added to effluent: phosphates are precipitated as insoluble iron phosphate (a fine sediment that can be used as fertiliser)
  • Lakes: dredging phosphate-rich sediments / adding iron(III) sulfate to reduce phosphate solubility (eg the Norfolk Broads)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 267-268; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 710 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 397-399 · RM-07
[6 marks]07.1Complete Table 1 to show a suitable sampling technique for each group of organisms and one limitation of the technique.
Table 1
OrganismsTechniqueOne limitation
Invertebrates active on the ground surface at night
Invertebrates living in leaf litter
Invertebrates on tree branches
[2 marks]07.2Explain why a light trap cannot be used to prove that a moth species is absent from a site.
[2 marks]07.3Explain how a motor-driven suction sampler can give a population density, whereas a pooter cannot.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Ground surface: pitfall trap (container sunk level with the surface, cover, checked after 24 h) - only mobile surface animals; trapped predators eat others; preservative attracts / repels; escapes
  • Leaf litter: Tullgren funnel (sample on mesh under a light; heat / light drive invertebrates down into a container for a standard time) - only species repelled by heat / light; some die or stop moving; large ones cannot pass the mesh
  • Branches: beating tray (branch beaten a set number of times over a sheet) - hard to standardise vigour; some species not dislodged / fly away; high branches unreachable
/ 6
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Moths only fly as adults for part of the year and activity depends on weather / season, so a species may be present but not caught
  • Brightness / wavelength of the lamp affects attractiveness so catches vary between traps
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • The area of ground covered by the suction pipe is known so invertebrates per m2 can be calculated
  • A pooter is only a handling / collection device for individuals already found, not a sampling method
/ 2

Table 1

Question 1015 marks · The atmosphere: energy processes (UV, IR, visible light) · spec 3.2.1.1, 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 86-89, 108-113 · ATM-06

Table 1 shows the three forms of oxygen found in the stratosphere.

Table 1
Form of oxygenNumber of atoms per molecule
Monatomic oxygen (O)1
Diatomic oxygen (O2)2
Triatomic oxygen / ozone (O3)3
[3 marks]10.1Explain how the three forms of oxygen protect life at the surface from ultraviolet radiation.
[2 marks]10.2Give two other ways in which the atmosphere supports life.
[1 mark]10.3Explain why the atmosphere is thermally stratified into layers.
[9 marks]10.4The troposphere and stratosphere are the layers most affected by human activities. Discuss how human activities alter the energy processes in these two layers and evaluate the difficulties of predicting the consequences.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (3 marks)
  • Together they form a dispersed layer in the stratosphere (the ozone layer / ozonosphere)
  • They absorb ultraviolet light, which drives a dynamic equilibrium of reactions that form and destroy ozone
  • The UV energy is converted to chemical / heat energy so most UV that passed the upper atmosphere never reaches the surface
/ 3
10.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Provides carbon, oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen (as N2, O2, CO2, H2O) for biological molecules
  • Delays the escape of infrared energy, keeping the surface warm
  • Distributes heat and water vapour by winds / ocean currents
  • Provides atmospheric pressure so liquid water can exist
  • Deflects damaging radiation of the solar wind in the upper atmosphere
/ 2
10.3 (1 marks)
  • Energy is absorbed at different altitudes (eg UV absorbed in the stratosphere, surface heating warms the troposphere) so temperature gradients change with altitude, producing layers
/ 1
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Troposphere: enhanced greenhouse effect - anthropogenic CO2 (fossil fuels, deforestation), methane (rice, livestock, landfill), N2O, CFCs, tropospheric ozone; more infrared absorbed and re-emitted; aerosols and smoke increase albedo / scatter light; land-use change alters surface albedo and evapotranspiration
  • Stratosphere: CFCs / halons / HCFCs release chlorine and bromine which catalyse ozone destruction, shifting the O / O2 / O3 equilibrium; less UV absorbed in the stratosphere, more reaches the surface; stratospheric cooling
  • Consequences: changes to climate, ocean currents (thermohaline), hydrological cycle, sea level, species distributions; increased UV damage to organisms
  • Difficulties of prediction: linked processes where one change alters others; feedback (positive and negative) and tipping points; time lags (ocean heat capacity, long residence times of CFCs and CO2); natural variability (solar output, volcanoes, Milankovitch cycles); incomplete understanding of atmospheric processes; limited historical data so proxies needed; models with different assumptions
  • Evaluation: some effects well understood (greenhouse physics, ozone chemistry proven, Montreal Protocol success) but scale and timing of regional impacts uncertain; precautionary principle
  • Justified conclusion
/ 9
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 86-89, 108-113; AQA spec 3.2.1.1, 3.2.1.3.
