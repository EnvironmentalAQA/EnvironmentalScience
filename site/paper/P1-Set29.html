Question 110 marks · Heavy metals: lead, mercury · spec 3.4.3.2.11 · Genn pp. 276-277 · HM-02

In the 1950s a chemical factory at Minamata, Japan, discharged inorganic mercury compounds into a shallow bay. Thousands of local people who ate fish and shellfish from the bay suffered severe neurological damage and many babies were born with birth abnormalities.

[4 marks]01.1Explain how the low-toxicity inorganic mercury discharged into the bay came to cause such severe poisoning.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why methyl mercury caused both neurological damage in adults and birth abnormalities.
[3 marks]01.3Compare the toxicity of the three chemical forms of mercury.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Inorganic mercury settled into anaerobic sediments where anaerobic microbes converted it to organic (methyl) mercury
  • Methyl mercury is liposoluble so it is absorbed easily and stored in fat / passes through cell membranes
  • It bioaccumulated in fish and shellfish and biomagnified along the food chain to reach much higher concentrations than in the water
  • Local people ate large amounts of fish (critical group) so received toxic doses
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Liposoluble so it crosses the blood-brain barrier into the brain, inhibiting enzymes / nerve function (neurotoxin)
  • It crosses the placenta and harms the developing unborn baby
  • Mercury is a teratogen, preventing normal gene expression / development
/ 3
01.3 (3 marks)
  • Elemental (liquid) mercury: not easily absorbed through skin or gut, though vapour is absorbed in the lungs
  • Inorganic compounds (eg mercury oxide): moderately well absorbed in the gut
  • Organic compounds (eg methyl mercury): easily absorbed through skin, gut and lungs; liposoluble so most toxic and bioaccumulates
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 276-277; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.11.
Question 210 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 112-113 · OZ-03
[3 marks]02.1Describe the main features of the Montreal Protocol (1987).
[4 marks]02.2For each of the following CFC uses, give the alternative process or material now used and one disadvantage of the alternative.
[2 marks]02.3Describe how waste CFCs from old refrigerators are disposed of safely.
[1 mark]02.4State one reason why the ozone layer is not expected to recover fully for many decades.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Manufacture and use of CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances (ODSs) phased out then banned
  • HCFCs to be phased out (by 2030); some essential uses (eg halon fire extinguishers in aircraft) still permitted
  • A fund to help (developing) countries implement the Protocol; legally binding targets (unlike the 1985 Vienna Convention)
/ 3
02.2 (4 marks)
  • Aerosols: pump-action sprays / roll-on deodorants (alternative process), or hydrocarbon propellants (propane, butane) - flammable / fire risk
  • Refrigerants: HCFCs (less stable so break down in the troposphere, but still some ozone depletion) then HFCs (no chlorine) - more expensive, less effective, powerful greenhouse gases
/ 4
02.3 (2 marks)
  • CFCs are drained from the appliance and incinerated / broken down at high temperature
  • Products are CO2 and acidic gases (HCl, HF) which are neutralised with crushed lime
/ 2
02.4 (1 marks)
  • CFCs are persistent, so those already released continue to reach the stratosphere and destroy ozone for many years; some ODSs (eg dichloromethane) are not banned
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 112-113; AQA spec 3.2.1.3.
Question 310 marks · The hydrosphere: hydrological cycle & unsustainable exploitation · spec 3.2.2.1 · Genn pp. 117 · HYD-06

A dam and reservoir are to be built on a river to supply water and regulate flow.

[6 marks]03.1Explain three effects the reservoir may have on wildlife in and around the river.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why decomposition of flooded vegetation in a new reservoir contributes to climate change.
[2 marks]03.3Explain how a river-regulation reservoir reduces both flooding and water shortages downstream.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (6 marks)
  • Habitat change: flooding destroys the previous terrestrial / river habitats but creates a wetland which may be valuable (wetlands are uncommon)
  • Wildlife barrier: the dam blocks migrating fish (salmon, sturgeon) and prevents recolonisation along the river
  • Changed river regime downstream: regulated flow removes low-flow periods needed by species such as river turtles nesting on sandbanks and high flows that wash sediment from gravel spawning beds
  • Sedimentation: sediment settles in the reservoir instead of fertilising the floodplain and building river banks / coastlines downstream, increasing erosion
  • Microclimate: water's high heat capacity reduces temperature fluctuations; higher wind speeds; more evaporation, humidity, cloud and precipitation downwind
/ 6
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Vegetation / dead organic matter under water decays anaerobically
  • Releasing methane, a powerful greenhouse gas
/ 2
03.3 (2 marks)
  • After heavy rain surplus water is stored, reducing peak flow / flooding downstream
  • In dry periods the dam releases water to maintain river levels / supplies
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 117; AQA spec 3.2.2.1.

Question 510 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 195-196 · FN-06
[2 marks]05.1Explain the difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.
[2 marks]05.2State the sources of the two fuels used in fusion research.
[3 marks]05.3Explain why very high temperatures, a vacuum and a magnetic field are needed in a toroidal (torus) fusion reactor.
[3 marks]05.4Give two potential advantages of fusion over fission and one reason why it is not yet a commercial energy source.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • Fission: splitting the nuclei of large atoms (U-235, Pu-239) when hit by neutrons
  • Fusion: joining the nuclei of small atoms (deuterium and tritium - isotopes of hydrogen) to form helium, releasing a neutron and energy - the energy source of stars
/ 2
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Deuterium (hydrogen-2) extracted from (sea)water
  • Tritium (hydrogen-3) produced by neutron bombardment of lithium (a lithium blanket around the reactor)
/ 2
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Very high temperature turns hydrogen into a plasma (electrons stripped away) and gives nuclei enough kinetic energy to overcome the repulsion between positive nuclei and collide
  • Vacuum so the plasma is not cooled by air
  • Magnetic field holds the plasma centrally so it does not touch the container walls and cool
/ 3
05.4 (3 marks)
  • Fuel (deuterium / lithium) is abundant / effectively unlimited
  • Much less radioactive waste (no long-lived fission products); no chain reaction / meltdown risk; no weapons material
  • Not yet achieved sustained net energy output: reactors (JET) use more energy than they release; ITER aims for 500 MW out from 50 MW in; laser fusion also experimental; commercial viability decades away
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 195-196; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.

Question 710 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 94-96 · CC-11

Figure 1 shows the monthly flow of a river in South America that is fed partly by glacier meltwater, in 1990 and 2020. The glacier feeding the river shrank considerably between the two dates.

Figure 1
[3 marks]07.1Compare the river flow in 1990 and 2020.
[3 marks]07.2Explain how the shrinking of the glacier explains the differences.
[2 marks]07.3Suggest two problems the change in flow causes for people living downstream.
[2 marks]07.4Explain why a glacier can be extending down its valley yet still be losing ice volume.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • 2020 has higher peak flow in the wet season (32 vs 22 m3 s-1 in March)
  • 2020 has much lower dry-season flow (2 vs 9 m3 s-1 in July / August)
  • Flow is much more variable / seasonal in 2020; the river almost dries up
/ 3
07.2 (3 marks)
  • In 1990 snow / ice stored wet-season precipitation and released it gradually as meltwater during the dry season, evening out the flow
  • With less ice, precipitation now falls as rain / melts immediately and runs off quickly, increasing wet-season flow and flood risk
  • Little stored ice remains to melt in the dry season so flow falls very low
/ 3
07.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Water shortages for irrigation / drinking / HEP in the dry season
  • Flooding in the wet season
  • Sudden floods from ice-lake outbursts (glacial lake outburst floods)
/ 2
07.4 (2 marks)
  • Surface meltwater flows down cracks to the base, lubricating the ice so it slides faster; if this exceeds the faster melting at the front, the front advances
  • But if snowfall at the source has not increased, total volume falls; both retreating and extending glaciers can indicate warming
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 94-96; AQA spec 3.2.1.2.
Question 810 marks · Importance & features of energy resources · spec 3.3.2-3.3.3 · Genn pp. 179-181 · ENF-04

Figure 1

Question 1010 marks · Ionising radiation · spec 3.4.3.2.14 · Genn pp. 295-304 · RAD-06
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the risks and benefits of the uses of ionising radiation, and explain the principles used to control exposure of workers and the public.
[1 mark]10.2Give one reason why the risks and benefits of nuclear electricity may not fall on the same people.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Uses / benefits: nuclear electricity (low carbon, high energy density), medicine (X-rays, CT, radiotherapy, tracers, sterilising equipment), industry (thickness gauges, polymer cross-linking, testing turbines, oil exploration), agriculture (food irradiation, sterile male technique, mutation breeding), research tracers, smoke detectors, ship / submarine propulsion
  • Risks: free radical production, DNA damage, mutation, cancer; acute and chronic, somatic and gonadic effects; contamination vs exposure; activation products; long-lived wastes; accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima); weapons fallout
  • Factors affecting risk: type of radiation (alpha, beta, gamma, neutrons - penetration, RBE / weighting factors), half-life, dose (Gy, Sv), route of exposure
  • Risk:benefit analysis and why it is difficult (latency, alternative causes, poor exposure data, risks and benefits to different people, offsets such as avoided surgery)
  • Principles: ALARA, BATNEEC; closed sources, remote handling, absorbers / shielding, distance (inverse square law), time, protective clothing, decontamination, potassium iodide
  • Monitoring: dosemeters, film badges, air and contamination monitors; environmental monitoring via CPA and CGM; waste categories and disposal (vitrification, encapsulation, Drigg)
  • Evaluation / judgement: benefits generally outweigh risks when controls are applied; public perception vs actual risk (background radon 50% of dose vs <1% from nuclear industry)
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Consumers of the electricity may live in a different country / region from the uranium miners or the communities near waste stores who bear the risks
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 295-304; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.14.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss how knowledge of the properties of pesticides can be used to reduce their environmental impacts while maintaining food production.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: toxicity (LD50, MDAF, MDNF), specificity, persistence, liposolubility/water solubility, mobility, mode of action (contact/systemic), synergism
  • Bioaccumulation and biomagnification explained by persistence + liposolubility; DDT and birds of prey as a case study
  • Comparison of organochlorines, organophosphates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids with their relative advantages and disadvantages
  • Reducing impact: restrictions/bans, choice of non-persistent and specific pesticides, systemic pesticides and seed coatings, application timing (night, still days, not in flower), droplet size
  • Alternatives: cultural control (rotation, barrier/companion crops), biological control, predator habitats, sterile males, pheromone traps, genetic resistance, GM crops, integrated pest management
  • Trade-offs: yield, cost, human health (organophosphates), resistance evolution, pollinator services; risk:benefit; sustainability and the circular economy
  • Judgement: no pesticide is perfectly specific; combination of property-based selection and integrated control is most sustainable
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)

[25 marks]11.2To what extent can renewable energy resources replace fossil fuels in meeting the energy needs of a modern society?
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Fossil fuel features that shaped society: energy density, storage, transport, high temperatures, liquid vehicle fuels, established infrastructure
  • Renewable features: intermittent / unpredictable (wind, solar) vs predictable (tidal) vs controllable (HEP, biofuel, geothermal); low energy density; location; low pollution but manufacturing impacts
