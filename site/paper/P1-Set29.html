03.13 marks1 mark each

1 mark for each of the 3 points below.

  1. Choosing by eye is biased: people are drawn to patches that look interesting, flowery or easy to reach, so the sample does not represent the whole field

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Deciding how much to control pollution starts with predicting where a pollutant will go. Critical Pathway Analysis uses the state of the effluent, wind and currents, geology, the effect of pH and oxygen on solubility, bioaccumulation and local food sources to predict whether a discharge will disperse harmlessly or concentrate somewhere sensitive; it is mainly used for radioactive discharges and means monitoring can focus on the sites at risk rather than sampling everywhere. Critical Group Monitoring then checks the exposure of the people most at risk from their lifestyle before any health effects occur: if their risk is acceptable everyone else's is lower, and if not, emissions are reduced.

The choice of control is guided by principles. The polluter pays principle gives a financial incentive to prevent damage, but only if penalties are high and polluters cannot escape responsibility. The precautionary principle assumes a waste is harmful until research shows otherwise, so ignorance is no defence. Because the cost of control rises steeply as emissions approach zero, emissions are set As Low As Reasonably Achievable using the Best Available Technology Not Entailing Excessive Cost. Where emission location and timing can be chosen (marine outfalls, downwind of towns, not over aquifers; avoiding temperature inversions) severity is reduced cheaply.

Overall, prevention of production (fuel desulfurisation) or of release (electrostatic precipitators, catalytic converters) is more effective than post-release remediation such as oil-spill clean-up, which never recovers all the pollutant. CPA depends on understanding pathways, and transboundary pollutants need international enforcement, but together these principles give a rational basis for deciding acceptable risk rather than aiming for an unaffordable zero.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

Pollution can be controlled in lots of ways. Critical Pathway Analysis looks at where pollution goes and Critical Group Monitoring looks at people. The polluter pays principle means the person who pollutes has to pay. The precautionary principle means being careful. Pollution can be prevented, or cleaned up afterwards with things like booms for oil spills. Electric cars are an alternative process. Pollution control can be expensive so ALARA is used. All of these are good ways of controlling pollution.

What makes the difference
  • The Level 3 answer explains how each principle works (why penalties matter, why costs rise near zero emissions); the weak answer only names them.
  • The Level 3 answer groups the material logically (predict, decide, select) rather than listing everything at the same weight.
  • 'Evaluate' needs comparison and a judgement: prevention vs remediation, CPA's dependence on understanding pathways, and a conclusion about acceptable risk. The weak answer ends with 'all of these are good'.
  • Precise examples (desulfurisation, precipitators, catalytic converters, temperature inversions) show applied knowledge; 'things like booms' does not.
