Question 110 marks · Solid wastes · spec 3.4.3.2.12 · Genn pp. 282-283 · SW-04

A coal-mine spoil heap on a hillside has been abandoned. The spoil contains shale with some iron pyrites and residual coal.

[6 marks]01.1Explain three problems this spoil heap could cause and describe how each could be managed.
[2 marks]01.2Explain why deep layers of soil are applied when a spoil heap containing toxic materials is restored.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why an old steel-works spoil heap may be valuable for wildlife conservation.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (6 marks)
  • Instability / landslips on the hillside, especially after heavy rain - compaction, drainage, establishing vegetation whose roots bind the spoil, regrading slopes
  • Acid drainage from oxidation of pyrites, with toxic metals - collect and treat leachate, add lime to raise pH
  • Spontaneous combustion of residual coal / hydrocarbons - layers of fine-grained material to reduce air flow
  • Lack of nutrients / poor plant growth - add topsoil, sewage sludge, fertiliser
  • Unattractive topography - landscaping / reshaping
  • Dust / heavy metals - cover and keep at high pH
/ 6
01.2 (2 marks)
  • So that plant roots cannot reach the toxic layer
  • Otherwise toxins could be taken up and brought to the surface / dispersed via leaf litter and food chains
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Steel slag is alkaline, producing unusual calcareous / high-pH soil conditions
  • Supports uncommon plants such as cowslips / orchids that cannot compete on fertile neutral soils
/ 2

Question 210 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 132-134 · MIN-01
[4 marks]02.1Describe how hydrothermal deposition produces exploitable concentrations of metal ores.
[2 marks]02.2Explain how Proterozoic marine sediments produced the world's major iron ore deposits.
[2 marks]02.3Explain how alluvial (physical sediment) processes separate and concentrate minerals such as gold and tin ore.
[2 marks]02.4Give two examples of mineral deposits formed by biological sedimentation and state their origin.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
  • Igneous intrusions (batholiths) of magma cool within the crust, heating groundwater to produce superheated, pressurised water
  • The hot water dissolves minerals from the surrounding rock and the mineral-rich solutions travel along fissures / cracks away from the batholith
  • As the solutions cool the dissolved minerals crystallise in order of solubility (least soluble first) - fractional crystallisation
  • So different metals are separated and concentrated in veins, eg tin, copper, lead, silver, gold, arsenic (SW England batholith)
/ 4
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Dissolved iron compounds in ancient seas were oxidised by the oxygen released by early photosynthetic organisms (2.5-1.8 billion years ago)
  • Iron oxides are insoluble so they were deposited as sediments (haematite, magnetite)
/ 2
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Flowing water carries solid particles; the ability to carry them depends on the water velocity and the density of the solids
  • As velocity falls, dense minerals (gold, tin ore) are deposited first while lighter material is carried further, concentrating the heavy minerals in particular places
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Limestone / chalk from the shells of marine organisms
  • Coal from terrestrial vegetation
  • Crude oil and natural gas from marine organisms (plankton)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 132-134; AQA spec 3.2.3.2.

[3 marks]04.1A house has 20 m2 of single glazing. The average temperature difference between inside and outside over the heating season is 12 °C. Calculate the reduction in the rate of heat loss if the windows are replaced with argon-filled low-e double glazing.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why the gap between the panes should be as large as possible but not too large.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how low-emissivity glass and inert gas filling each reduce heat loss.

04.1 (3 marks)
  • Single: 5.8 x 20 x 12 = 1392 W
  • Argon: 1.6 x 20 x 12 = 384 W
  • Reduction = 1008 W (accept 1010)
/ 3
04.2 (2 marks)
  • A wider gap of trapped gas reduces conduction because gas is a poor conductor

  • Used where there is not room for thick gas-filled multiple glazing (eg historic buildings)
/ 2

  • Each extra layer saves less energy (diminishing returns) while cost rises, so beyond the optimum the cost of insulation exceeds the value of energy saved

Question 610 marks · The hydrosphere: hydrological cycle & unsustainable exploitation · spec 3.2.2.1 · Genn pp. 116-117 · HYD-02
[6 marks]06.1Explain how each of the following human activities alters processes in the hydrological cycle.
[2 marks]06.2Describe two ways in which global climate change alters the hydrological cycle.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why the impact of industrial and domestic water use on the hydrological cycle is usually local.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (6 marks)
  • Deforestation: less interception so less evaporation from leaves; less transpiration so less water returned to the atmosphere and less precipitation downwind; more infiltration and surface runoff, higher river flows / flooding
  • Urban development: impermeable concrete / tarmac reduce infiltration and aquifer recharge; runoff is faster and greater, increasing river flooding downstream; drains carry water quickly to rivers
  • Agriculture: soil compaction by machinery / trampling reduces infiltration and soil moisture, increases runoff; irrigation increases evaporation; loss of soil biota (worms) reduces infiltration and water retention; drainage lowers water tables
/ 6
06.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Higher temperatures increase evaporation and change the amount, timing, type (rain vs snow) and location of precipitation
  • More rapid melting of snow and ice changes river regimes: more flooding after rain, lower flows in dry periods
  • Changed wind patterns move humid air to different areas
  • Sea level rise / salt incursion into coastal aquifers
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Most water abstracted is cleaned and returned to the river / sea so the main impact is on quality (pollution) not quantity
  • Effects are noticeable only where large urban centres draw on restricted local supplies (aquifers, small rivers)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 116-117; AQA spec 3.2.2.1.
Question 75 marks · Pesticides · spec 3.4.3.2.8 · Genn pp. 263-266 · PEST-06
[1 mark]07.1Define the term persistence as applied to a pollutant.
[4 marks]07.2Explain how the persistence of a pesticide is affected by two named environmental factors.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (1 marks)
  • The length of time a pollutant remains in the environment before it is broken down / degraded (chemically or biologically)
/ 1
07.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Light: UV light degrades many pesticides (eg neonicotinoids break down fastest in sunlight) so persistence is shorter in bright conditions
  • Temperature: higher temperatures increase the rate of chemical breakdown / microbial decomposition
  • Soil microbes / oxygen: aerobic microbial activity breaks pesticides down so persistence is shorter in well-aerated, biologically active soils
  • pH / presence of other chemicals may increase or decrease the rate of degradation
  • Adsorption onto clay / organic matter may protect from degradation and increase persistence

Question 910 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.2.2 · Genn pp. 240-242, 255 · PPRO-04
[6 marks]09.1Explain how each of the following environmental factors affects the rate of degradation of pollutants. Give a named example of a pollutant for each factor.
[2 marks]09.2Explain how the presence of adsorbent materials such as clay can reduce the severity of pollution by heavy metals or radioactive caesium-137.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why a fall in pH increases the harm caused by many heavy metals.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (6 marks)
  • Temperature: most chemical / biological reactions are faster at higher temperature so degradable pollutants (eg sewage) break down faster; but faster decomposition may increase deoxygenation
  • Light: provides the activation energy for chemical reactions; photodegradation of pesticides (eg neonicotinoids break down fastest in sunlight); light drives photochemical smog reactions producing secondary pollutants
  • Oxygen: needed for aerobic bacterial decomposition of organic wastes / sewage; oxidation of sulfide ores produces sulfur dioxide / acid mine drainage; oxidation state affects the solubility of metals
/ 6
09.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Pollutant ions adsorb onto (the surface of) clay particles / organic matter in soil or sediment
  • So they are immobilised and cannot be absorbed by organisms / leached into water; radiation is absorbed in the ground
  • But they may be released later if conditions change (eg storms disturbing sediment, low pH)
/ 2
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Many heavy metals (eg lead, aluminium) are more soluble at low pH
  • So they are mobilised / leached from soil or sediments and can be absorbed by organisms / enter water bodies
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 240-242, 255; AQA spec 3.4.2.2.
Question 1010 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 196-217 · REN-08
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the potential contribution of renewable energy resources to future energy supplies in a country such as the UK.
[1 mark]10.2State the factor by which the kinetic energy of wind increases when wind velocity doubles.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Properties of renewables: intermittency, predictability, low energy density, storage, applicability, locational constraints, impacts, technology level, economics
  • Wind: large offshore resource in shallow seas, falling costs, HAWT / VAWT / direct drive developments; intermittent, needs storage and grid; land-use conflicts
  • Solar: PV costs falling rapidly, roof space, but low winter insolation in the UK; CSP unsuitable; heat pumps and photothermal for heating
  • HEP: most large sites used; low-head, helical and micro-hydro extend use; pumped storage vital
  • Tidal: large range (Severn, Mersey), predictable; barrages costly with high impact, lagoons and in-stream turbines lower impact
  • Wave: long Atlantic fetch, but technology immature
  • Biofuels: wastes, anaerobic digestion, energy crops vs food / habitat
  • Geothermal: limited to low-temperature schemes / heat pumps
  • Systems issues: storage (pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen, P2G, V2G), grid management, diversity of sources, energy conservation reducing demand, electrification of transport
  • Judgement: renewables could supply most electricity with storage and a diverse mix, but heat and transport are harder; transition needs investment and lifestyle change
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Eight times (energy proportional to velocity cubed)
/ 1

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how knowledge of the properties of pesticides can be used to reduce their environmental impacts while maintaining food production.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: toxicity (LD50, MDAF, MDNF), specificity, persistence, liposolubility/water solubility, mobility, mode of action (contact/systemic), synergism
  • Bioaccumulation and biomagnification explained by persistence + liposolubility; DDT and birds of prey as a case study
  • Comparison of organochlorines, organophosphates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids with their relative advantages and disadvantages
  • Reducing impact: restrictions/bans, choice of non-persistent and specific pesticides, systemic pesticides and seed coatings, application timing (night, still days, not in flower), droplet size
  • Alternatives: cultural control (rotation, barrier/companion crops), biological control, predator habitats, sterile males, pheromone traps, genetic resistance, GM crops, integrated pest management
  • Trade-offs: yield, cost, human health (organophosphates), resistance evolution, pollinator services; risk:benefit; sustainability and the circular economy
  • Judgement: no pesticide is perfectly specific; combination of property-based selection and integrated control is most sustainable
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
