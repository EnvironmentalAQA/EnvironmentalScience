Question 110 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 15-17 · BIOV-02

Table 1 shows characteristics introduced into commercial crops from crop wild relatives (CWRs).

Table 1

TomatoSingle wild speciesResistance to nine major diseases

[3 marks]01.1Explain why commercial crop varieties often lack the characteristics shown in Table 1.
[3 marks]01.2Explain what is meant by a Vavilov centre (centre of diversity) and why these areas are conservation priorities.
[3 marks]01.3Explain why a large population does not necessarily have a large gene pool, and why a small gene pool is a problem.
[1 mark]01.4Give one wild plant species with potential as a new commercial food crop and the characteristic that makes it valuable.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • Domesticated crops were bred from a small number of original plants so they have a small gene pool / low genetic diversity
  • Selective breeding for yield, appearance or shelf life may have lost other useful genes (eg taste, disease resistance)
  • Genetically similar plants share the same susceptibilities so a new disease or environmental change can affect the whole crop
/ 3
01.2 (3 marks)
  • A region with a high concentration of the wild relatives of important crops - usually where the crop was first domesticated (eg wheat / barley in the Middle East; potato, tomato in South America; rice in India; maize in Central America)
  • They hold the largest gene pools / most genetic variety for future crop breeding (disease resistance, drought and salt tolerance, yield)
  • Many are in regions where environmental degradation / habitat loss threatens the wild plants
/ 3
01.3 (3 marks)
  • A large population may all descend from a few closely related individuals so they are genetically similar
  • Similar adaptations mean all are susceptible to the same change (disease, climate)
  • Greater risk of inbreeding so disadvantageous recessive genes are expressed (eg amelanism, poor muscle development in lion cubs); so species should be protected across their whole range to retain regional genes
/ 3
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Morama bean (drought resistant, soya-like protein); yeheb tree (drought resistant nuts, poor soils); potato bean (high protein); perennial maize / Kernza (no annual replanting, less ploughing / erosion); salt-tolerant spinach relatives
/ 1

[3 marks]02.1Explain why traditional 'slash and burn' subsistence agriculture was sustainable but has become a threat.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why clearance for cattle ranching or cash crops is usually unsustainable.
[3 marks]02.3Describe how each of the following threatens rainforest.
[2 marks]02.4Describe how a debt-for-nature swap works.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Small clearings were farmed for a few years then abandoned as nutrients declined; the forest recolonised and soil fertility recovered before the area was cleared again
  • Population growth shortens the gap between clearances so the forest and soil do not recover
  • Clearings enlarge and degrade; less regrowth
/ 3
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Rainforest soils are thin and nutrient-poor (nutrients held in the biomass); once cleared, fertility falls in a few years and yields drop, so more land is cleared
  • Abandoned land may not be recolonised if no forest survives nearby; erosion of exposed soil
/ 2
02.3 (3 marks)
  • Mining (aluminium, iron, gold, copper) destroys habitat directly and pollutes rivers with turbid drainage, cyanide and mercury from processing
  • Reservoirs (eg Tucurui, 2850 km2) flood large areas, block migratory fish and river dolphins; decomposing vegetation produces acidic, deoxygenated water; fluctuating downstream flows
  • Droughts increase frequency, area and duration of fires (long fires boil sap and kill trees); cloud forests lose humidity as clouds form higher so frogs and epiphytes decline; some models predict replacement by savannah
/ 3
02.4 (2 marks)
  • A creditor country or conservation charity cancels / buys part of a debtor country's foreign debt
  • In return the country guarantees to protect an area of rainforest (eg USA - Guatemala, Brazil; WWF - Costa Rica, Philippines)
/ 2

[3 marks]03.1Use Table 1 to identify which species is r-selected and which is k-selected, and explain the difference between the strategies.
[3 marks]03.2Explain why k-selected species are much more easily over-exploited than r-selected species.
[1 mark]03.3State the equation used to forecast a change in population size.
[3 marks]03.4Explain what is meant by maximum sustainable yield and how it relates to carrying capacity.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • Anchovy r-selected; blue whale k-selected
  • r-selected: mature quickly, produce many young, disperse widely, short lives; adapted to respond rapidly to high death rates / unstable conditions (mice, locusts, greenfly, rabbits)
  • k-selected: mature late, few young with parental care, long-lived; populations stable near carrying capacity (whales, elephants, rhinos)
/ 3
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Low reproductive rate means losses from hunting / habitat change cannot be replaced quickly
  • An increase in death rate above the low birth rate causes a population crash
  • Recovery takes decades (eg blue whale still far below pre-whaling numbers after the 1986 moratorium); r-selected species rebound within a few years once exploitation stops
/ 3
03.3 (1 marks)
  • New population = starting population + births + immigrants - deaths - emigrants
/ 1
03.4 (3 marks)
  • The greatest rate of exploitation (harvest) possible without causing long-term population decline
  • Equals the surplus production: highest when the population is well below carrying capacity where growth rate is fastest (middle of the sigmoid curve)
  • At carrying capacity net growth is zero so any harvest reduces the population; harvesting above MSY causes decline
/ 3

[2 marks]04.3Explain why keeping livestock warm increases the amount of food converted to growth.

04.1 (3 marks)
  • B: frost-free period (150 days) exceeds the 120 needed whereas A (110) is too short - late frosts would kill seedlings
  • B is warmer / longer thermal growing season because south-facing slopes receive more insolation; valley floors are frost pockets where cold dense air collects
  • But B has lower rainfall and a steeper slope (erosion / machinery difficulties) so irrigation / contour cultivation may be needed
/ 3
04.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Greenhouses / polytunnels heated by solar gain or burning gas / paraffin (ventilated to prevent overheating)
  • Woven plastic cloches / fleece over rows to retain warm air near the ground
  • Burning oil / gas or using large fans in orchards to prevent frost damage to blossom
  • Site selection (aspect, avoiding frost pockets); heated buildings and shelter for livestock
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Mammals and birds use food energy to maintain a constant body temperature (thermoregulation)
  • Reducing heat loss (shelter, heated buildings) means less food energy is used for heat and more is available for growth
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Long days increase milk production and egg laying; poultry grow best with short days; sheep mate as days shorten in autumn
  • Artificial lighting extends the growing season for greenhouse crops or simulates autumn in spring to give sheep a second mating / lambing season

Question 610 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-06
[3 marks]06.1Describe three methods of reducing seabird by-catch in long-line fisheries.
[3 marks]06.2Explain how restricting fishing effort and restricting fishing methods reduce overfishing.
[2 marks]06.3Describe how captive rearing and release ('population seeding') can increase wild populations of lobsters or oysters.
[2 marks]06.4Explain why collecting data on planktonic fish eggs and larvae helps to prevent population collapse.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Sinkers / weighted lines so hooks sink quickly below the reach of albatrosses
  • Setting lines at night when birds are not feeding
  • Bird-scaring streamers / decoys
  • Curved hooks that catch tuna but not birds
/ 3
06.2 (3 marks)
  • Effort: limits on boat size, engine power, days at sea, number of licences; decommissioning payments to reduce fleet size where overfishing is unavoidable
  • Methods: bans or length limits on drift nets; bans on demersal trawling over sensitive seabed (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank deep-water reefs)
  • Shorter trawl tows so turtles / by-catch survive (over 99% survive under 10 minutes)
/ 3
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Eggs / larvae are reared in captivity through the planktonic stage when most die (predation, failure to find habitat)
  • Juveniles are released when survival chances are good, increasing recruitment to the adult population
/ 2
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Egg / larval survival varies greatly between years so adult numbers do not predict recruitment
  • Several poor years would lower the MSY; egg surveys allow catches to be reduced in advance rather than after a sudden collapse
/ 2

Question 710 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.6 · Genn pp. 402-406 · MON-02
[8 marks]07.1Explain how each of the following new technologies improves the validity of ecological research.
[2 marks]07.2Explain why new technologies allow the collection of 'more representative' data.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (8 marks)
  • Tracking: continuous data on movements, territory size, migration routes and habitat use without disturbance, over large areas and long periods; acoustic tags in seawater; geolocators for small birds; more representative than occasional sightings
  • DNA: identifies individuals, gene pool size / inbreeding and regional populations (tracing ivory, timber, fish); eDNA detects rare or hidden aquatic species (great crested newt, invasive carp) from shed cells without capture
  • Image recognition: software identifies individuals from unique markings (tiger stripes, whale shark spots, fin damage) in camera-trap / drone / satellite images, giving population size, lifespan, social structure from large image sets quickly and objectively
  • Acoustic: bat detectors, hydrophones and sonograms record species that are hard to see (bats, cetaceans, insects), giving presence, abundance and activity continuously, day and night
/ 8
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Data collected continuously / over large areas / at night rather than at a few times and places a researcher can visit
  • Less disturbance so animals behave naturally; larger sample sizes; objective rather than subjective identification
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 402-406; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.6.

Question 1010 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3-3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 32-49 · CBR-09
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the relative importance of ex-situ and in-situ methods in the conservation of endangered species.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by rewilding.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • In-situ: species remain in natural conditions with the whole community and inter-species relationships; protected areas (SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Ramsar, MCZ), land ownership, agri-environment schemes, habitat creation and design (area, shape, corridors, diversity), management of abiotic and biotic factors, plagioclimax maintenance, population control, reintroductions and rewilding (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish beavers)
  • Limits of in-situ: external threats (pollution, climate change), enforcement, cost, conflicts with land use, small fragmented populations
  • Ex-situ: captive breeding when in-situ is impossible or insufficient; selection criteria; difficulties keeping and breeding (habitat size, food, relationships, breeding triggers, group dynamics, gene pool / inbreeding, hybridisation); techniques (stud books, cryopreservation, AI, embryo transfer, micropropagation, cloning); seed banks; successes (Hawaiian goose, Iberian lynx, red kite, large blue)
  • Limits of ex-situ: expensive, few species, loss of wild behaviour, small gene pools, release requires suitable habitat and post-release support (hard vs soft release, monitoring)
  • Judgement: ex-situ is a temporary safeguard / support that only succeeds if in-situ habitat exists to return to; both needed, in-situ fundamental
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Restoring natural processes / communities to a habitat changed by humans, often by reintroducing missing (keystone) species and removing human management such as grazing
/ 1

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how feedback mechanisms, tipping points and diversity influence the stability of natural systems, and how human activities affect them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Sustainability definitions; population and per capita impact; attitudes of rural and urban societies
  • Negative feedback: cloud cover and albedo, CO2 and photosynthesis, evaporation and precipitation, density-dependent population regulation
  • Positive feedback driven by human activities: permafrost, acidification, albedo decline, methane hydrates, fires, cirrus clouds, decomposition
  • Tipping points: rare under negative feedback; large events (Permian methane hydrate); self-sustaining change from human activities
  • Diversity and resistance: reefs and rainforests vs low-diversity agroecosystems lacking detritivores, pollinators and predators
  • Human responses that override feedback (fertilisers, irrigation, pesticides); links to climate change chapter; justified conclusion
