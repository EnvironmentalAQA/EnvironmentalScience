Question 110 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 40-42 · CBR-05

A conservation organisation is converting 700 ha of arable farmland on the coast into a wetland reserve for breeding and wintering birds (as at Wallasea Island).

[4 marks]01.1Explain why habitat area and shape are important in the design of the reserve.
[3 marks]01.2Explain how the reserve could be designed to provide a range of niches.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why some species may need to be deliberately introduced while others colonise naturally.
[1 mark]01.4Explain the value of a biological corridor linking the reserve to other wetlands.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Area must be large enough to support viable populations (breeding numbers high enough, gene pool large enough to avoid inbreeding); species higher up food chains need larger areas (lower density)
  • Large areas contain a full range of resources (eg different fruiting trees / feeding areas through the year)
  • Shape: the perimeter has edge conditions (a mix of neighbouring habitats); a compact shape maximises core habitat for species needing core conditions
  • Some species benefit from edges (higher light) or small isolated habitats (ponds too small for predatory fish suit amphibians)
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Varied topography: banks, lagoons, islands, ditches created with machinery give a range of water depths (emergent, floating-leaved, submerged plant zones)
  • Islands provide roosting / nesting sites safe from terrestrial predators (foxes)
  • Control of salinity (eg intertidal conditions for opossum shrimps feeding dunlin and avocet); scrapes for skylarks; different water depths for waders and ducks
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Mobile species (birds, flying insects) colonise easily
  • Less mobile species (many plants, amphibians, invertebrates) may not reach an isolated site so must be introduced; keystone species especially
/ 2
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Allows dispersal of surplus young, mixing of gene pools (less inbreeding) and recolonisation if a population dies out; avoids hazards (eg rope bridges for howler monkeys over roads)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 40-42; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 210 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.2-3.1.1.3 · Genn pp. 8-9 · LIFE-03
[3 marks]02.1Explain how the development of biogeochemical cycles and of transpiration by land plants helped to maintain stable conditions for life.
[3 marks]02.2Describe three limitations of early methods of studying past conditions on Earth.
[4 marks]02.3Describe how each of the following modern techniques provides information about past conditions.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Interconnected biological processes recycle nutrient elements so small amounts support life indefinitely without depletion; waste products of one process are raw materials of another so wastes do not build up
  • Transpiration returned water vapour to the atmosphere increasing rainfall elsewhere
  • Allowing more plant growth, further stabilising climate and soils
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Lack of ancient historical / instrumental data
  • Reliance on proxy data (tree rings, pollen) of limited reliability for ancient conditions
  • Lack of sophisticated equipment / inability to measure many factors
  • Limited coordination between researchers; lack of data collection in many areas (oceans, poles)
/ 3
02.3 (4 marks)
  • The ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in the ice indicates the temperature when the snow fell
  • Air bubbles trapped in ice give the composition of the past atmosphere (CO2, methane)
  • Ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 dates organic material (eg pollen in sediments)
  • More magnesium is incorporated into calcite at higher temperatures, so the ratio estimates past sea temperature
/ 4

[2 marks]03.1Define ecological footprint and biocapacity.
[2 marks]03.2Explain what is meant by a global hectare and why it is used.

03.1 (2 marks)
  • Ecological footprint: the area of the Earth's surface (global hectares) needed to provide the resources used by a person / population / activity and to absorb the wastes produced
  • Biocapacity: the biological productivity of an area (forests, cropland, grazing, fisheries, built land) - its ability to supply resources and absorb wastes, expressed in global hectares
/ 2
03.2 (2 marks)
  • A hectare of land / sea with the world-average biological productivity
  • Biocapacities of real areas vary widely so a standard unit allows footprints and biocapacities of different places to be compared
/ 2
03.3 (1 marks)
  • 8.1 / 1.7 = 4.8 (accept 4.7-4.8)
/ 1
03.4 (3 marks)
  • Higher HDI is associated with a larger footprint (USA 8.1 vs Bangladesh 0.8), though the UK achieves the same HDI as the USA with about half the footprint
  • Global footprint (2.8) exceeds global biocapacity (1.7) so humanity uses resources faster than they regenerate and CO2 accumulates (overshoot)
  • Earth Overshoot Day is the date each year when a year's worth of resources / waste absorption has been used up; it falls earlier each year (8 August in 2016)
/ 3
03.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Renewable energy and energy conservation (carbon footprint is the largest component)
  • Reduced meat consumption / food waste; local seasonal food
  • Circular economy: repair, re-use, recycling; longer product lives
  • Public transport, efficient buildings

Question 410 marks · Manipulation of food species & agricultural energetics · spec 3.5.1.2 · Genn pp. 326-328 · FS-04

Table 1 shows food conversion ratios (FCR) for livestock: the mass of feed needed to produce 1 kg of live mass gain.

Table 1
LivestockFCR / kg feed per kg gain
Farmed salmon1.2
Chicken1.8
Pork3.0
Beef (feedlot)7.0
Sheep8.0
[3 marks]04.1Use Table 1 to explain why intensive poultry and fish production convert feed to food more efficiently than beef.
[3 marks]04.2Explain three ways farmers reduce food chain energy losses in livestock production.
[4 marks]04.3Explain why growing crops for direct human consumption usually produces more food per hectare than rearing livestock, and describe one situation where livestock is the better option.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Chicken and salmon need far less feed per kg gained (1.2-1.8 vs 7.0)
  • Fish are ectotherms so no energy is used for thermoregulation and water supports their mass; poultry grow fast and are kept warm indoors with movement restricted
  • Cattle are large endotherms, use energy in movement, and lose energy in methane from cellulose digestion
/ 3
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Control of movement (housing / restricted grazing) so less energy is used in muscle activity
  • Temperature control (heated / insulated buildings) so less energy is used in maintaining body temperature
  • Species selection: choose species with low FCRs (poultry, fish); high-energy easily digested diets; slaughter at the fastest growth stage
/ 3
04.3 (4 marks)
  • Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next; most is lost as heat in respiration / metabolism or in undigested material
  • Humans eating plants (primary consumers) receive more of the energy captured by photosynthesis than eating animals fed on those plants (secondary consumers)
  • Livestock better where land cannot grow crops (upland, semi-arid, steep, thin soils): grazing animals with symbiotic gut bacteria digest cellulose in grass that humans cannot
  • Omnivores (pigs) convert food wastes (dairy by-products, catering waste) into meat
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 326-328; AQA spec 3.5.1.2.
Question 510 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 368-369 · FOR-04
[4 marks]05.1Explain the effects of deforestation on soil.
[4 marks]05.2Explain how deforestation affects hydrology and climate.
[2 marks]05.3Give two ecological impacts of deforestation other than the direct loss of species.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • Less dead organic matter / leaf litter so less humus and slower soil formation; fewer nutrients returned
  • Loss of canopy and litter exposes soil to rain splash and wind
  • Decomposing roots no longer bind soil so erosion, gullying and landslides increase (especially on slopes)
  • Reduced organic matter and biota lower infiltration and water retention
/ 4
05.2 (4 marks)
  • Reduced interception and transpiration so less water returned to the atmosphere and less rainfall downwind
  • Increased runoff: more flooding after rain, lower flows in dry periods; sedimentation of rivers
  • Increased albedo of cleared land (reflects more sunlight)
  • Reduced carbon sequestration and loss of the carbon reservoir (CO2 released by burning / decay) contributing to climate change
/ 4
05.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Fragmentation of remaining forest into isolated patches with non-viable populations / edge effects
  • Loss of inter-species relationships (pollinators, seed dispersers) causing further declines
  • Loss of genetic resources / potential medicines before they are discovered
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 368-369; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.
Question 610 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 78-81 · LP-05

Figure 1 shows the population of red deer on an island after they were introduced in year 0. No predators are present.

Figure 1
[4 marks]06.1Name the shape of the growth curve and explain the changes in growth rate shown.
[2 marks]06.2Explain the difference between density-dependent and density-independent factors, with an example of each.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why the deer population on the island may need to be culled even though it appears stable.
[2 marks]06.4Explain how predator and prey populations regulate each other.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (4 marks)
  • Sigmoidal (S-shaped) growth curve
  • Slow start (few breeding individuals), then rapid / exponential growth (years 4-10) as abundant food and space mean birth rate far exceeds death rate
  • Growth slows as density-dependent factors (intra-species competition for food, disease) raise the death rate
  • Population stabilises / fluctuates around about 920-950 - the carrying capacity - where birth rate equals death rate
/ 4
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Density-dependent: effect on survival increases with population density - food shortage / intra-species competition, disease spread, predation
  • Density-independent: chance of survival unaffected by density - drought, flood, volcanic eruption, severe winter
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Without wolves (natural predators removed) the population is regulated only by starvation / disease at a high density
  • At this density deer over-graze, preventing tree regeneration and damaging habitats / other species; culling keeps numbers below the damaging level (as in Scotland)
/ 2
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Rising prey numbers give predators more food so predator numbers rise; high predation then reduces the prey population
  • Food shortage reduces predators, allowing prey to recover - cyclic fluctuations with predators lagging behind prey
/ 2

Question 810 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 18-19 · BIOV-07
[2 marks]08.1Explain why the ecosystem services provided by other species are often given a low conservation priority.
[3 marks]08.2Explain how soil organisms provide services that humans depend on.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why pollination by insects is more useful than wind pollination to plants growing in forests.
[3 marks]08.4Explain how seed dispersal by animals increases the survival of a plant's offspring.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • The processes (eg regulation of atmospheric gases by photosynthesis and respiration) act in a dynamic equilibrium and cancel each other out
  • Because there is no overall change their actions go unnoticed / their importance is not appreciated until they are lost
/ 2
08.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Invertebrates, fungi and bacteria break down / decompose dead organic matter, releasing nutrients for plant growth
  • The organic matter and humus produced hold the soil together (reducing erosion) and regulate the water cycle, giving more even river flow and less flooding
  • Organic acids from decomposition aid the weathering of rock, producing more soil and releasing more nutrients
  • Microbes carry out the processes of the carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles so wastes do not build up and nutrients are not depleted
/ 3
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Trees reduce wind velocity so wind pollination would be ineffective / unreliable over long distances
  • Insects search over long distances for flowers so dispersed populations can be pollinated, and less pollen needs to be produced (energy saving); specialised flowers reduce pollen being wasted on other species
/ 2
08.4 (3 marks)
  • Dispersal is not haphazard: animals live in the same habitat as the plant so are likely to carry seeds to locations where they can survive
  • Seeds can be carried longer distances than by wind and can be larger, containing more nutrients for the seedling
  • Seeds passing through the gut are deposited in faecal material which acts as a fertiliser, and passage through the intestine may stimulate germination
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 18-19; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.3.

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss how feedback mechanisms, tipping points and diversity influence the stability of natural systems, and how human activities affect them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Sustainability definitions; population and per capita impact; attitudes of rural and urban societies
  • Negative feedback: cloud cover and albedo, CO2 and photosynthesis, evaporation and precipitation, density-dependent population regulation
  • Positive feedback driven by human activities: permafrost, acidification, albedo decline, methane hydrates, fires, cirrus clouds, decomposition
  • Tipping points: rare under negative feedback; large events (Permian methane hydrate); self-sustaining change from human activities
  • Diversity and resistance: reefs and rainforests vs low-diversity agroecosystems lacking detritivores, pollinators and predators
  • Human responses that override feedback (fertilisers, irrigation, pesticides); links to climate change chapter; justified conclusion
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
