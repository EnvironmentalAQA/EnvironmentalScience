Question 110 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.1, 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 158-159, 412 · SOIL-07

A student determined the water and organic matter content of a soil sample. Table 1 shows the results.

Table 1
MeasurementMass / g
Empty crucible25.0
Crucible + fresh soil65.0
Crucible + soil after drying at 105 °C to constant mass55.0
Crucible + soil after heating in a furnace at 550 °C52.6
[2 marks]01.1Calculate the percentage water content of the fresh soil.
[2 marks]01.2Calculate the percentage organic matter content of the dry soil.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why the soil is dried to constant mass before the organic matter is burnt off.
[3 marks]01.4Explain how the organic matter content affects the fertility and erosion risk of this soil.
[1 mark]01.5Suggest one reason why two students measuring the same field might get different organic matter results.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Water = 65.0 - 55.0 = 10.0 g; fresh soil = 40.0 g
  • 10.0 / 40.0 x 100 = 25.0%
/ 2
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Organic matter = 55.0 - 52.6 = 2.4 g; dry soil = 30.0 g
  • 2.4 / 30.0 x 100 = 8.0%
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
  • So all water is removed and the mass loss on burning is due to organic matter only
  • Heating until mass no longer changes shows all water has evaporated
/ 2
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Organic matter releases nutrients as it decomposes and provides food for soil biota
  • Increases water retention and, as humus, binds particles into crumb peds improving structure / aeration
  • Surface organic matter / humus adhesion reduce rain splash and wind erosion
/ 3
01.5 (1 marks)
  • Samples taken from different depths / locations (organic matter is concentrated near the surface); incomplete burning; different heating times
/ 1

Question 315 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 103-104, 371-372 · CC-07
[2 marks]03.1Explain the difference between a negative and a positive feedback mechanism.
[4 marks]03.2Describe two negative feedback mechanisms that may reduce global warming.
[4 marks]03.3Explain how melting permafrost and melting methane hydrate act as positive feedback mechanisms.
[3 marks]03.4Give three other positive feedback mechanisms.
[2 marks]03.5Explain what is meant by a climate tipping point.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Negative feedback: a change causes other changes that reduce the rate or size of the original change, re-establishing equilibrium
  • Positive feedback: a change causes other changes that increase the original change
/ 2
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Increased low-level cloud: higher temperatures increase evaporation and condensation, producing more cloud; clouds have a high albedo so more sunlight is reflected and warming is reduced
  • Increased photosynthesis: higher temperatures and CO2 increase photosynthesis, removing CO2; if stored in woody tissue atmospheric CO2 rises less
/ 4
03.3 (4 marks)
  • Permafrost: frozen waterlogged Arctic soils contain dead organic matter that decomposed anaerobically producing methane trapped in the frozen ground
  • Warming thaws the permafrost, releasing methane (a powerful greenhouse gas) and allowing further decomposition, causing more warming
  • Methane hydrate: methane from decomposed organic matter in deep sea sediments is held as a solid under high pressure and low temperature
  • If deep water warms the hydrate melts, releasing methane into the atmosphere and causing further warming
/ 4
03.4 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Reduced albedo as snow / ice melts
  • Ocean acidification reducing coral survival and carbon sequestration
  • Increased forest and peat fires releasing CO2
  • Faster decomposition of soil organic matter releasing CO2
  • Increased water vapour (a greenhouse gas) in warmer air
  • Increased cirrus (high) cloud which traps infrared
/ 3
03.5 (2 marks)
  • A point at which natural processes triggered by human-caused warming become self-sustaining / unstoppable
  • So that stopping the original human activity would no longer stop climate change (eg runaway permafrost melt, methane hydrate release, forest fires)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 103-104, 371-372; AQA spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1.
Question 410 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 199-203 · REN-01

Table 1 shows the efficiency and cost of four types of solar photovoltaic (PV) cell.

Table 1
PV cell typeEfficiency / %Relative cost per m2
Amorphous silicon (thin film)81
Polycrystalline silicon162
Monocrystalline silicon with anti-reflective surface223
Multi-junction cell4025
[2 marks]04.1Describe how a photovoltaic cell generates electricity.
[3 marks]04.2Explain how anti-reflective surfaces and multi-junction cells increase efficiency.
[3 marks]04.3Use Table 1 to suggest which cell type would be chosen for a large solar farm on cheap desert land and which for a satellite. Justify your choices.
[2 marks]04.4Explain why solar PV output is intermittent and unpredictable.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • Photons of light absorbed by the (n-type silicon) upper layer dislodge electrons, making it negatively charged
  • Electrons flow through an external conductor / circuit to the relatively positive lower layer; the current powers appliances
/ 2
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Smooth cells reflect about 30% of the light; grooved / textured surfaces (mimicking moth eyes) reflect light into the cell rather than away
  • Multi-junction cells have several layers of different materials, each absorbing different wavelengths
  • So a greater proportion of the available light is absorbed and converted to electricity
/ 3
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Solar farm: polycrystalline / thin film - lowest cost per unit electricity where land area is not limiting (efficiency per m2 matters less)
  • Satellite: multi-junction - highest efficiency where mass / area is critical and cost is less important
  • Cost per watt = cost / efficiency: thin film 0.125, poly 0.125, mono 0.136, multi-junction 0.625 per unit
/ 3
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Intermittent: no output at night and low in winter (daily and seasonal cycles) - predictable
  • Unpredictable: cloud cover, dust and smoke reduce intensity and scatter light and cannot be forecast accurately
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 199-203; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.

Question 710 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 266-268 · NUT-01

Figure 1 shows the dissolved oxygen concentration in a shallow lowland lake over 48 hours in late summer. The lake receives runoff from arable farmland and effluent from a sewage works.

Figure 1
[2 marks]07.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]07.2Explain the causes of the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why fish kills in eutrophic lakes usually happen at night or at the end of summer.
[2 marks]07.4Give two other harmful effects of algal blooms.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Dissolved oxygen fluctuates daily / 24-hour cycle: highest in the afternoon (about 13-13.5 mg dm-3 at 16 and 40 h) and lowest at night / early morning (2.5-3.5 mg dm-3 at 4 and 28 h)
  • The night-time minimum is lower on the second night (2.5 vs 3.5)
/ 2
07.2 (4 marks)
  • Nitrates (from fertiliser runoff) and phosphates (from sewage effluent) cause eutrophication: rapid growth of algae / cyanobacteria (algal bloom)
  • During the day photosynthesis by the dense algae releases oxygen so concentrations rise (above the normal saturation level)
  • At night there is no photosynthesis but respiration by algae / animals / bacteria continues, using oxygen
  • Aerobic bacterial decomposition of dead algae / shaded macrophytes uses oxygen; warm summer water holds less oxygen and decay is rapid
/ 4
07.3 (2 marks)
  • At night no photosynthesis replaces the oxygen used in respiration / decomposition so concentrations fall below the minimum fish can tolerate
  • At the end of summer large amounts of dead vegetation decay rapidly in warm water causing severe deoxygenation
/ 2
07.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Cyanobacteria release toxins harmful to livestock, pets and people
  • Floating algae shade submerged / rooted macrophytes which die, disrupting food webs
  • Algae are an unreliable food source (die off rapidly) so food webs are poor
  • Blocked filters / unpleasant taste and smell in water supplies
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 266-268; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.

[1 mark]09.1Calculate how many times more concentrated cadmium is in the kidneys of village A residents than in their rice.
[1 mark]09.2Explain why cadmium and zinc pollution often occur together.
[4 marks]09.3Use Table 1 and your knowledge of the properties of cadmium to explain the high rate of bone disease in village A.
[2 marks]09.4Suggest two ways cadmium pollution from the mine could be controlled.
[2 marks]09.5Cadmium is used in cadmium-telluride photovoltaic panels. Suggest why this new use may cause pollution in the future.

09.1 (1 marks)
  • 180 / 1.2 = 150

  • Cadmium and zinc occur together in the same ores so mine drainage / smelting releases both

  • Panels have a limited life; disposal of old panels (landfill, incineration of pigments) could release cadmium unless recycled

Question 1015 marks · Water treatment, conservation & new sources · spec 3.2.2.4-3.2.2.5 · Genn pp. 122-127 · HYD-13

Figure 1 shows the river level below a reservoir site during one year, before and after a river-regulation reservoir was built.

Figure 1
[2 marks]10.1Use Figure 1 to calculate the reduction in the annual range of river level produced by the reservoir.
[2 marks]10.2Explain how the reservoir produces the changes shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]10.3Explain why human activities in the catchment may have increased the extremes in river flow before the reservoir was built.
[9 marks]10.4Discuss the factors that should be considered when selecting a site for a new reservoir, and evaluate the alternative strategies for increasing the sustainability of water supplies.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (2 marks)
  • No reservoir range = 3.9 - 0.7 = 3.2 m; with reservoir = 2.7 - 1.7 = 1.0 m
  • Reduction = 2.2 m
/ 2
10.2 (2 marks)
  • After heavy rainfall surplus water is stored, reducing peak flows / flooding downstream
  • During dry periods the dam is opened to release water and maintain river levels
/ 2
10.3 (2 marks)
  • Deforestation / urbanisation reduce infiltration and interception
  • So runoff reaches the river faster after rain and less water is stored to maintain flow in dry periods
/ 2
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Site factors: topography - narrow exit from a large deep basin so a small dam holds a large volume; impermeable, strong bedrock without faults or seismic activity; large catchment with regular high rainfall and low evaporation; catchment land use with low pollution risk (pesticides, industry) and low soil erosion to avoid sedimentation; forest / vegetation in the flooded area decaying anaerobically releasing methane; access for workers and materials and distance to demand; value of the land lost (urban, conservation, agricultural) - loss-benefit analysis differs between countries
  • Alternatives for supply: rainwater collection, rivers, estuary barrages, desalination (energy-intensive so only where freshwater is inadequate), unexploited aquifers (N Africa, Kenya), inter-basin transfers (China, Australia, Wales-England), artificial aquifer recharge using surplus wet-season water, river-regulation reservoirs, afforestation to even out flow
  • Conservation strategies: low-water appliances, dual-flush toilets, xeriscaping, drip irrigation, grey-water re-use, leak repair (about 20% lost in the UK), metering
  • Treatment / pollution control allowing re-use of contaminated sources: sewage treatment, buffer strips, mine drainage control
  • Evaluation: reservoirs give large reliable supplies and flood control but flood land, cause habitat loss and methane; conservation is cheap and quick but limited by behaviour; transfers create conflicts between regions; desalination has high energy / carbon cost; combination needed as demand grows
  • Justified conclusion
/ 9

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how knowledge of the properties of energy resources can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: energy density, renewability, abundance, location, intermittency / predictability, storage, transport, conversions, technology level, environmental impacts
  • Fossil fuels: choosing lower-impact fuels (gas over coal), desulfurisation, CCS, efficient combustion, siting power stations near cooling water and coal ports; new extraction technologies (directional drilling, secondary / tertiary recovery) and their impacts (fracking, tar sands)
  • Nuclear: high energy density reduces transport; waste properties (half-life) determine storage; thorium and fusion prospects
  • Renewables: matching resource to location (wind, HEP, solar, wave, tidal, geothermal, biofuel siting); intermittency managed by storage (pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen, P2G, V2G), grid management and diversity; low-impact designs (helical turbines, VAWT, tidal lagoons)
  • Energy conservation reduces demand: transport, buildings, industry (CHP, heat exchangers)
  • Impacts before and during use; life-cycle / embodied energy
  • Evaluation: no resource is impact-free; knowledge allows the best mix and mitigation; judgement
