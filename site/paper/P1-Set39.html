  • Still need condenser cooling water (coast / large lake) and a stable site

  • Uranium must be mined, purified, concentrated and processed into fuel using (fossil) energy; reactor construction and decommissioning have embodied energy
/ 1
02.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:

  • Large projects often overrun original cost estimates
  • Long-term waste disposal costs uncertain
  • State subsidies may hide costs

  • Dissolves / weakens limestone and marble buildings and statues (eg York Minster)
/ 2

  • Sulfur trioxide dissolves in water droplets to form sulfuric acid (SO2 alone gives the weaker sulfurous acid)

Figure 1
[1 mark]05.1Use Figure 1 to estimate the LD50 of the insecticide for the pest species.
[1 mark]05.2Define the term MDNF (maximum dose never fatal).
[3 marks]05.3Use Figure 1 to explain why it may not be possible to kill all of the pest species without harming bees.
[2 marks]05.4Suggest how the farmer could reduce the harm to bees without reducing the dose applied.
[3 marks]05.5Explain why a pesticide with high specificity is less likely to cause environmental damage.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • 25 g ha-1 (accept 24-27)
/ 1
05.2 (1 marks)
  • The highest dose that can be applied without killing any members of the population / any individuals of the (non-target) species
/ 1
05.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • The MDAF (minimum dose always fatal) of the pest is about 50 g ha-1
  • This is higher than the MDNF of the bee (about 20 g ha-1)
  • So a dose that kills all pests kills some bees / the two curves overlap between about 20 and 50 g ha-1
  • A dose of 30-40 g ha-1 would kill most pests with limited bee deaths (compromise)
/ 3
05.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Spray at night / when bees are not flying
  • Spray when the crop is not in flower
  • Spray on still days to reduce spray drift
  • Use a systemic pesticide / seed coating rather than spraying
  • Use smaller droplets for more even coverage
/ 2
05.5 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Specificity is the extent to which the pesticide is toxic only to the target species / range of species affected
  • A highly specific pesticide has low toxicity to non-target species (eg bees, fish, mammals)
  • So fewer non-target deaths / less loss of predators, pollinators
  • Fewer indirect (food chain) effects / fewer inter-species relationships lost
  • Example: pyrethroids have high insect toxicity but low mammal toxicity
/ 3

Question 610 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 151-152 · BGC-03
[3 marks]06.1Explain why planting a new forest only sequesters carbon for a limited period, and how the storage can be extended.
[4 marks]06.2Compare pre-combustion and post-combustion methods of carbon capture.
[3 marks]06.3Describe two ways captured CO2 can be stored and give one concern about long-term storage.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Trees absorb CO2 by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood as they grow
  • Once mature there is no further net storage (photosynthesis balanced by respiration / decomposition), though the standing forest remains a reservoir
  • Harvesting the timber and keeping it as wood (eg house construction) then replanting produces additional storage as the new trees grow
/ 3
06.2 (4 marks)
  • Pre-combustion: the fuel is modified before use so no CO2 is released when burnt - eg gasification of coal to hydrogen and CO2 (CO2 stored, hydrogen distributed); oxy-fuel combustion in pure oxygen giving only CO2 and water vapour which is condensed out
  • Advantage: capture at a large central facility; suits small dispersed sources (vehicles) that cannot capture CO2
  • Post-combustion: CO2 removed from exhaust gases by solvents, membrane filtration, adsorption / desorption, cryogenic separation, graphene
  • Disadvantage: only about 20% of flue gas is CO2 so separation is costly / energy-intensive; none fully developed
/ 4
06.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Injected into depleted oil / gas fields or saline aquifers
  • Injected into oil reservoirs to provide pressure for secondary oil recovery
  • Reaction with fractured basalt to form solid carbonate minerals
  • Concern: leakage back to the atmosphere / monitoring over centuries; limited suitable geology; cost
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 151-152; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.

Question 810 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.3 · Genn pp. 234-235 · ECON-08
[2 marks]08.1Explain how integrated manufacture saves energy in the iron and steel industry.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why recycling is not always energy efficient.
[4 marks]08.3Explain how each of the following manages electricity infrastructure to conserve energy.
[2 marks]08.4Give two locational factors affecting where new generating infrastructure is built.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • Molten iron from the blast furnace is converted directly to steel on the same site
  • So it does not cool and need re-melting; transport energy between sites is also avoided; waste heat from one process used by another (eg Kalundborg)
/ 2
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Collecting and processing small dispersed quantities can use more energy in transport than is saved
  • Mass production and large-scale mining have economies of scale; some materials (glass) take more energy to make than plastic but can be refilled many times
/ 2
08.3 (4 marks)
  • Transmitting at high voltage means low current so less energy is lost as heat in the cables (loss proportional to current squared); overhead cables cheaper than underground which need cooling
  • ICT / smart grids co-ordinate real-time data on demand and supply from many sources, switching generation, storage (pumped storage, batteries) and flexible loads so surplus is not wasted and inefficient peaking plants are avoided
/ 4
08.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Proximity to the grid / demand to reduce transmission losses and cable costs
  • Access to cooling water for thermal stations
  • Resource availability (wind, tidal range, fuel deliveries)
  • Land availability and planning / public acceptance; stable geology

Question 910 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 212-214 · REN-07
[3 marks]09.1Explain three advantages of biofuels over other renewable energy resources.
[3 marks]09.2Explain three disadvantages of biofuel crops.
[2 marks]09.3Describe two new biofuel technologies.
[2 marks]09.4Explain how new low-temperature turbine technology could allow geothermal electricity generation in the UK.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Supply rate of energy crops can be controlled and fuels stored so supply matches demand (not intermittent)
  • Liquid biofuels (alcohol, biodiesel) can replace petrol / diesel in vehicles
  • Carbon neutral in principle: CO2 released equals CO2 absorbed in photosynthesis
  • Energy density of alcohol / biodiesel nearly as high as fossil fuels; wastes (straw, sewage, landfill gas) are cheap
/ 3
09.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Large areas of farmland compete with food production or cause clearance of wildlife habitats (eg palm oil)
  • Intensive farming inputs (fertiliser, pesticide, machinery fuel) may release nearly as much CO2 as using fossil fuels directly
  • Low energy density of straw, wood, Miscanthus makes transport uneconomic - must be used locally
  • Supply from wastes limited to the amount produced
/ 3
09.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Hydrogen from algae: some algae deprived of sulfur produce hydrogen during photosynthesis
  • Anaerobic digestion extended to crop wastes and marine algae; small-scale digesters (eg one cow's manure supplying a family's cooking gas)
  • Biofuels from microorganisms (eg oil-producing microalgae)
/ 2
09.4 (2 marks)
  • Conventional geothermal steam turbines need rocks above 150 °C (recent volcanic activity), too deep to reach economically in the UK
  • New turbines use fluids such as butane / pentane that boil at low temperatures, so water as cool as 60 °C from deep boreholes / hot dry rock systems can drive them
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 212-214; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 1010 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3 · Genn pp. 130-147 · MIN-10
[9 marks]10.1As high-grade mineral deposits become depleted, discuss the strategies that could be used to secure future supplies of metals.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by the stock (resource base) of a mineral.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Context: Lasky's principle - vast quantities exist at low grade; reserves vs resource; COOG affected by price, technology, energy costs
  • Improved exploration: higher resolution multispectral satellite sensors, IR spectroscopy, gravimetry, magnetometry, seismic, resistivity, portable field analysis, targeted trial drilling
  • Mechanisation allowing deeper underground mines and larger open-cast mines
  • Low-energy extraction of low-grade ores: bioleaching with acidophilic bacteria and fungi, phytomining, iron displacement, bacterial adsorption of rare earths, polymer adsorption from seawater
  • Previously inaccessible deposits: polymetallic nodules, deep sea sediments, Antarctica (currently protected) - environmental and legal issues
  • Extending use: recycling (advantages, difficulties), Cradle to Cradle design, product lifetime extension, substitution of scarce materials, circular economy
  • Evaluation: environmental impacts vs benefits; energy costs; economics; which strategies are most sustainable; conclusion
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • All of the material that exists in the lithosphere, including that which will never be technologically or economically exploitable
/ 1

[25 marks]11.1Discuss the strategies that can be used to provide sustainable supplies of water for a growing population.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Context: uneven distribution of water, growing population and per capita demand, unsustainable exploitation of rivers and aquifers, hydrological cycle links
  • New sources: rainwater collection, rivers (discharge, fluctuations, contaminants), reservoirs and site selection, estuary barrages, desalination, unexploited aquifers
  • Management: artificial aquifer recharge, river-regulation reservoirs, inter-basin transfers, afforestation
  • Conservation: low-water appliances, xeriscaping, drip irrigation, grey water, leak reduction, metering; pollution control to protect sources
  • Treatment: matching quality to use; screens, sedimentation, aeration, flocculation, filtration, sterilisation, pH adjustment, activated carbon, reverse osmosis
  • Evaluation of costs, energy, environmental impacts (habitat loss, methane, inter-tidal habitats, conflicts) and justified conclusion
