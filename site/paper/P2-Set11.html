The Isle of Eigg (area 31 km2, population about 100) is not connected to the national grid. Table 1 shows its electricity sources.

Table 1
SourceCapacity / kW

[3 marks]01.1Explain why Eigg uses a mix of three renewable resources rather than one, and why diesel back-up is still needed.
[3 marks]01.2Describe three other ways in which the Eigg community has increased its sustainability.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why small self-contained communities often find it easier to adopt sustainable lifestyles than large cities.

Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • Diversity: HEP (wet winters), solar (summer) and wind peak at different times so supply is more reliable - diverse systems resist change
  • No single source could meet demand all year; battery storage on the island grid smooths fluctuations
  • Calm, dry, dull periods still leave gaps so diesel generators provide security (fossil fuel use minimised, not yet eliminated)
/ 3
01.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Community ownership (Isle of Eigg Trust with Scottish Wildlife Trust) managing the island for people and wildlife
  • Wood biomass heating; insulation such as straw-bale housing; some electric vehicles
  • Habitat protection and creation: fencing rare plants from grazing, planting indigenous woodland (rowan, birch, willow); eagles and hen harriers nesting
  • Recyclables shipped off; garden / food waste composted; low-impact food production; beach litter collection
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Small groups can organise, agree and act quickly; decisions benefit people who see the results directly
  • Resources and wastes are local and visible so feedback is immediate, unlike cities supplied from far away
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Renewable energy is abundant but geographically and temporally variable; a continental grid moves electricity from areas of surplus (desert solar, offshore wind) to areas of demand
  • DC grids do not need every generator synchronised in phase as AC grids do, so coordination across many countries is easier

Question 310 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 52-55 · HAB-03
[4 marks]03.1Explain why tropical rainforests have such high biodiversity.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why rainforest species may be particularly vulnerable to environmental change.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why rainforest plants and animals such as frogs are a rich source of medicines.
[2 marks]03.4Explain the importance of tropical rainforest for the hydrological cycle and for soils.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Stable tropical climate over long periods gave time for species to evolve and adapt to local abiotic and biotic factors; many rare species with small ranges
  • High light, warm temperatures and high rainfall all year allow continuous photosynthesis and growth, supporting rich food webs with no seasonal food shortage (no hibernation / migration)
  • Stratification (emergent, canopy, understorey, shrub, ground layers) and epiphytes create many niches; intense competition for light drives specialisation
  • Biotic pressures (predation, inter-species relationships) drive evolution of specialised pollination / seed dispersal relationships; plants flower and fruit at different times
/ 4
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Adapted to stable conditions so they have narrow ranges of tolerance; a small change can move conditions outside the range
  • Many species are rare with small ranges / small populations
/ 2
03.3 (2 marks)
  • No cold winter kills herbivores so plants defend themselves with toxic chemicals (alkaloids) that are medicinally active in controlled doses (quinine, diosgenin, curare)
  • Animals without teeth / claws (frogs, reptiles) rely on chemical defences or venoms (fer-de-lance venom - captopril)
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Over half the Amazon's rainfall comes from transpiration within the forest; clearance reduces downwind rainfall
  • Rapid decay recycles nutrients so soils are shallow / poor; canopy and roots prevent erosion of the thin soil - clearance leads to rapid erosion and infertility
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 52-55; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Question 510 marks · Manipulation of food species & agricultural energetics · spec 3.5.1.2 · Genn pp. 326-328 · FS-04

Table 1 shows food conversion ratios (FCR) for livestock: the mass of feed needed to produce 1 kg of live mass gain.

Table 1
LivestockFCR / kg feed per kg gain
Farmed salmon1.2
Chicken1.8
Pork3.0
Beef (feedlot)7.0
Sheep8.0
[3 marks]05.1Use Table 1 to explain why intensive poultry and fish production convert feed to food more efficiently than beef.
[3 marks]05.2Explain three ways farmers reduce food chain energy losses in livestock production.
[4 marks]05.3Explain why growing crops for direct human consumption usually produces more food per hectare than rearing livestock, and describe one situation where livestock is the better option.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Chicken and salmon need far less feed per kg gained (1.2-1.8 vs 7.0)
  • Fish are ectotherms so no energy is used for thermoregulation and water supports their mass; poultry grow fast and are kept warm indoors with movement restricted
  • Cattle are large endotherms, use energy in movement, and lose energy in methane from cellulose digestion
/ 3
05.2 (3 marks)
  • Control of movement (housing / restricted grazing) so less energy is used in muscle activity
  • Temperature control (heated / insulated buildings) so less energy is used in maintaining body temperature
  • Species selection: choose species with low FCRs (poultry, fish); high-energy easily digested diets; slaughter at the fastest growth stage
/ 3
05.3 (4 marks)
  • Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next; most is lost as heat in respiration / metabolism or in undigested material
  • Humans eating plants (primary consumers) receive more of the energy captured by photosynthesis than eating animals fed on those plants (secondary consumers)
  • Livestock better where land cannot grow crops (upland, semi-arid, steep, thin soils): grazing animals with symbiotic gut bacteria digest cellulose in grass that humans cannot
  • Omnivores (pigs) convert food wastes (dairy by-products, catering waste) into meat
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 326-328; AQA spec 3.5.1.2.

Question 810 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 47-49 · CBR-08
[4 marks]08.1Describe coppicing and explain how it increases the biodiversity of a woodland.
[4 marks]08.2Explain how the reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park increased biodiversity.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why culling or removing species may be necessary in habitat management.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (4 marks)
  • Trees (hazel, oak) are cut to ground level every 3-30 years and allowed to regrow from the stump
  • About 0.5 ha cut each year on a rotation (eg 8 years) so the wood is a patchwork of areas of different ages
  • Each age has its own community: newly cut areas get high light for wildflowers and butterflies; older areas give dense cover for nesting birds / dormice
  • Pollarding cuts above browsing height so deer / livestock cannot eat the regrowth
/ 4
08.2 (4 marks)
  • Wolves (top predator, exterminated by 1926) reduced deer populations and changed their behaviour
  • Fewer young trees eaten so trees and riverside vegetation recovered
  • More trees allowed beaver populations to increase; beaver dams increased wetland area
  • Wetland species (birds, fish, invertebrates) became more common; overall biodiversity rose, though wolves cause problems for livestock farmers
/ 4
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Undesirable species (introduced predators / competitors, or natural succession species such as scrub, conifers in the New Forest heathland) reduce survival of the target species
  • Removal restores suitable conditions (eg heathland for sand lizards and smooth snakes) or reduces predation / competition
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 47-49; AQA spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4.
Question 910 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.1 · Genn pp. 338-339 · FISH-01

Figure 1 shows the seasonal changes in light intensity, nutrient concentration and phytoplankton biomass in the surface water of a temperate ocean in the northern hemisphere.

Figure 1
[4 marks]09.1Use Figure 1 to explain the spring peak and summer decline in phytoplankton biomass.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why tropical open oceans have low productivity all year despite high light and temperature.
[3 marks]09.3Explain why areas of upwelling and coastal waters are the most productive parts of the sea.
[1 mark]09.4Explain why phosphate is often the limiting nutrient in the sea.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (4 marks)
  • In winter nutrients are high (storms disturb sediments, deep mixing) but light is too low for photosynthesis
  • In spring light rises while nutrients are still high so growth is rapid - the spring bloom (peak about May)
  • The bloom uses up nutrients (falling to 10 by July); dead plankton sink out of the photic zone carrying nutrients to the seabed
  • So nutrients become limiting in summer despite high light; a smaller autumn bloom follows when storms return nutrients while light is still adequate
/ 4
09.2 (2 marks)
  • Warm surface water is stable (no seasonal mixing / storms) so nutrients lost to deep water in sinking dead organisms are not replaced
  • Nutrient (especially phosphate) concentrations in the photic zone remain very low - the limiting factor
/ 2
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Upwelling (offshore winds, seamounts, currents deflected upwards) brings cold nutrient-rich deep water into the photic zone
  • Coastal waters receive nutrients washed off the land in rivers and storms disturb shallow sediments
  • Nutrients plus light in the shallow photic zone allow high algal productivity supporting rich food webs and fisheries (eg Peru anchovy)
/ 3
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Phosphates have low solubility and no atmospheric reservoir; they are only added by river runoff or upwelling and are lost to sediments
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 338-339; AQA spec 3.5.2.1.
