Question 110 marks · Ionising radiation · spec 3.4.3.2.14 · Genn pp. 302-303 · RAD-03

Figure 1 shows the radiation dose rate measured at different distances from a small gamma source.

Figure 1
[2 marks]01.1Use Figure 1 to show that the dose rate follows the inverse square law.
[1 mark]01.2Calculate the dose rate at 20 m from the source.
[4 marks]01.3Explain how each of the following reduces the exposure of workers handling radioactive sources.
[2 marks]01.4Explain why potassium iodide tablets are issued to people near a nuclear accident.
[1 mark]01.5State the meaning of ALARA.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Doubling the distance from 1 m to 2 m reduces the dose rate from 400 to 100 (one quarter)
  • Distance x 10 (1 to 10 m) reduces it by 100 times (400 to 4); dose is proportional to 1/distance2
/ 2
01.2 (1 marks)
  • 400 / 202 = 1 µSv h-1
/ 1
01.3 (4 marks)
  • Sealed container prevents contact / contamination so exposure stops when the worker moves away; container may absorb alpha completely; remote handling

  • Dose received is proportional to time near the source so work is organised to minimise time close to it (job rotation)
  • Dosemeters / film badges record exposure; air monitors detect alpha-emitting particles; contamination monitors on leaving check that no material is carried out

  • So radioactive iodine-131 released is not taken up / concentrated in the thyroid gland
/ 2

Question 510 marks · Pesticides · spec 3.4.3.2.8 · Genn pp. 262-263 · PEST-01

Figure 1 shows how the percentage mortality of a pest species and of a non-target bee species change with the dose of an insecticide applied to a crop.

Figure 1
[1 mark]05.1Use Figure 1 to estimate the LD50 of the insecticide for the pest species.
[1 mark]05.2Define the term MDNF (maximum dose never fatal).
[3 marks]05.3Use Figure 1 to explain why it may not be possible to kill all of the pest species without harming bees.
[2 marks]05.4Suggest how the farmer could reduce the harm to bees without reducing the dose applied.
[3 marks]05.5Explain why a pesticide with high specificity is less likely to cause environmental damage.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • 25 g ha-1 (accept 24-27)
/ 1
05.2 (1 marks)
  • The highest dose that can be applied without killing any members of the population / any individuals of the (non-target) species
/ 1
05.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • The MDAF (minimum dose always fatal) of the pest is about 50 g ha-1
  • This is higher than the MDNF of the bee (about 20 g ha-1)
  • So a dose that kills all pests kills some bees / the two curves overlap between about 20 and 50 g ha-1
  • A dose of 30-40 g ha-1 would kill most pests with limited bee deaths (compromise)
/ 3
05.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Spray at night / when bees are not flying
  • Spray when the crop is not in flower
  • Spray on still days to reduce spray drift
  • Use a systemic pesticide / seed coating rather than spraying
  • Use smaller droplets for more even coverage
/ 2
05.5 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Specificity is the extent to which the pesticide is toxic only to the target species / range of species affected
  • A highly specific pesticide has low toxicity to non-target species (eg bees, fish, mammals)
  • So fewer non-target deaths / less loss of predators, pollinators
  • Fewer indirect (food chain) effects / fewer inter-species relationships lost
  • Example: pyrethroids have high insect toxicity but low mammal toxicity
/ 3

Question 610 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.1 · Genn pp. 245-247 · AIR-02
[3 marks]06.1Describe how an electrostatic precipitator removes smoke particles from the effluent gases of a coal-fired power station.
[2 marks]06.2Explain how a cyclone separator works.
[1 mark]06.3Explain why heating coal to remove tar reduces smoke emissions.
[2 marks]06.4Explain why turbochargers reduce the smoke produced by diesel engines.
[2 marks]06.5Explain why smaller smoke particles (PM1) are a greater health risk than larger ones.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Effluent gases pass through a chamber containing electrically charged wires / plates
  • Smoke particles become (negatively) charged and are attracted to the oppositely charged plates where they collect / stick
  • Plates are shaken and the particles fall as fly ash which is collected (and may be used, eg in building materials)
/ 3
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Effluent gases are forced to rotate / spin in a cylindrical chamber
  • The (denser) particles are thrown outwards to the chamber walls where they fall and collect; cleaned gases leave through a central pipe
/ 2
06.3 (1 marks)
  • The tar / volatile organic compounds that produce smoke on incomplete combustion are driven off, producing smokeless coal / coke
/ 1
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Turbochargers deliver more air / oxygen to the combustion chamber
  • So combustion is more complete and fewer unburnt carbon particles / soot are produced (especially during acceleration)
/ 2
06.5 (2 marks)
  • Smaller particles remain suspended in the atmosphere for longer so they are more likely to be inhaled
  • They penetrate deeper into the lungs / alveoli and are not cleared; may carry toxic / carcinogenic chemicals
/ 2

[4 marks]08.1Describe and explain the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why the atmosphere is described as being in a state of dynamic equilibrium.
[2 marks]08.3Give two ways in which the atmosphere supports life other than by providing gases for biological molecules.
[2 marks]08.4Name the two layers of the atmosphere most affected by human activities.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (4 marks)
  • CO2 rises from about 412 ppm in January to a peak of 416 ppm in May, falls to a minimum of 407 ppm in September, then rises again
  • During spring / summer, photosynthesis by plants (growing season) removes CO2 faster than respiration releases it, so the concentration falls
  • In autumn / winter photosynthesis is reduced (leaf fall) but respiration and decomposition continue, so CO2 rises
  • The atmosphere is in dynamic equilibrium: the concentration fluctuates around a mean; more land / vegetation in the northern hemisphere makes the cycle large
/ 4
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Natural processes that add gases (respiration, combustion, volcanoes, denitrification) are balanced by processes that remove them (photosynthesis, dissolving, nitrogen fixation)
  • So the average composition stays the same over long timescales although the rates vary and concentrations fluctuate around a mean
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Absorbs harmful UV / solar wind radiation in the stratosphere (ozone layer)
  • Greenhouse gases delay the escape of infrared, keeping the surface warm (33 °C warmer than without)
  • Atmospheric pressure allows liquid water to exist
  • Distributes heat from the tropics to higher latitudes by winds
  • Transports water vapour to areas that would otherwise be dry

08.4 (2 marks)
  • Troposphere
  • Stratosphere

Question 1010 marks · Fluctuations in supply/demand & energy storage · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 217-224 · EST-06
[9 marks]10.1Explain why the development of energy storage technologies is essential if renewable energy resources are to provide most of a country's electricity.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by peak shaving.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Renewables are intermittent (solar at night, calm days, slack tides) and some unpredictable; demand fluctuates daily, weekly, seasonally and short-term; supply and demand rarely match
  • Without storage, surplus is wasted and shortages must be met by fossil-fuel peaking plants; large-scale electricity storage not yet possible directly so energy must be converted
  • Pumped-storage HEP: proven, rapid response, peak shaving, but limited sites
  • Batteries: lithium-ion for grid and vehicles; factors affecting viability; V2G
  • Hydrogen: electrolysis, storage methods, fuel cells, hydrogen economy, P2G into the gas grid; high energy density enables vehicle use
  • Compressed air with heat recovery; thermal storage (molten salt for CSP, high-volume stores, thermal mass); flywheels; supercapacitors
  • Grid management / ICT and diversity of resources (wind + solar + tidal + biofuel) reduce but do not remove the need for storage; energy conservation reduces peaks
  • Evaluation of efficiency losses, costs, resource limits (lithium), and which technologies suit short-term vs inter-seasonal storage; judgement
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Storing surplus energy at times of low demand / high supply to meet later peaks in demand, so peak generating capacity can be reduced
/ 1

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Explain how feedback mechanisms and tipping points influence the rate of global climate change and discuss the implications for the strategies needed to control it.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Negative feedback: increased low-level cloud (albedo), increased photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle evaporation-precipitation
  • Positive feedback: melting permafrost (methane), reduced albedo of snow / ice / sea ice, methane hydrate release, ocean acidification and coral loss, forest and peat fires, soil decomposition, water vapour, cirrus clouds
  • Tipping points: self-sustaining processes; why control must precede them; examples
  • Implications: urgency and precautionary principle; need for deep cuts in greenhouse gases (energy, transport, buildings, agriculture, forestry, CCS) rather than only adaptation; geoengineering risks
  • Difficulty of prediction because feedbacks interact at different scales; models; international agreements and per capita footprints
  • Judgement on whether current strategies are adequate
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
