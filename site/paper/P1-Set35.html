Question 210 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.1 · Genn pp. 158-161 · SOIL-02
[6 marks]02.1Explain the roles of three named groups of soil organisms in maintaining soil fertility.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why a soil pH between 5.5 and 7.0 is best for fertility.
[2 marks]02.3Explain how soil compaction by heavy machinery reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (6 marks)
  • Detritivores (worms, beetle larvae, millipedes, woodlice) physically break up dead organic matter, increasing its surface area, and release nutrients; worms create tunnels improving drainage and aeration
  • Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) secrete enzymes that break down dead organic matter, releasing nutrient ions (ammonification) and forming humus which binds particles into crumb peds
  • Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas to ammonium ions; nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite then nitrate for root absorption
  • Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with roots increasing phosphate uptake
/ 6
02.2 (2 marks)
  • This is the range of tolerance of most plants and soil biota; acidic soils leach nutrients and mobilise toxic aluminium, damaging root membranes
  • In alkaline soils phosphates become insoluble / unavailable
/ 2
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Interstitial spaces are reduced so aeration falls (aerobic processes such as nitrification and decomposition slow) and roots cannot penetrate
  • Infiltration falls so waterlogging is more likely and runoff / erosion increase
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 158-161; AQA spec 3.2.5.1.

Question 410 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.7 · Genn pp. 143-145 · MIN-07
[5 marks]04.1Describe how bioleaching is used to extract copper from low-grade ores or spoil heaps, and explain why it may be more sustainable than smelting.
[3 marks]04.2Describe the process of phytomining.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how polymer adsorption may provide a low-energy source of uranium in the future.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (5 marks)
  • Acidophilic bacteria (eg Acidithiobacillus) oxidise sulfide ores, producing sulfuric acid which dissolves the metal ions
  • Leachate (rainwater / recirculated solution) percolating through the spoil heap is collected; recirculation increases the metal concentration
  • Copper is recovered from solution by electrolysis or by displacement with scrap iron (iron is more reactive so copper is deposited as solid metal)
  • Smelting needs chemical reduction at high temperatures; energy needed rises rapidly as ore grade falls, so low-grade ores are uneconomic
  • Bioleaching works at low temperatures with low energy inputs, so it can exploit low-grade deposits and wastes (increasing reserves) - mimics natural low-temperature processes
/ 5
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Plants (hyperaccumulators) absorb metal ions from soil / water and concentrate them in their leaves
  • The vegetation is harvested and incinerated
  • The concentrated metal in the ash is dissolved in acid and separated by electrolysis; also decontaminates polluted sites
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Uranium ions dissolved in seawater adsorb onto the surface of synthetic or natural polymers (lignin from wood, chitin from shrimp shells)
  • The polymers are collected and the uranium recovered, avoiding mining / high-energy extraction
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 143-145; AQA spec 3.2.3.7.

Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • 7 x 1011 tonnes
/ 1
05.2 (2 marks)
  • 10% of 10 000 = 1000 billion tonnes
  • 1000 / 700 x 100 = 143% (accept 142.9)
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Carbon is transferred into and out of rocks extremely slowly (fossilisation / sedimentation, weathering, volcanic release)
  • Residence time is millions of years so the flux to the atmosphere is tiny compared with respiration / photosynthesis / combustion
/ 2
05.4 (3 marks)
  • CO2 dissolves in the sea (forming carbonic acid / hydrogen carbonate ions) and is absorbed by phytoplankton through photosynthesis
  • Organisms such as molluscs, corals and planktonic organisms build calcium carbonate shells / exoskeletons
  • When they die the shells sink and sediment on the seabed, becoming limestone / chalk over long periods
/ 3
05.5 (2 marks)
  • Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break down organic compounds completely to CO2 (and water), releasing much more energy
  • Without oxygen, anaerobic organisms only partly break down organic compounds, releasing carbon as methane (CH4), which is later oxidised to CO2 in the atmosphere
/ 2

Question 1010 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3, 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 108-113, 105-106 · OZ-04
[9 marks]10.1Ozone depletion is often described as an environmental problem that has been tackled successfully. Compare the effectiveness of the methods used to restore the ozone layer with the effectiveness of attempts to tackle global climate change, and explain the reasons for the difference.
[1 mark]10.2Name the international agreement of 1985 that preceded the Montreal Protocol and state its main weakness.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Stages of scientific method illustrated by ozone: identification (Antarctic UV data), hypothesis (Rowland-Molina 1974), data collection / analysis (Dobson units, satellites, balloons confirming ClO), proposed solutions, enactment (Vienna 1985, Montreal 1987)
  • Evidence of success: ozone hole area and depth stabilised and beginning to shrink; CFC concentrations in the atmosphere falling; UV levels not rising further; global ozone loss limited to about 4%
  • Reasons for success: international recognition of serious consequences (skin cancer); agreement of nearly every country; legally binding targets with a fund for developing countries; relatively few producers / products; alternatives (HCFCs, HFCs, pump sprays, hydrocarbons) already available and cheap; no lifestyle change needed
  • Limits: persistence of CFCs means recovery takes decades; some ODSs still used; HFC replacements are greenhouse gases
  • Climate change contrast: greenhouse gases come from almost every human activity (energy, transport, agriculture, industry) in every country; fossil fuels underpin economies so control requires major lifestyle and economic change
  • Agreements (Kyoto, Paris) have weaker / non-binding targets, disputes between developed and developing nations; emissions still rising; natural fluctuations, time delays and tipping points make cause-effect less clear to the public
  • Judgement: ozone control was a technical substitution problem; climate change is a systemic one, so the ozone success is only partly transferable
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer - no legally binding goals
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-113, 105-106; AQA spec 3.2.1.3, 3.2.1.2.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Explain how feedback mechanisms and tipping points influence the rate of global climate change and discuss the implications for the strategies needed to control it.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Negative feedback: increased low-level cloud (albedo), increased photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle evaporation-precipitation
  • Positive feedback: melting permafrost (methane), reduced albedo of snow / ice / sea ice, methane hydrate release, ocean acidification and coral loss, forest and peat fires, soil decomposition, water vapour, cirrus clouds
  • Tipping points: self-sustaining processes; why control must precede them; examples
  • Implications: urgency and precautionary principle; need for deep cuts in greenhouse gases (energy, transport, buildings, agriculture, forestry, CCS) rather than only adaptation; geoengineering risks
  • Difficulty of prediction because feedbacks interact at different scales; models; international agreements and per capita footprints
  • Judgement on whether current strategies are adequate
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 103-107, 371-372; spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1.
