Question 110 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 310-312 · AGR-03

Table 1 compares organic and inorganic fertilisers.

[6 marks]01.1Complete Table 1 by giving two advantages and two disadvantages of each type of fertiliser.
Table 1
FertiliserAdvantagesDisadvantages
Organic (manure, sewage sludge, compost)
Inorganic (eg ammonium nitrate, NPK)
[4 marks]01.2Explain how hydroponic cultivation maximises productivity and give two disadvantages.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (6 marks)
  • Organic advantages: often waste products, locally available and cheap; add humus improving structure and water retention; increase soil biota; slow release reduces leaching
  • Organic disadvantages: nutrient composition cannot be controlled; released slowly so needs long-term planning; bulky with high water content so expensive to transport; cannot be applied to a growing crop; odour / pathogens
  • Inorganic advantages: composition controlled to match crop needs; nutrients released rapidly; concentrated and easy to apply
  • Inorganic disadvantages: high energy (fossil fuel) manufacture (Haber process); no organic matter so humus and biota decline; some toxic to worms; high solubility so leached causing eutrophication; raw materials (phosphate rock) finite
/ 6
01.2 (4 marks)
  • Crops grown in a circulating nutrient solution (inert medium) in greenhouses; nutrient concentrations monitored and adjusted so nutrients are never limiting; abiotic factors controlled
  • Roots are small (all in contact with nutrients) so more growth goes into the harvested crop; no soil-borne pathogens or weeds; crop stays fresh with roots
  • Disadvantages: high inputs of nutrients and energy (heating, lighting, pumping); high level of technical knowledge; only economic for high-value crops
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 310-312; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.

[6 marks]02.1Use Table 1 to explain why the plantation has a much higher timber yield but far lower biodiversity.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why commercial plantations are felled before the trees reach full size.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why there is little demand today for coppiced or pollarded wood.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (6 marks)
  • Non-indigenous species (Sitka spruce from North America) chosen for fast growth and pest resistance; indigenous wildlife's needs for food, pollination, seed dispersal and habitat are not met
  • Single species: easy planting, thinning and felling over large areas; but each tree species supports its own community so few species overall
  • Close planting makes trees compete for light so they grow tall and straight (good timber) but shades out ground flora and the species that depend on them
  • Simple age structure: uniform size makes management easy but gives little variety of light, temperature, wind; no old trees or dead wood for invertebrates and woodpeckers
  • Mixed woodland: varied niches, stratification, dead wood, indigenous trees each with their own dependent species
/ 6
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Trees are felled when income will be greatest - approaching full size when the growth rate starts to decline
  • Continuing to grow them ties up land and capital for little extra timber; natural forests keep old and dead trees
/ 2
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Industrial society replaced species-specific uses (baskets, fencing, charcoal, tool handles) with large-scale uses: chipboard, plywood, pulp
  • Wood fuel replaced by fossil fuels / electricity; so traditional management declined and woods became uniform
/ 2

[3 marks]03.1Explain why commercial crop varieties often lack the characteristics shown in Table 1.
[3 marks]03.2Explain what is meant by a Vavilov centre (centre of diversity) and why these areas are conservation priorities.
[3 marks]03.3Explain why a large population does not necessarily have a large gene pool, and why a small gene pool is a problem.
[1 mark]03.4Give one wild plant species with potential as a new commercial food crop and the characteristic that makes it valuable.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • Domesticated crops were bred from a small number of original plants so they have a small gene pool / low genetic diversity
  • Selective breeding for yield, appearance or shelf life may have lost other useful genes (eg taste, disease resistance)
  • Genetically similar plants share the same susceptibilities so a new disease or environmental change can affect the whole crop
/ 3
03.2 (3 marks)
  • A region with a high concentration of the wild relatives of important crops - usually where the crop was first domesticated (eg wheat / barley in the Middle East; potato, tomato in South America; rice in India; maize in Central America)
  • They hold the largest gene pools / most genetic variety for future crop breeding (disease resistance, drought and salt tolerance, yield)
  • Many are in regions where environmental degradation / habitat loss threatens the wild plants
/ 3
03.3 (3 marks)
  • A large population may all descend from a few closely related individuals so they are genetically similar
  • Similar adaptations mean all are susceptible to the same change (disease, climate)
  • Greater risk of inbreeding so disadvantageous recessive genes are expressed (eg amelanism, poor muscle development in lion cubs); so species should be protected across their whole range to retain regional genes
/ 3
03.4 (1 marks)
  • Morama bean (drought resistant, soya-like protein); yeheb tree (drought resistant nuts, poor soils); potato bean (high protein); perennial maize / Kernza (no annual replanting, less ploughing / erosion); salt-tolerant spinach relatives
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 15-17; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.2.
Question 410 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 356-359 · AQUA-03
[8 marks]04.1Explain how each of the following environmental impacts of salmon farming arises and how it can be reduced.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why culling seals and cormorants near fish farms is controversial and give an alternative.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (8 marks)
  • Carnivores fed pellets of sandeels, herring, anchovy; overfishing sandeels reduces puffins and terns; reduced by more plant protein / vegetable oil in pellets or farming herbivores (tilapia)
  • High density spreads lice (which spread to wild fish) and infections; pesticides (organophosphates, pyrethroids, hydrogen peroxide) kill wildlife; antibiotics breed resistant bacteria; reduced by lower densities, single-age tanks with flow from young to old, wrasse as biological control, warm-water washes, circulating flow
  • Escaped farmed fish breed with wild fish introducing disadvantageous genes (bright colour), or non-indigenous escapees colonise as predators / competitors; reduced by better cage design, sterile triploid fish (pressure-treated eggs), species that cannot breed locally (Japanese oysters in UK)
  • Faeces and uneaten food decompose causing deoxygenation under cages; reduced by monitored feeding, siting cages in strong currents, effluent treatment / reed beds for freshwater farms, IMTA with seaweed and shellfish absorbing wastes
/ 8
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Predators are killed under licence to protect stock, reducing wild populations of protected / valued species
  • Better cage designs, netting, fencing, bird scarers exclude predators without killing
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 356-359; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.

Question 610 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.4, 3.1.3.1 · Genn pp. 43-46 · CBR-06
[6 marks]06.1Describe how each of the following abiotic factors can be managed to increase biodiversity in a nature reserve.
[4 marks]06.2Explain how biotic factors can be managed to increase the survival of a species. Give four examples.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (6 marks)
  • Light: selective felling to create clearings for plants needing high light while retaining dense canopy for shade species; clearings also give warm sand / soil for lizard eggs; leave dead wood; woodland floor plants (bluebells) grow before the canopy leafs
  • Dissolved oxygen: narrowing a slow river increases flow and turbulence so more oxygen dissolves, suiting trout and mayfly larvae; shallow water warms quickly for egg development
  • Nutrients: removing fertile topsoil / not fertilising allows plants of nutrient-poor soils (eg sundew on acidic nitrate-deficient soils) that cannot compete on fertile ground; dung patches add local nutrients
/ 6
06.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Food: wildflower grasslands for seed-eating birds; providing food species
  • Control of predation: island nest sites unreachable by foxes; trapping / culling / eradicating introduced rats (Scilly Isles - Manx shearwater and storm petrel breeding success increased)
  • Control of competitors: removing rhododendron so wildflowers get light
  • Pollination: a range of flowering plants with different flowering times so pollinators have food all season
  • Seed dispersal species (hippos, elephants, monkeys) conserved; control of introduced pathogens
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 43-46; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4, 3.1.3.1.

Table 1 shows some events in the history of life on Earth.

Table 1

First life (Archaea) on Earth3.5
First photosynthetic organisms2.7
Oxygen concentration in the atmosphere starts to rise2.45
End of the period of oxygen build-up (Proterozoic)0.54
[1 mark]09.1Use Table 1 to calculate how many million years passed between the appearance of photosynthetic organisms and the start of the rise in atmospheric oxygen.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why atmospheric oxygen did not start to rise as soon as photosynthesis began.
[3 marks]09.3Explain how the rise in atmospheric oxygen made life on the land surface possible.
[2 marks]09.4Explain how early photosynthetic organisms helped to prevent a long-term rise in the Earth's temperature.
[2 marks]09.5Give two other ways in which living organisms changed the conditions on Earth.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (1 marks)
  • 2.7 - 2.45 = 0.25 billion = 250 million years
/ 1
09.2 (2 marks)
  • For millions of years all the oxygen produced reacted with (dissolved) iron in the oceans
  • Only when all the iron had reacted did surplus dissolved oxygen build up in the oceans and then escape into the atmosphere
/ 2
09.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Oxygen in the atmosphere absorbed ultraviolet light
  • A dynamic equilibrium of reactions between O, O2 and O3 (the ozone layer) developed
  • Less UV reached the surface so organisms were no longer restricted to water (which had absorbed the UV)
  • Aerobic respiration became possible so more complex / larger organisms (plants and animals) evolved; many anaerobic Archaea and bacteria died out
/ 3
09.4 (2 marks)
  • Photoautotrophs absorbed carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, and some of the carbon was stored in geological sediments such as carbonate rocks and fossil fuels (carbon sequestration)
  • The lower CO2 concentration reduced the retention of heat, offsetting the increase in the brightness of the Sun (about 10% per billion years)
/ 2
09.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Biogeochemical cycles developed so small amounts of nutrient elements could support life for long periods without depletion
  • Transpiration by land plants returned water vapour to the atmosphere and increased rainfall in other areas
  • Regulation of atmospheric composition (eg oxygen / CO2 by photosynthesis and respiration)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 6-8; AQA spec 3.1.1.2.

Indicative content
