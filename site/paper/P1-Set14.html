01.1 (6 marks)

[5 marks]02.1Complete Table 1 to explain how each method reduces soil erosion.
Table 1

[2 marks]02.2Explain why long-term crops such as tea, coffee or orchards reduce erosion compared with annual arable crops.
[3 marks]02.3Describe three ways livestock can be managed to reduce soil erosion.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (5 marks)
  • Terracing: sloping land divided into narrow level fields held by retaining walls along contours; water slows as it crosses each terrace and infiltrates instead of running off
  • Tied ridging: on nearly flat land a criss-cross of intersecting ridges retains rainwater, increasing infiltration and preventing runoff
  • Windbreaks: hedgerows / tree rows reduce wind velocity (for up to 30 times their height) so the wind has less kinetic energy to carry particles; protect bare fields after harvest
  • Mulching: organic matter on the surface protects soil from raindrop impact and wind; decomposes to humus which binds particles; retains moisture
  • Strip cropping: strips of different crops harvested at different times (at 90° to the wind) so there is always a crop protecting part of the field; slows runoff
/ 5
02.2 (2 marks)
  • No frequent replanting / soil disturbance (ploughing) that breaks up soil structure and exposes particles
  • Permanent root systems and canopy cover protect the soil all year
/ 2
02.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Control stocking density so vegetation regrows faster than it is eaten
  • Restrict access to vulnerable areas such as river banks (fencing)
  • Move / remove livestock at high-risk times, eg after heavy rain when soil is wet
  • Rotational grazing to rest pastures
/ 3

[1 mark]04.1Define the term half-life.

Show mark scheme
04.1 (1 marks)
  • The time taken for half of the atoms of a radioactive isotope to decay (activity halves)
/ 1
04.2 (2 marks)
  • 32 / 8 = 4 half-lives
  • 6400 / 24 = 400 Bq
/ 2
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Short half-life isotopes (I-131) release all their radiation quickly: dangerous but only briefly so short-term precautions (potassium iodide tablets, evacuation) are sufficient
  • Very long half-life isotopes (U-238) emit only small amounts of radiation in any period so the level of danger is low
  • Decades-long half-lives combine a high rate of radiation release with persistence for most or all of a human lifetime, so short-term protection is inadequate (land contaminated for generations)
/ 3
04.4 (3 marks)
  • Alpha particles have very low penetrating power and are absorbed by clothing / dead skin so external sources cause little exposure
  • Inside the body the alpha particles are absorbed in a very small mass of tissue so damage is concentrated (high RBE - weighting factor 20)
  • Long half-life means it continues to emit for the rest of the person's life / cannot be excreted quickly (contamination not just exposure)
/ 3
04.5 (1 marks)
  • A previously stable nucleus that becomes radioactive after absorbing a neutron (eg in reactor structures), later decaying to emit ionising radiation
/ 1

Question 510 marks · The hydrosphere: hydrological cycle & unsustainable exploitation · spec 3.2.2.1-3.2.2.2 · Genn pp. 119-121 · HYD-05
[3 marks]05.1Explain why abstraction from an aquifer containing 'fossil' (palaeo) water, such as the High Plains / Ogallala Aquifer in the USA, cannot be sustainable.
[3 marks]05.2Describe how the GRACE satellites are used to monitor aquifer depletion and give one advantage over monitoring boreholes.
[3 marks]05.3Describe three measures being introduced on the North China Plain to reduce aquifer depletion.
[1 mark]05.4Suggest why large-scale afforestation on the North China Plain may reduce aquifer recharge even though trees reduce soil erosion.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • The water was recharged during or before the last ice age (10 000-20 000 years ago) when the climate was wetter
  • Current recharge is little or none so any abstraction reduces the stored volume
  • The large volume means shortages take a long time to appear, but eventually the supply runs out; ancient water is often saline, causing soil salinisation
/ 3
05.2 (3 marks)
  • Two satellites whose orbits are affected by the force of gravity
  • The mass of water in aquifers below changes gravity, so changes in the orbit / distance between the satellites indicate changes in groundwater mass
  • Advantage: the whole Earth is surveyed every 30 days, whereas borehole water-table measurements are slow and hard to coordinate over large areas
/ 3
05.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Low-pressure irrigation pipes to reduce leaks
  • Growing crops that need less water than wheat / maize
  • Charging consumers by volume used (metering)
  • Inter-basin transfer of water 450 km from the Yangtze
/ 3
05.4 (1 marks)
  • Trees intercept rainfall and transpire large volumes, reducing soil moisture and the water percolating down to the aquifer
/ 1

Question 710 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.1 · Genn pp. 225-227 · ECON-02
[6 marks]07.1Explain how each of the following features of vehicle design reduces energy use.
[2 marks]07.2Explain why solid steel wheels on rails lose less energy than pneumatic tyres on roads.
[2 marks]07.3Describe two ways combustion efficiency in an internal combustion engine can be improved.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Aerodynamic shapes / fairings reduce friction with the air (air resistance) so less energy is needed to propel the vehicle, especially at speed; hydrodynamics for ships
  • Reduced mass (plastics, carbon-fibre composites, high-strength steel alloys, aluminium engine blocks, lighter batteries and magnets, rounded shapes) means less energy to accelerate and less rolling resistance, so lower fuel consumption
  • Conventional friction brakes convert kinetic energy to waste heat; regenerative braking uses a generator to convert it to electricity stored in a battery (or returned to the rail grid) which then propels the vehicle, so less fuel is needed to accelerate again
/ 6
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Pneumatic tyres deform as they rotate; the continual change in shape and air movement inside generates frictional heat (more if under-inflated)
  • Steel wheels do not deform so rolling resistance is very low
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • More valves per cylinder to remove exhaust gases fully so they do not mix with fresh fuel
  • Better temperature control (thermostatic fan)
  • Electronic ignition control / regular servicing to keep spark timing correct
  • Turbochargers supplying more oxygen
/ 2

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss how knowledge of the properties of pollutants can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: state of matter, density, persistence, toxicity, specificity, reactivity, adsorption, solubility, bioaccumulation / biomagnification, synergism, mutagenic / carcinogenic / teratogenic action, mobility
  • How properties predict behaviour: dispersal (gases vs solids, density - Bhopal), how far and how long (persistence, mobility), where they concentrate (adsorption on sediments, food chains)
  • Environmental features interacting with properties: temperature inversions, currents, pH, oxygen, light, adsorbent materials
  • Control based on properties: persistent / liposoluble pesticides banned (organochlorines) and replaced by non-persistent / specific ones; systemic pesticides and seed coatings
  • Heavy metals: lead uses discontinued, storage at high pH to reduce solubility; mercury chemical form; radioactive waste categorised by half-life / activity and contained (encapsulation, sealed storage)
  • Reactive primary pollutants controlled to prevent secondary pollutants (catalytic converters, FGD, urea sprays); oil properties (floats, insoluble) exploited by booms and skimmers; density in cyclone separators
  • Principles: CPA and CGM based on properties; emission location / timing; ALARA and BATNEEC; polluter pays; precautionary principle
  • New technologies: satellite oil-spill monitoring, GPS, polymer adsorption, phytoremediation, bioremediation
  • Evaluation: prevention better than cure; limits of remediation; cost-effectiveness; contrast success of ozone (Montreal) with climate change; judgement
