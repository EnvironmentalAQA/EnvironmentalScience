Question 110 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.3 · Genn pp. 245-253 · AIR-08

Table 1 shows the annual emissions of two pollutants in the UK in 1970 and 2015.

Table 1
PollutantEmissions in 1970 / thousand tonnesEmissions in 2015 / thousand tonnesMain source in 2015
Sulfur dioxide6 400220Energy industries
Nitrogen oxides2 700930Road transport
[2 marks]01.1Calculate the percentage reduction in sulfur dioxide emissions between 1970 and 2015.
[3 marks]01.2Suggest three reasons why sulfur dioxide emissions fell by a much larger percentage than nitrogen oxide emissions.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why a reduction in NOx emissions would also reduce concentrations of tropospheric ozone and PANs.
[3 marks]01.4Explain why lichens are used to monitor sulfur dioxide pollution.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • (6400 - 220) / 6400 x 100
  • = 96.6% (accept 97%)
/ 2
01.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Switch from coal to natural gas (which is desulfurised) / closure of coal-fired power stations removed the main SO2 source
  • Flue gas desulfurisation fitted to remaining coal plants; coal / oil desulfurisation
  • NOx comes mainly from road transport and vehicle numbers / distance travelled increased greatly
  • NOx is produced from air (nitrogen + oxygen) in any high-temperature combustion, so cannot be removed from the fuel like sulfur
  • Catalytic converters only work when warm / diesel engines produce more NOx / older vehicles remained in use
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Ozone and PANs are secondary pollutants formed by photochemical reactions involving NO2 (broken down by UV to monatomic oxygen which forms ozone)
  • If the primary pollutant is reduced the reactants are not present so the secondary pollutants cannot form
/ 2
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Lichens are very sensitive to acidic conditions / SO2 and different species have different tolerances
  • The species present, their abundance, size / health can be used as a biotic index of the pollution level
  • They give a long-term integrated measure of pollution rather than a single instantaneous reading; cheap, no equipment needed

Question 210 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.1 · Genn pp. 227-228 · ECON-03

[4 marks]02.2Describe four principles of designing vehicles for end of life.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why there is an optimum speed for fuel efficiency in a car.
[1 mark]02.4Give one way driver behaviour or vehicle systems can reduce fuel use in congested traffic.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • The energy used to extract raw materials, manufacture the components and assemble the vehicle (for a typical car about 1.5 years of its fuel use)
  • Reduced by using recycled materials (eg recycled aluminium needs 1/20 of the energy of new)
  • Lighter designs using less material; longer vehicle life spreading the embodied energy
/ 3
02.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Use of recyclable materials wherever possible
  • Easy identification of components and their composition (code stamps)
  • Easy dismantling and separation of components / materials
  • Reusable components for new vehicles
  • Compostable materials for parts that cannot be recycled
/ 4
02.3 (2 marks)
  • At low speed a large proportion of the energy just runs the engine rather than moving the car
  • At high speed energy is wasted overcoming air resistance (which rises with the square of speed); optimum for many cars about 56 mph
/ 2
02.4 (1 marks)
  • Automatic stop / start systems cutting the engine when stationary; smooth driving in higher gears avoiding sudden braking / acceleration

Question 310 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 394-395, 401-402 · RM-05

A student used 0.5 m x 0.5 m grid quadrats to survey ground flora in two meadows, one grazed and one ungrazed. Table 1 shows the results for the grazed meadow (10 quadrats).

Table 1
SpeciesNumber of individuals
Ribwort plantain48
Perennial ryegrass120
White clover36
Daisy24
Creeping buttercup12
[2 marks]03.1Explain why a 0.5 m x 0.5 m quadrat was chosen rather than a 10 cm x 10 cm or 100 m x 100 m quadrat.
[3 marks]03.2Calculate Simpson's Index of Biodiversity (D) for the grazed meadow using D = N(N-1) / Σn(n-1). Show your working.
[2 marks]03.3The ungrazed meadow had D = 1.8 with the same number of species. Explain what this shows.
[2 marks]03.4Explain why a point quadrat would be better than a frame quadrat in tall vegetation.
[1 mark]03.5Give one limitation of using the DAFOR abundance scale.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Quadrat size depends on the size / spacing of the organisms: 0.5 m x 0.5 m is typical for ground flora
  • 10 cm x 10 cm is for lichens (too small to contain a representative sample of plants); 100 m x 100 m is for trees (impractical, too much work)
/ 2
03.2 (3 marks)
  • N = 240; N(N-1) = 240 x 239 = 57 360
  • Σn(n-1) = 48x47 + 120x119 + 36x35 + 24x23 + 12x11 = 2256 + 14280 + 1260 + 552 + 132 = 18 480
  • D = 57 360 / 18 480 = 3.10 (accept 3.1)
/ 3
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Lower D means lower biodiversity: the ungrazed meadow is dominated by one or a few species (eg tall grass) with the others rare (less even)
  • Grazing (plagioclimax management) prevents dominance by vigorous species so abundance is more evenly spread
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • A frame quadrat flattens tall plants, making percentage cover estimates inaccurate and hiding shorter plants
  • Point quadrat pins are lowered through the vegetation; each touch counts (10 pins = 10% each) without flattening
/ 2
03.5 (1 marks)
  • Subjective judgement varies between recorders; results are not quantitative so statistical analysis is not possible (but quick for many areas)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 394-395, 401-402; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 410 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.2.2.3 · Genn pp. 96-97 · CC-04
[2 marks]04.1Describe the two processes that drive the North Atlantic Conveyor.
[4 marks]04.2Explain how global climate change could weaken the North Atlantic Conveyor and describe the possible consequence for the climate of the UK.
[3 marks]04.3Explain why the effects of climate change on UK temperatures are difficult to predict.
[1 mark]04.4Describe how deep ocean currents are monitored.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • Friction with the prevailing south-westerly winds drags surface water north-eastwards from the tropical Atlantic
  • Water in the NE Atlantic cools, becomes denser (and more saline as ice forms) and sinks, drawing warm surface water in to replace it; the cold water returns southwards at depth (thermohaline circulation)
/ 2
04.2 (4 marks)
  • Higher temperatures melt land ice on Greenland which flows into the North Atlantic
  • The fresh water dilutes the seawater, reducing its salinity and density
  • Less dense water sinks less easily so the flow rate of the current is reduced
  • Less heat is transported to NW Europe so the UK could become colder (like Moscow / Churchill at the same latitude) even while global temperatures rise
/ 4
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Several interconnected systems act at once over different timescales: jet stream changes may raise temperatures, a slowing Conveyor may lower them, greenhouse gases raise them
  • Natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) can hide or exaggerate anthropogenic change; individual events do not show trends
  • Time delays between cause and effect (oceans take a long time to warm because of volume and heat capacity); incomplete understanding of the processes
/ 3
04.4 (1 marks)
  • Argo floats sink to a set depth for about 10 days, then surface and transmit temperature, salinity and position data; the sequence of positions shows current direction and speed (surface currents also by satellites and buoys)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 96-97; AQA spec 3.2.1.2, 3.2.2.3.
Question 510 marks · Principles of pollution control (CPA, CGM, new technologies) · spec 3.4.3.1 · Genn pp. 243-245 · PCON-02
[6 marks]05.1Explain how the control of emission location and emission timing can reduce the severity of pollution. Use examples.
[2 marks]05.2Explain the polluter pays principle and suggest why it encourages pollution prevention.
[2 marks]05.3Explain the precautionary principle as applied to the release of a new chemical.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (6 marks)
  • Location: discharge into the sea / large water bodies where currents dilute and disperse the pollutant
  • Location: chimneys / emissions sited downwind of urban areas so pollutants are not carried over populations; avoiding valleys and enclosed water bodies where pollutants concentrate
  • Location: not discharging wastes onto permeable rock above an aquifer / not siting landfill on aquifers
  • Timing: restrict polluting activities (industrial combustion, vehicle use) during temperature inversions when dispersal is poor
  • Timing: discharge liquid effluent when river flow is high / on an ebb tide so it is diluted and carried away
  • Timing: pesticide spraying at night / on still days; noisy operations restricted to daytime; night-flight restrictions
/ 6
05.2 (2 marks)
  • The organisation / person that causes the pollution is responsible for (paying for) the damage caused
  • If the cost of preventing the pollution is less than the cost of the damage / fines, there is a financial incentive to prevent it; works best when penalties are high and escape is unlikely
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • It is assumed that the waste will cause pollution / harm until research shows it is unlikely to
  • So release is not permitted until it has been tested; lack of knowledge of a problem is not an excuse / does not reduce responsibility
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 243-245; AQA spec 3.4.3.1.

Question 710 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 195-196 · FN-06
[2 marks]07.1Explain the difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.
[2 marks]07.2State the sources of the two fuels used in fusion research.
[3 marks]07.3Explain why very high temperatures, a vacuum and a magnetic field are needed in a toroidal (torus) fusion reactor.
[3 marks]07.4Give two potential advantages of fusion over fission and one reason why it is not yet a commercial energy source.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Fission: splitting the nuclei of large atoms (U-235, Pu-239) when hit by neutrons
  • Fusion: joining the nuclei of small atoms (deuterium and tritium - isotopes of hydrogen) to form helium, releasing a neutron and energy - the energy source of stars
/ 2
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Deuterium (hydrogen-2) extracted from (sea)water
  • Tritium (hydrogen-3) produced by neutron bombardment of lithium (a lithium blanket around the reactor)
/ 2
07.3 (3 marks)
  • Very high temperature turns hydrogen into a plasma (electrons stripped away) and gives nuclei enough kinetic energy to overcome the repulsion between positive nuclei and collide
  • Vacuum so the plasma is not cooled by air
  • Magnetic field holds the plasma centrally so it does not touch the container walls and cool
/ 3
07.4 (3 marks)
  • Fuel (deuterium / lithium) is abundant / effectively unlimited
  • Much less radioactive waste (no long-lived fission products); no chain reaction / meltdown risk; no weapons material
  • Not yet achieved sustained net energy output: reactors (JET) use more energy than they release; ITER aims for 500 MW out from 50 MW in; laser fusion also experimental; commercial viability decades away
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 195-196; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.

Question 1010 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 266-272 · NUT-07
[9 marks]10.1Explain how organic wastes and inorganic nutrients cause water pollution and evaluate the effectiveness of the methods used to control them.
[1 mark]10.2Give one reason why algae cause eutrophication problems more quickly than rooted plants when nutrients are added to a lake.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Organic nutrients (sewage, manure, silage effluent, paper mill / tannery / food processing effluent) are food for aerobic microbes whose respiration deoxygenates the water, killing fish and invertebrates; bacterial growths cover the bed, blocking light
  • Pathogens in sewage (cholera, typhoid, dysentery) spread via contaminated water; decay of organic matter releases inorganic nutrients
  • Inorganic nutrients: nitrates (fertiliser / manure runoff, leaching) and phosphates (sewage effluent, detergents, eroded soil) cause cultural eutrophication: algal blooms, shading of macrophytes, cyanobacterial toxins, night-time and end-of-summer deoxygenation
  • Human health: nitrites and methaemoglobinaemia; possible carcinogen via nitrosamines
  • Control of organic wastes: sewage treatment (pre-treatment, primary sedimentation removing >95% organic matter, aeration / activated sludge or trickling filters, anaerobic digestion of sludge with biogas)
  • Control of nutrients: tertiary phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate, denitrification in filter beds, micro-strainers / UV; Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, fertiliser timing / amount, low-solubility fertilisers, legumes, manure management; dredging sediments
  • Evaluation: sewage treatment has virtually eliminated deoxygenation and waterborne disease in developed countries but storm overflows and combined sewers still discharge untreated sewage; tertiary treatment is expensive so only used at sensitive sites; diffuse agricultural nitrate is hard to control and aquifers respond slowly; phosphate legacy in sediments
  • Judgement on point-source (sewage) control being more successful than diffuse-source (agriculture) control
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Algae / cyanobacteria absorb nutrients directly from the water (not the sediments) so they use dissolved nutrients more easily and grow rapidly
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 266-272; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss the extent to which the study of ozone depletion illustrates how scientific investigation can lead to the successful solution of an environmental problem.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Stages of investigation: identifying the issue (Halley Station UV data, Dobson unit measurements), hypothesis formation (Rowland-Molina 1974: persistence, UV dissociation, chlorine reactions), data collection (ground, satellite, balloon and aircraft air sampling detecting ClO), analysis of spatial, temporal and altitude variability, proposal of solutions, enactment
  • Why Antarctica: low stratospheric temperatures, ice crystals, stratospheric clouds, polar vortex, spring sunlight
  • Effects of UVB: skin cancer, cataracts, DNA damage, plant and marine damage - motivating action
  • Solutions: Vienna Convention, Montreal Protocol - phase-out and bans, alternative processes (pump sprays), alternative materials (HCFCs, HFCs, hydrocarbons, HFAs), collection and incineration of CFCs, fund for developing countries
  • Evaluation of success: ozone hole area / depth stabilising, CFC levels falling; limitations - persistence, remaining ODSs, HFCs as greenhouse gases, decades to full recovery
  • Comparison with other atmospheric problems (climate change, acid rain) - why ozone was easier; role of peer review, international cooperation, availability of substitutes
  • Conclusion on the extent to which it is a model for other issues
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-113; spec 3.2.1.3.
[25 marks]11.2Explain how feedback mechanisms and tipping points influence the rate of global climate change and discuss the implications for the strategies needed to control it.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Negative feedback: increased low-level cloud (albedo), increased photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle evaporation-precipitation
  • Positive feedback: melting permafrost (methane), reduced albedo of snow / ice / sea ice, methane hydrate release, ocean acidification and coral loss, forest and peat fires, soil decomposition, water vapour, cirrus clouds
  • Tipping points: self-sustaining processes; why control must precede them; examples
  • Implications: urgency and precautionary principle; need for deep cuts in greenhouse gases (energy, transport, buildings, agriculture, forestry, CCS) rather than only adaptation; geoengineering risks
  • Difficulty of prediction because feedbacks interact at different scales; models; international agreements and per capita footprints
  • Judgement on whether current strategies are adequate
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
