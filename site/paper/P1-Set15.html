Question 210 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 135-136 · MIN-02

Figure 1 shows the mass of copper contained in the Earth's crust at different ore purities, illustrating Lasky's principle.

Figure 1
[1 mark]02.1State Lasky's principle.
[1 mark]02.2Use Figure 1 to calculate how many times more copper is contained in deposits of 0.25-0.5% purity than in deposits above 2.0%.
[3 marks]02.3Explain the difference between the reserves and the resource of a mineral.
[2 marks]02.4Explain, using Lasky's principle, why the ability to exploit slightly lower grade ores greatly increases reserves.
[3 marks]02.5Explain why the main problem for future mineral supplies is not the quantity that exists in the crust.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (1 marks)
  • As the (linear) purity / grade of a mineral deposit decreases, the amount of the mineral present increases exponentially / logarithmically
/ 1
02.2 (1 marks)
  • 2400 / 40 = 60 times
/ 1
02.3 (3 marks)
  • Reserves: the amount that can be exploited now, economically, with existing technology (deposits above the cut-off ore grade)
  • Resource: all the material that could be exploited technically and economically now or in the future (with realistic price rises or new technology), including deep, low-grade or protected deposits
  • The resource is finite but the reserves change: they rise if prices rise or technology improves, and fall if prices fall
/ 3
02.4 (2 marks)
  • Lowering the cut-off ore grade includes deposits of lower purity in the reserves
  • Because the amount of material rises exponentially as purity falls, a small drop in COOG adds a very large mass (eg 0.5-1.0% has 4x the copper of 1.0-2.0%)
/ 2
02.5 (3 marks)
  • The total quantity (stock / resource base) is far greater than could ever be used
  • But most is at purities too low or in locations too inaccessible for current economic exploitation
  • The challenge is developing technologies (low-energy extraction such as bioleaching, phytomining; better exploration) that make low-grade deposits exploitable; energy needs rise rapidly as ore grade falls
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 135-136; AQA spec 3.2.3.3.
Question 310 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.2 · Genn pp. 162-164 · SOIL-03

Figure 1 shows the annual soil loss measured on experimental plots with different vegetation cover on a 10% slope in a region with heavy seasonal rain.

Figure 1
[2 marks]03.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]03.2Explain four ways in which vegetation reduces the rate of soil erosion.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why ploughing up and down a slope causes more erosion than contour ploughing.
[2 marks]03.4Explain why the region's climate makes the soil vulnerable to both wind and water erosion.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Soil loss increases as vegetation cover decreases: forest lowest (0.5), bare soil highest (60 t ha-1 yr-1) - over 100 times more
  • Ploughing downslope loses nearly three times as much as contour ploughing for the same crop
/ 2
03.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Leaves / leaf litter intercept raindrops so soil particles are not dislodged by rain splash
  • Roots bind soil particles together
  • Vegetation acts as a windbreak reducing wind velocity and the kinetic energy available to carry particles
  • Plants increase infiltration (root channels, organic matter) so less surface runoff
  • Organic matter / humus from vegetation binds particles into stable peds
/ 4
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Furrows running downslope act as channels / gulleys that increase the velocity (kinetic energy) of runoff
  • Furrows along the contour trap the water so it loses energy, deposits particles and infiltrates
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • In the dry season the soil (especially low-clay soils) is loose and dry with little cohesion so wind blows it away
  • In the wet season heavy rain causes rain splash and surface runoff when infiltration capacity is exceeded
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 162-164; AQA spec 3.2.5.2.

Table 1 shows the mass of carbon in the main reservoirs of the carbon cycle.

[1 mark]05.1Express the mass of carbon in the atmosphere in standard form, in tonnes.
[2 marks]05.2Calculate the percentage increase in atmospheric carbon that would result from burning 10% of the fossil fuel reservoir, assuming all the carbon remained in the atmosphere.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why the sedimentary carbonate rocks, although by far the largest reservoir, have little effect on atmospheric CO2 concentrations.
[3 marks]05.4Describe how carbon moves from the atmosphere into sedimentary carbonate rocks.
[2 marks]05.5Explain why aerobic respiration returns carbon to the atmosphere as CO2 but anaerobic respiration returns it mainly as methane.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • 7 x 1011 tonnes
/ 1
05.2 (2 marks)
  • 10% of 10 000 = 1000 billion tonnes
  • 1000 / 700 x 100 = 143% (accept 142.9)
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Carbon is transferred into and out of rocks extremely slowly (fossilisation / sedimentation, weathering, volcanic release)
  • Residence time is millions of years so the flux to the atmosphere is tiny compared with respiration / photosynthesis / combustion
/ 2
05.4 (3 marks)
  • CO2 dissolves in the sea (forming carbonic acid / hydrogen carbonate ions) and is absorbed by phytoplankton through photosynthesis
  • Organisms such as molluscs, corals and planktonic organisms build calcium carbonate shells / exoskeletons
  • When they die the shells sink and sediment on the seabed, becoming limestone / chalk over long periods
/ 3
05.5 (2 marks)
  • Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break down organic compounds completely to CO2 (and water), releasing much more energy
  • Without oxygen, anaerobic organisms only partly break down organic compounds, releasing carbon as methane (CH4), which is later oxidised to CO2 in the atmosphere
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 148-149; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.
Question 610 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 112-113 · OZ-03

[2 marks]06.3Describe how waste CFCs from old refrigerators are disposed of safely.
[1 mark]06.4State one reason why the ozone layer is not expected to recover fully for many decades.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Manufacture and use of CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances (ODSs) phased out then banned
  • HCFCs to be phased out (by 2030); some essential uses (eg halon fire extinguishers in aircraft) still permitted
  • A fund to help (developing) countries implement the Protocol; legally binding targets (unlike the 1985 Vienna Convention)
/ 3
06.2 (4 marks)
  • Aerosols: pump-action sprays / roll-on deodorants (alternative process), or hydrocarbon propellants (propane, butane) - flammable / fire risk
  • Refrigerants: HCFCs (less stable so break down in the troposphere, but still some ozone depletion) then HFCs (no chlorine) - more expensive, less effective, powerful greenhouse gases
/ 4
06.3 (2 marks)
  • CFCs are drained from the appliance and incinerated / broken down at high temperature
  • Products are CO2 and acidic gases (HCl, HF) which are neutralised with crushed lime
/ 2
06.4 (1 marks)
  • CFCs are persistent, so those already released continue to reach the stratosphere and destroy ozone for many years; some ODSs (eg dichloromethane) are not banned

[3 marks]07.1Explain how a high bypass ratio reduces engine noise.
[2 marks]07.2Describe two other features of aircraft design that reduce noise.
[4 marks]07.3Explain how each of the following airport operating procedures reduces noise nuisance.
[1 mark]07.4Explain why aircraft are noisier on take-off than on landing.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • A turbofan at the front forces bypass air around the inner cowling / core jet
  • The bypass air smooths the flow of the turbulent hot exhaust gases, reducing the turbulence that creates noise
  • The higher the ratio of bypass air to jet exhaust the quieter the engine (Trent 1000 quietest, Olympus noisiest)
/ 3
07.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Chevron (serrated) nozzles mix bypass air with surrounding air more smoothly
  • Acoustic liners in the engine cowlings absorb noise
  • Hush kits fitted to older low-bypass engines
  • Fairings on undercarriage and wing-flap hinges reduce turbulence; welded rather than riveted panels
  • Lighter composite aircraft need less thrust
  • Blended wing with engines on top so the body shields the ground
/ 2
07.3 (4 marks)
  • Constant (3°) descent avoids periods of high thrust in a stepped descent and keeps aircraft higher for longer
  • Night flights (especially take-offs, which are noisier) are restricted / banned when people are sleeping and background noise is low
  • Noisier aircraft pay higher charges / use up more quota points and fines for exceeding limits, so airlines are encouraged to buy quieter aircraft
  • More time to slow down after landing so wheel brakes can be used instead of noisy reverse thrusters
/ 4
07.4 (1 marks)
  • Engines run at higher power / maximum thrust and the steep climb angle needs full power (although the aircraft climbs quickly out of earshot)
/ 1

Question 810 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 210-211, 214-217 · REN-05
[2 marks]08.1Explain the locational requirements for harnessing wave power.
[2 marks]08.2Describe how one named wave power device generates electricity.
[2 marks]08.3Give two design problems that have delayed the development of wave power.
[4 marks]08.4Compare the environmental impacts of tidal barrages, tidal lagoons and in-stream tidal turbines.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • High mean wind velocities that are consistent in strength and direction
  • A long fetch (distance of open water) over which waves build up, eg Atlantic coasts of the UK
/ 2
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Oscillating water column (eg Islay Limpet): waves force water up and down in a submerged chamber; the air forced in and out drives a turbine
  • Or point absorber (floating buoy moving relative to a fixed base turns a generator); surface attenuator (Pelamis - hinged sections push pistons pumping fluid over a turbine); overtopping device (Wave Dragon - waves fill a reservoir above sea level which drains through turbines); oscillating wave surge converter (Oyster)
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Equipment must withstand storms and corrosion
  • Difficult to anchor off stormy coasts / in deep water
  • Expensive to transmit electricity from isolated coasts to consumers
  • Competition with established technologies
/ 2
08.4 (4 marks)
  • Barrage: huge quantities of construction materials; reduces tidal range so intertidal mudflats used by wading birds are lost; changes sedimentation (erosion near turbines, deposition elsewhere), lower turbidity; pollutants concentrate in the lagoon; blocks migratory fish and shipping
  • Lagoon: similar but smaller scale; only part of the estuary enclosed so the most sensitive areas and main channel can be avoided
  • In-stream turbines: do not block the tidal flow so little impact on range or sediment; but noise affects cetaceans and many dispersed turbines could have a larger total impact; harness less energy
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 210-211, 214-217; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 910 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 416-418 · RM-11

A student investigated the effect of pH on the germination of cress seeds.

[2 marks]09.1Explain why cress is a suitable species for this investigation but the results may not apply directly to natural ecosystems.
[2 marks]09.2Describe how the student should decide the range of pH values to test.
[4 marks]09.3Give three variables that should be standardised and explain how long the experiment should continue.
[2 marks]09.4The student wants to compare the effect of nutrient concentration on the growth of duckweed. Explain why leaf area is a better measure of growth than fresh mass.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • Small seeds, genetically uniform, high and rapid germination rate so results are quick and reliable
  • Wild species have different ranges of tolerance so cress results cannot be applied directly
/ 2
09.2 (2 marks)
  • Preliminary study with widely spaced pH values to find where germination occurs
  • Final range from below the lowest pH giving germination to above the highest, with closer intervals
/ 2
09.3 (4 marks)
  • Temperature (constant, natural germination temperature)
  • Light, water volume, number of seeds per dish, seed source
  • Growth medium (filter paper; soil would alter pH); nutrients not needed for germination
  • Continue until it is clear that ungerminated seeds will not germinate, so that a delay in germination is not mistaken for failure
/ 4
09.4 (2 marks)
  • Surface water affects fresh mass and drying damages the plants; dry mass requires killing the plants so the same plants cannot be re-measured
  • Leaf area is a reasonable estimate of biomass that allows repeated measurement of the same plants over time

Question 1010 marks · Ionising radiation · spec 3.4.3.2.14 · Genn pp. 295-304 · RAD-06
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the risks and benefits of the uses of ionising radiation, and explain the principles used to control exposure of workers and the public.
[1 mark]10.2Give one reason why the risks and benefits of nuclear electricity may not fall on the same people.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Uses / benefits: nuclear electricity (low carbon, high energy density), medicine (X-rays, CT, radiotherapy, tracers, sterilising equipment), industry (thickness gauges, polymer cross-linking, testing turbines, oil exploration), agriculture (food irradiation, sterile male technique, mutation breeding), research tracers, smoke detectors, ship / submarine propulsion
  • Risks: free radical production, DNA damage, mutation, cancer; acute and chronic, somatic and gonadic effects; contamination vs exposure; activation products; long-lived wastes; accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima); weapons fallout
  • Factors affecting risk: type of radiation (alpha, beta, gamma, neutrons - penetration, RBE / weighting factors), half-life, dose (Gy, Sv), route of exposure
  • Risk:benefit analysis and why it is difficult (latency, alternative causes, poor exposure data, risks and benefits to different people, offsets such as avoided surgery)
  • Principles: ALARA, BATNEEC; closed sources, remote handling, absorbers / shielding, distance (inverse square law), time, protective clothing, decontamination, potassium iodide
  • Monitoring: dosemeters, film badges, air and contamination monitors; environmental monitoring via CPA and CGM; waste categories and disposal (vitrification, encapsulation, Drigg)
  • Evaluation / judgement: benefits generally outweigh risks when controls are applied; public perception vs actual risk (background radon 50% of dose vs <1% from nuclear industry)
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Consumers of the electricity may live in a different country / region from the uranium miners or the communities near waste stores who bear the risks
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 295-304; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.14.

[25 marks]11.1To what extent can renewable energy resources replace fossil fuels in meeting the energy needs of a modern society?
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Fossil fuel features that shaped society: energy density, storage, transport, high temperatures, liquid vehicle fuels, established infrastructure
  • Renewable features: intermittent / unpredictable (wind, solar) vs predictable (tidal) vs controllable (HEP, biofuel, geothermal); low energy density; location; low pollution but manufacturing impacts
  • Technologies and developments for each resource with examples; new technologies improving usability
  • Storage and grid management: pumped storage, batteries, hydrogen economy, P2G, V2G, thermal storage, peak shaving; the role of electricity as a secondary fuel
  • Economics and politics: development costs, interest, subsidies, external costs of fossil fuels, falling renewable costs
  • Energy conservation as part of the solution; sustainability principles - natural systems run on low-density renewable energy
  • Judgement: electricity largely yes; heat, aviation, shipping and high-temperature industry harder; transition period with nuclear / CCS

Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
