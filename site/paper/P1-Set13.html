Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Renewable electricity is intermittent - solar stops at night, wind on calm days, tidal power at slack water - and some of it is unpredictable, while demand fluctuates by the minute, day, week and season, so supply and demand rarely coincide. Without storage, surplus renewable output is wasted and shortfalls must be met by fossil-fuel peaking plants, which defeats the purpose. Electricity itself cannot be stored at scale, so the energy must be converted into another form and back.

Pumped-storage HEP is proven and responds within seconds for peak shaving, but suitable sites are few. Lithium-ion batteries now work at grid scale and in vehicles, with vehicle-to-grid schemes turning car batteries into distributed storage, though lithium supplies and lifetime limit them. Hydrogen from electrolysis can be stored, used in fuel cells or injected into the gas grid (power-to-gas), and its high energy density suits vehicles, although each conversion loses energy. Compressed air with heat recovery, molten-salt thermal stores for concentrating solar plants, thermal mass in buildings, flywheels and supercapacitors each fit particular timescales.

Storage is not the only answer: a diverse mix of wind, solar, tidal and biofuel smooths supply, smart-grid management shifts demand, and conservation reduces peaks. But these reduce rather than remove the mismatch. Short-term storage can be batteries and pumped hydro; the harder problem is seasonal storage, where only hydrogen or fuels made from surplus electricity look plausible. Without both, renewables can supplement but not dominate a country's supply.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Renewable energy is intermittent. Solar panels do not work at night and wind turbines do not work when it is not windy, but people still need electricity then. Storage means the energy can be saved for when it is needed. Pumped storage pumps water uphill when there is spare electricity and lets it flow back through turbines at peak times. Batteries can also store electricity. Hydrogen can be made from electricity and used in fuel cells. So storage is needed for renewables to provide most electricity.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 explains the mismatch and describes three storage methods correctly, but does not evaluate them (sites, lithium, conversion losses, timescales).
  • Level 3 distinguishes short-term from seasonal storage - the key insight for 'most of a country's electricity'.
  • Level 3 also considers the alternatives to storage (diversity, grid management, conservation) and explains why they are insufficient alone.

Model answers: what a Level 5 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 5 (22 marks)

Pollutants cause damage in proportion to how much reaches sensitive organisms and how harmful it is when it gets there. Both depend on properties, so knowing a pollutant's properties lets us predict where it will go and which control will work.

State of matter and density decide dispersal. Solid particulates settle near the source unless they are very small: PM10 and smaller stay suspended, travel far and penetrate lungs, so smoke is controlled at the point of release with electrostatic precipitators, cyclones and bag filters, and smokeless zones prevent its production. Gases disperse widely, and dense gases such as the hydrogen cyanide released at Bhopal hug the ground, so location and emission height matter. Persistence decides whether dilution is acceptable. Sewage and other biodegradable wastes break down, so treatment plus dilution in a river or the sea is sufficient; organochlorine pesticides, CFCs, heavy metals and long-lived radioisotopes are not broken down, so they travel, accumulate and must be contained, banned or replaced - DDT and CFCs were both controlled by substitution once their persistence was understood.

Solubility governs the pathway. Water-soluble nitrate leaches into rivers and aquifers, so it is controlled by nitrate vulnerable zones, fertiliser timing and buffer strips; liposoluble pollutants such as DDT and methyl mercury pass into fat and are biomagnified along food chains, as at Minamata, so even low concentrations in water are dangerous and the only effective control is to stop release. Adsorption on clay immobilises caesium and metals, which is why radioactive discharges are monitored in sediments and why activated carbon and polymer adsorbents are used deliberately in effluent treatment.

Toxicity, specificity and mode of action set acceptable doses and identify species at risk. Neonicotinoids are toxic to insects but not vertebrates because they cannot cross the vertebrate blood-brain barrier, which explains both their popularity and their effect on bees; pyrethroids replaced organochlorines because they are less persistent. For mutagens and carcinogens such as ionising radiation there is no safe dose, so exposure is kept As Low As Reasonably Achievable with shielding, distance, time limits and monitoring. Reactivity explains secondary pollutants - sulfur dioxide oxidised to sulfuric acid, nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons forming ozone and PANs in photochemical smog - so control targets the primary emissions with flue-gas desulfurisation and catalytic converters. Synergism between smoke and sulfur dioxide, or ozone and sulfur dioxide, means limits must consider pollutants together.

Properties interact with the environment: temperature inversions trap pollutants, currents disperse them, pH and oxygen alter solubility. Critical Pathway Analysis combines the two to predict where a discharge will concentrate and to focus monitoring, and Critical Group Monitoring checks the most exposed people. These principles have reduced smoke, sulfur dioxide, lead and organochlorines dramatically in developed countries. Their limits are pollutants with diffuse sources or that underpin economies - nitrate, carbon dioxide - where knowing the properties is not the problem; changing behaviour is. Even there, the precautionary principle applies the same logic: predict the properties of a new substance from similar ones and control it before release, because prevention is always cheaper than remediation.

What makes the difference
  • Every paragraph is organised around a property, each linked to a prediction and a named control - the essay answers the question throughout rather than describing pollutants.
  • Examples span the specification (smoke, acid rain, pesticides, heavy metals, radiation, nutrients, oil), with quantitative and named detail (Bhopal, Minamata, PM10, ALARA), which is what Level 5 breadth means.
  • The final paragraph evaluates: where the approach has worked, where it has not and why, and reaches a justified conclusion.
  • A Level 3 version of this essay would describe several pollutants and their effects accurately but only occasionally connect a property to a control, and would end without a judgement.
