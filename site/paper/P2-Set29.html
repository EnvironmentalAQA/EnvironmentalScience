Question 110 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 340-343 · FISH-02

Table 1 shows features of some fishing methods.

[6 marks]01.1Complete Table 1.
Table 1

[2 marks]01.2Explain why by-catch is a particular problem with shrimp trawling and drift netting.
[2 marks]01.3Explain what is meant by ghost fishing and describe two ways it can be reduced.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (6 marks)
  • Demersal trawl: seabed species - cod, haddock, plaice, shrimps, scampi; chains / weights damage the seabed (mix aerobic and anaerobic layers, destroy sea fans, corals, seagrass); high by-catch in mixed fisheries; high fuel use
  • Purse seine: shoaling pelagic species - tuna, sardines, herring, anchovy; can remove whole shoals; dolphins associated with tuna shoals may be caught
  • Pelagic long-line: tuna, squid; albatrosses (long-lived, low breeding rate) drown on baited hooks; sharks and turtles caught
/ 6
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Shrimp trawls use very small mesh so crabs, molluscs, sea urchins, starfish, juvenile fish cannot escape - by-catch can be 99% of the catch
  • Drift nets are non-selective curtains near the surface catching whales, dolphins, turtles, sharks
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Lost or discarded nets and traps continue to catch and kill organisms; dead animals act as bait attracting more
  • Biodegradable ropes / panels so lost traps fall apart; radio transmitters on traps so they can be recovered; net recovery schemes
/ 2

Question 210 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 61-64 · HAB-08
[2 marks]02.1Explain how overfishing of the giant triton mollusc damaged coral reefs in Australia.
[4 marks]02.2Describe four management practices used in marine protected areas to protect coral reefs.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why deep-water coral reefs recover much more slowly from trawling damage than tropical reefs.
[2 marks]02.4Give two threats to deep-water reefs and one conservation measure.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (2 marks)
  • Giant tritons are predators of the crown-of-thorns starfish which eats coral polyps
  • Removing tritons allowed starfish populations to explode, destroying large areas of reef (food web / trophic cascade)
/ 2
02.2 (4 marks)
  • Fixed mooring buoys so boats do not anchor on coral
  • Education of divers / snorkellers not to touch coral or kick up sediment; bans on spear fishing and souvenir collection
  • Fishing controls: no-take zones (Chagos, Great Barrier Reef, Lundy, Lamlash Bay), seasons, minimum and maximum catch sizes, bans on dynamite / small-mesh nets / trawling
  • Protection of turtle nesting beaches; control of coastal development; protection of mangroves and seagrass; artificial reefs (reef balls, sunk ships / oil rigs)
/ 4
02.3 (2 marks)
  • No sunlight so no symbiotic algae; polyps rely only on captured food so have far less energy and grow very slowly
  • Damage from trawls takes hundreds of years to repair; the slow-growing, low-fecundity fish (orange roughy) they support are easily over-exploited
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
  • Deep-water trawling (also by-catch); oil and gas exploration in deeper water; ocean acidification
  • Protected areas banning trawling, eg Darwin Mounds SAC off NW Scotland, Oculina Bank off Florida
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 61-64; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Question 410 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 36-37 · CBR-03
[3 marks]04.1Explain the difference between hard and soft release and state the types of species for which each is suitable.
[5 marks]04.2Describe five features of a suitable release site.
[2 marks]04.3Explain the importance of post-release monitoring.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Hard release: individuals released with no post-release support - suitable for species whose behaviour is instinctive and need no learned skills (insects, fish, amphibians, reptiles)
  • Soft release: gradual release into larger areas with food provided while they learn to find food, avoid predators - needed for mammals and birds
  • Problems faced: finding / recognising food and poisonous foods, hunting skills (captive-born predators never learned), avoiding predators, acceptance into wild social groups
/ 3
04.2 (5 marks)
Any 5 from:
  • Large enough suitable habitat for a viable population
  • Reliable food supply
  • Low predation risk / original threat removed
  • Suitable breeding sites
  • Water
  • Support of the local human population
  • Official / legal protection of habitat and species
/ 5
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Tracking (radio / GPS tags, rings) shows survival, dispersal and breeding of released animals so the success of the project can be assessed
  • Identifies causes of failure so future releases can be improved / extra support given
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 36-37; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 510 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1-3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 410-413, 417 · RM-10
[3 marks]05.1Describe how soil texture is determined using a stack of sieves.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why a soil auger gives a more representative sample than a trowel.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why the organic matter content will be overestimated if a soil sample is heated above 550 °C.
[3 marks]05.4Describe how a turbidimeter measures water turbidity and explain how quantitative results are obtained.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Sieves assembled with the coarsest mesh on top; a dried, crushed soil sample is placed in the top sieve
  • Stack shaken (about 2 minutes) until no more particles pass; each fraction weighed
  • Percentages of sand (0.02-2 mm), silt (0.002-0.02 mm) and clay (base pan, below 0.002 mm) calculated; material over 2 mm (stones, twigs) is not soil; plotted on a soil triangle
/ 3
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Auger collects a core of known cross-sectional area and depth
  • So samples from different sites are directly comparable (also needed for bulk density = dry mass / volume)
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Minerals such as calcium carbonate break down releasing carbon dioxide
  • So mass is lost that is not organic matter
/ 2
05.4 (3 marks)
  • A beam of light passes through the sample in a dark chamber; a light sensor at 90° measures light scattered by suspended particles - more turbid, more scatter
  • Standardise bulb brightness / colour, distance and container
  • A calibration curve is produced using samples with known amounts of suspended matter so readings convert to concentrations; Secchi discs / turbidity bottles give only semi-quantitative results
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 410-413, 417; AQA spec 3.7.2.1-3.7.2.2.

Table 1 shows some designated protected areas in the UK.

[4 marks]07.1Complete Table 1 to show the legislation under which each area is designated and its key feature.
Table 1
DesignationDesignated underKey feature
Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI)
Special Protection Area (SPA)
Ramsar site
Marine Conservation Zone (MCZ)
[3 marks]07.2Explain how designating a protected area protects habitats and species.
[3 marks]07.3Explain why voluntary agri-environmental schemes are needed in addition to legislation.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • SSSI: UK legislation (Wildlife and Countryside Act); best examples of UK flora, fauna, geological features; management plan agreed with owner who must notify Operations Likely to Damage (ploughing, drainage, pesticides, tree planting / removal)
  • SPA: EU Birds Directive; places of international importance for birds (with SACs under the Habitats Directive form the Natura 2000 network; may restrict activities in surrounding areas)
  • Ramsar: Ramsar Convention (intergovernmental agreement); internationally important wetlands, originally for waterfowl, now wetland conservation and wise use generally
  • MCZ: UK marine legislation (Marine and Coastal Access Act) under the W&CA framework; protects marine habitats / species by restricting activities such as fishing and dredging; NNR: best SSSIs with complete communities
/ 4
07.2 (3 marks)
  • Legal status: restrictions on damaging activities within the area (and sometimes in surrounding areas, eg drainage)
  • Management agreements between owners and statutory bodies to maintain suitable conditions
  • But it does not protect against external influences such as air pollution or climate change; very few sites are state-owned so protection depends on owner cooperation
/ 3
07.3 (3 marks)
  • Much of the UK's wildlife lives in habitats created by farming (hay meadows, hedgerows) which only survive if the farming continues
  • Managing land for wildlife costs money / reduces income so farmers need payments (points for beetle banks, hedgerows, buffer strips, wild bird seed, low-input grassland)
  • Benefits everyone so society pays through central funding; legislation cannot compel positive management
/ 3

Question 1010 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3 · Genn pp. 72-85 · LP-07
[9 marks]10.1Explain how an understanding of ecological succession and population dynamics can inform conservation decision-making.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by a taxon.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Succession: pioneers, seres (hydrosere, lithosere, psammosere), modification of abiotic conditions, climax communities controlled by climate; deflected succession and plagioclimaxes
  • Application: deciding whether to manage (plagioclimax habitats need grazing, mowing, burning, coppicing, pollarding continued) or leave alone (climax habitats); habitat creation planning the stages; controlling scrub / secondary succession; understanding that new habitats change over time
  • Population dynamics: birth and death rates, density-dependent and independent factors, carrying capacity, sigmoid growth, predator-prey cycles, r- and k-selection, MSY
  • Application: setting sustainable harvest levels (fisheries, whaling, forestry); recognising k-selected species need strict protection and recover slowly; deciding when culling is needed (deer without wolves, introduced species) or when releases / captive breeding are needed (low breeding rate); predicting effects of reintroducing predators
  • Monitoring population size, distribution, survival rate and age structure to assess success; adaptation and range of tolerance guiding abiotic management
  • Evaluation: uncertainties (natural fluctuations, unknown carrying capacity); examples (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish deer, chalk grassland); judgement
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Any taxonomic group of organisms classified together by similarity (eg species, genus, family)
/ 1
