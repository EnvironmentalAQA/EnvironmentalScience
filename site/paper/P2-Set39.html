Question 110 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 69-71 · HAB-12
[4 marks]01.1Describe how each of the following threatens Antarctica.
[6 marks]01.2Describe the main features of the Antarctic Treaty (1959) and the regulations that protect Antarctica.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Warming melts ice shelves that hold back glaciers and lubricates ice flow so land ice reaches the sea faster (Antarctic Peninsula most affected); sea ice loss removes krill habitat and lowers albedo
  • Raised UV harms planktonic algae, krill and fish / sea urchin larvae in surface waters (land animals protected by fur / feathers)
  • Disturbance of wildlife, introduction of non-indigenous species and pathogens, fuel and sewage pollution; visitor numbers rising rapidly at a few landing sites
  • Reserves of coal, oil, gold, uranium, platinum etc; pressure to exploit will rise as reserves elsewhere deplete though currently banned and operations extremely difficult
/ 4
01.2 (6 marks)
  • Treaty signed by 52 countries including all active there; no military activity (except supporting research), no nuclear explosions or radioactive waste dumping
  • No commercial mineral exploitation
  • Fisheries regulated by CCAMLR: krill and some fish exploited, fisheries closed if over-exploited; no commercial exploitation of mammals
  • Tourism control: no accommodation on land (wastes stay on ships), one ship per site with maximum 100 ashore, trained guides, keep 5 m from wildlife, no litter, cleaned protective clothing to prevent introductions
  • Waste management: all wastes removed (fuel containers, batteries, plastics, oil); sewage only discharged where sea currents disperse it; old waste removed unless removal causes more damage
  • Only long-term residents are research scientists and support staff; strict regulation of research impacts
/ 6
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 69-71; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Question 310 marks · Scientific methodologies & sampling design · spec 3.7.1, 3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 390-393, 419-420 · RM-03

A group of students wants to investigate the effect of vegetation cover on rain-splash soil erosion.

[5 marks]03.1Describe how they could carry out the investigation in the laboratory.
[3 marks]03.2Give three variables, other than those you have standardised, that affect rain-splash erosion in the field.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why it is easier to obtain valid results in the laboratory than in the field, and give one disadvantage of laboratory studies.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (5 marks)
  • Trays of the same soil (texture, compaction, organic matter, depth) with different percentage vegetation cover (eg 0, 25, 50, 75, 100% grass), replicated
  • Same slope / gradient; simulated rain from a standard height at a standard rate and drop size (eg watering can / sprinkler) for a standard time
  • Collect the soil splashed / washed off (eg onto a sheet or into a trough at the base), dry and weigh it
  • Repeat each treatment several times and calculate means (and standard deviation)
  • Plot soil loss against cover; statistical test (Spearman's rank / t-test) for significance
/ 5
03.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Rainfall intensity / raindrop size / height
  • Soil texture, compaction, organic matter, depth
  • Permeability of the material beneath
  • Gradient
  • Root binding
/ 3
03.3 (2 marks)
  • All variables except the independent variable can be standardised in the laboratory; in the field uncontrollable variables (weather) must be measured instead
  • Laboratory conditions may not represent natural conditions so results may not apply directly to real ecosystems
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 390-393, 419-420; AQA spec 3.7.1, 3.7.2.2.
Question 410 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2 · Genn pp. 7-8 · LIFE-02
[4 marks]04.1Explain four properties of water that make it essential for life.
[4 marks]04.2Describe how photosynthetic organisms changed the atmosphere and explain how this allowed life to colonise the land.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how carbon sequestration by early photoautotrophs helped maintain a suitable temperature on Earth.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • General physiological solvent: most biological reactions take place between substances dissolved in water
  • Transport medium in blood / sap carrying gases, sugars, amino acids, nutrients, wastes
  • High specific heat capacity moderates temperature changes; evaporation absorbs heat (cooling)
  • Anomalous expansion: ice floats so water bodies do not freeze solid; absorbs UV protecting early marine life; provides aquatic habitats
/ 4
04.2 (4 marks)
  • From about 2.7 billion years ago photosynthetic archaea / bacteria (then algae and plants) released oxygen; at first it reacted with dissolved iron (forming iron ore deposits) then built up in the oceans and atmosphere (from about 2.45 billion years ago)
  • Oxygen in the stratosphere absorbed UV, forming ozone (O3) in a dynamic equilibrium of O, O2 and O3
  • The ozone layer reduced UV reaching the surface so organisms could survive out of the water
  • Oxygen allowed aerobic respiration releasing more energy so complex multicellular organisms evolved; many anaerobic archaea died out
/ 4
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Photosynthesis removed CO2 (a greenhouse gas) from the atmosphere, storing carbon in sediments, carbonate rocks and fossil fuels
  • This reduced infrared retention, offsetting the increasing brightness of the Sun (about 10% per billion years) so temperatures did not rise excessively
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 7-8; AQA spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2.
Question 510 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.3.3 · Genn pp. 47-49 · CBR-07

Table 1 shows the number of plant species per m2 in a chalk grassland reserve under different management, ten years after management began.

Table 1
ManagementMean plant species per m2Percentage of area covered by scrub / bushes
No management (abandoned)855
Sheep grazing245
Annual mowing (August)190
Winter burning1410
[2 marks]05.1Explain what is meant by a plagioclimax community and why chalk grassland is an example.
[4 marks]05.2Use Table 1 to explain why grazing produced the highest plant diversity.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why different grazing animals are chosen for different plagioclimax habitats.
[2 marks]05.4Explain how burning maintains heathland.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • A community maintained at a stage before the climax by regular human activity (grazing, mowing, burning, coppicing) that deflects succession
  • Chalk grassland was produced by centuries of sheep grazing; without it succession to scrub and woodland resumes
/ 2
05.2 (4 marks)
  • Grazing removes taller / dominant grasses and prevents scrub establishing (5% cover) so small plants are not shaded out
  • Trampling creates bare ground for wildflower seed germination and warm patches for invertebrates / reptiles
  • Dung creates small nutrient-rich patches supporting different plants and invertebrates; sheep eat short grasses and some bushes
  • Mowing removes everything at once including plants grazers avoid, and removes flowers / seeds before setting; burning removes vegetation but also kills invertebrates and allows scrub regrowth; abandoned plot dominated by scrub (55%) so few species
/ 4
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Sheep eat short grass (some breeds eat scrub); ponies avoid flowers but eat bracken; cattle remove long rough grass
  • Choice depends on the vegetation to be controlled and the plants to be protected
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Removes mature heather bushes and young trees that grazers will not eat, creating open unshaded ground
  • Heat stimulates dormant heather seeds to germinate so young heather re-establishes
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 47-49; AQA spec 3.1.3.3.

Question 910 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 18-19 · BIOV-07
[2 marks]09.1Explain why the ecosystem services provided by other species are often given a low conservation priority.
[3 marks]09.2Explain how soil organisms provide services that humans depend on.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why pollination by insects is more useful than wind pollination to plants growing in forests.
[3 marks]09.4Explain how seed dispersal by animals increases the survival of a plant's offspring.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • The processes (eg regulation of atmospheric gases by photosynthesis and respiration) act in a dynamic equilibrium and cancel each other out
  • Because there is no overall change their actions go unnoticed / their importance is not appreciated until they are lost
/ 2
09.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Invertebrates, fungi and bacteria break down / decompose dead organic matter, releasing nutrients for plant growth
  • The organic matter and humus produced hold the soil together (reducing erosion) and regulate the water cycle, giving more even river flow and less flooding
  • Organic acids from decomposition aid the weathering of rock, producing more soil and releasing more nutrients
  • Microbes carry out the processes of the carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles so wastes do not build up and nutrients are not depleted
/ 3
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Trees reduce wind velocity so wind pollination would be ineffective / unreliable over long distances
  • Insects search over long distances for flowers so dispersed populations can be pollinated, and less pollen needs to be produced (energy saving); specialised flowers reduce pollen being wasted on other species
/ 2
09.4 (3 marks)
  • Dispersal is not haphazard: animals live in the same habitat as the plant so are likely to carry seeds to locations where they can survive
  • Seeds can be carried longer distances than by wind and can be larger, containing more nutrients for the seedling
  • Seeds passing through the gut are deposited in faecal material which acts as a fertiliser, and passage through the intestine may stimulate germination
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 18-19; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.3.

[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by industrial symbiosis.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Water: catchment management, conservation, metering, greywater, re-use of treated effluent, aquifer recharge, sustainable urban drainage - largely cyclical already; limits in arid regions
  • Minerals: exploiting low-grade ores with low-temperature processes (bioleaching), extending product lifespans, repair / re-use / repurpose / recycle, design for disassembly, reduced packaging; limits: dispersal, alloys, energy of collection

  • Energy: renewables, storage, energy density technologies, low-temperature manufacturing, CHP; limits of intermittency, transport fuels

10.2 (1 marks)
  • Locating industries so that the wastes of one (heat, gases, by-products) become the raw materials of another, mimicking natural cycles (eg Kalundborg eco-industrial park)
