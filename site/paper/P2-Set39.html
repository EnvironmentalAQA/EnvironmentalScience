Question 110 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 308-310, 313 · AGR-02
[3 marks]01.1Explain why a moderate water shortage reduces crop productivity even though the plants survive.
[4 marks]01.2Explain the problems caused by waterlogged soils and describe two ways farmers reduce soil water levels.
[2 marks]01.3Explain how irrigating with groundwater can cause soil salinisation and how this harms crops.
[1 mark]01.4State how a farmer could raise the pH of an acidic soil and explain why a low pH reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
01.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Plants close their stomata to reduce transpiration losses
  • 1Closed stomata stop the absorption of carbon dioxide so photosynthesis and growth stop
  • 1Less water for nutrient absorption / transport and cell turgidity; severe shortage dehydrates cells and inhibits biochemical reactions
/ 3
01.2 4 marks
Up to 4 marks in total; award marks in proportion to the completeness of each point.
  • up to 2Anaerobic soil favours denitrifying bacteria and inhibits nitrifying bacteria so nitrate is lost faster than it forms; higher risk of fungal disease; root respiration inhibited
  • up to 2Drainage ditches or pipes
  • up to 2Deep ploughing; avoiding compaction by machinery / livestock; adding organic matter to encourage worms
/ 4
01.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Groundwater often contains dissolved salts; when the water evaporates from the soil the salts are left behind and accumulate
  • 1High soil salinity causes osmotic dehydration of roots / crop death; washing out with extra water uses more water and salinises rivers
/ 2
01.4 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Add crushed lime (calcium carbonate / hydroxide); low pH increases leaching, inhibits nutrient uptake and mobilises toxic aluminium and lead

Question 910 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.1 · Genn pp. 338-339 · FISH-01

Figure 1 shows the seasonal changes in light intensity, nutrient concentration and phytoplankton biomass in the surface water of a temperate ocean in the northern hemisphere.

Figure 1
[4 marks]09.1Use Figure 1 to explain the spring peak and summer decline in phytoplankton biomass.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why tropical open oceans have low productivity all year despite high light and temperature.
[3 marks]09.3Explain why areas of upwelling and coastal waters are the most productive parts of the sea.
[1 mark]09.4Explain why phosphate is often the limiting nutrient in the sea.
Show mark scheme
09.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1In winter nutrients are high (storms disturb sediments, deep mixing) but light is too low for photosynthesis
  • 1In spring light rises while nutrients are still high so growth is rapid - the spring bloom (peak about May)
  • 1The bloom uses up nutrients (falling to 10 by July); dead plankton sink out of the photic zone carrying nutrients to the seabed
  • 1So nutrients become limiting in summer despite high light; a smaller autumn bloom follows when storms return nutrients while light is still adequate
/ 4
09.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Warm surface water is stable (no seasonal mixing / storms) so nutrients lost to deep water in sinking dead organisms are not replaced
  • 1Nutrient (especially phosphate) concentrations in the photic zone remain very low - the limiting factor
/ 2
09.3 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Upwelling (offshore winds, seamounts, currents deflected upwards) brings cold nutrient-rich deep water into the photic zone
  • 1Coastal waters receive nutrients washed off the land in rivers and storms disturb shallow sediments
  • 1Nutrients plus light in the shallow photic zone allow high algal productivity supporting rich food webs and fisheries (eg Peru anchovy)
/ 3
09.4 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Phosphates have low solubility and no atmospheric reservoir; they are only added by river runoff or upwelling and are lost to sediments
/ 1

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Context: wild catches have not increased since about 1990 as stocks are fully or over-exploited; aquaculture output growing rapidly and approaching the wild catch; fish are the last large-scale hunted food
  • Advantages of aquaculture: control of limiting factors, selective breeding, gender control, high yields per area; fish have low FCRs (no thermoregulation / support) compared with livestock; herbivores (tilapia, carp) and molluscs very efficient; polyculture, IMTA, aquaponics, rice-fish
  • Limits: carnivorous species (salmon, shrimp) need fishmeal from wild fish so still depend on fishing and deplete forage fish (sandeels - puffins); some species (bluefin tuna) will not breed in captivity; consumer preference for carnivores
  • Environmental impacts: habitat loss (mangroves), organic pollution, pesticides, antibiotics and resistance, lice, escapes and gene pool contamination, predator control; and how they can be reduced
  • Fishing can be sustainable with quotas, gear design, NTZs, size limits, effort limits; MSY management needs good data
  • Judgement: aquaculture can supply much of the growth in demand, especially herbivores and shellfish, but cannot fully replace fishing; both need sustainable management
