Question 110 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.2-3.4.3.2.3 · Genn pp. 252-253 · AIR-04
[2 marks]01.1Describe how sulfur can be removed from coal before combustion.
[3 marks]01.2Describe the process of dry flue gas desulfurisation and explain how the product can be used.
[2 marks]01.3In wet flue gas desulfurisation the flue gases are bubbled through sodium sulfite solution. Explain one advantage of this method over dry FGD.
[3 marks]01.4Explain how each of the following reduces NOx emissions.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Most sulfur in coal is present as (solid) iron pyrites (FeS2)
  • Coal is crushed and put in a stream of flowing water: the coal is carried away while the denser pyrites are left behind (washing / streaming)
/ 2
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Flue gases are passed through a bed of crushed calcium carbonate (limestone)
  • Sulfur dioxide reacts (with oxygen and calcium carbonate) to form solid calcium sulfate (and carbon dioxide)
  • If the gases have already been cleaned of smoke, the calcium sulfate is pure enough to be sold as gypsum for building plaster
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • The sodium hydrogen sulfite formed can be heated to regenerate sodium sulfite (re-used) and release concentrated sulfur dioxide
  • The pure sulfur dioxide is a valuable raw material for making sulfur / sulfuric acid, so a waste becomes a resource
/ 2
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Fluidised bed: increased surface area allows rapid combustion at lower temperatures so less nitrogen and oxygen react to form NOx (production prevented)
  • Catalytic converter: platinum / palladium catalyst chemically reduces NOx back to nitrogen and oxygen gases in the exhaust
  • Urea sprayed into the hot flue / exhaust gases reacts with NOx, converting it to nitrogen, carbon dioxide and water
/ 3

[2 marks]04.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]04.2Explain the causes of the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why fish kills in eutrophic lakes usually happen at night or at the end of summer.
[2 marks]04.4Give two other harmful effects of algal blooms.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • Dissolved oxygen fluctuates daily / 24-hour cycle: highest in the afternoon (about 13-13.5 mg dm-3 at 16 and 40 h) and lowest at night / early morning (2.5-3.5 mg dm-3 at 4 and 28 h)
  • The night-time minimum is lower on the second night (2.5 vs 3.5)
/ 2
04.2 (4 marks)
  • Nitrates (from fertiliser runoff) and phosphates (from sewage effluent) cause eutrophication: rapid growth of algae / cyanobacteria (algal bloom)
  • During the day photosynthesis by the dense algae releases oxygen so concentrations rise (above the normal saturation level)
  • At night there is no photosynthesis but respiration by algae / animals / bacteria continues, using oxygen
  • Aerobic bacterial decomposition of dead algae / shaded macrophytes uses oxygen; warm summer water holds less oxygen and decay is rapid
/ 4
04.3 (2 marks)
  • At night no photosynthesis replaces the oxygen used in respiration / decomposition so concentrations fall below the minimum fish can tolerate
  • At the end of summer large amounts of dead vegetation decay rapidly in warm water causing severe deoxygenation
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Cyanobacteria release toxins harmful to livestock, pets and people
  • Floating algae shade submerged / rooted macrophytes which die, disrupting food webs
  • Algae are an unreliable food source (die off rapidly) so food webs are poor
  • Blocked filters / unpleasant taste and smell in water supplies
/ 2

Question 510 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 394-395, 401-402 · RM-05

A student used 0.5 m x 0.5 m grid quadrats to survey ground flora in two meadows, one grazed and one ungrazed. Table 1 shows the results for the grazed meadow (10 quadrats).

Table 1
SpeciesNumber of individuals
Ribwort plantain48
Perennial ryegrass120
White clover36
Daisy24
Creeping buttercup12
[2 marks]05.1Explain why a 0.5 m x 0.5 m quadrat was chosen rather than a 10 cm x 10 cm or 100 m x 100 m quadrat.
[3 marks]05.2Calculate Simpson's Index of Biodiversity (D) for the grazed meadow using D = N(N-1) / Σn(n-1). Show your working.
[2 marks]05.3The ungrazed meadow had D = 1.8 with the same number of species. Explain what this shows.
[2 marks]05.4Explain why a point quadrat would be better than a frame quadrat in tall vegetation.
[1 mark]05.5Give one limitation of using the DAFOR abundance scale.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • Quadrat size depends on the size / spacing of the organisms: 0.5 m x 0.5 m is typical for ground flora
  • 10 cm x 10 cm is for lichens (too small to contain a representative sample of plants); 100 m x 100 m is for trees (impractical, too much work)
/ 2
05.2 (3 marks)
  • N = 240; N(N-1) = 240 x 239 = 57 360
  • Σn(n-1) = 48x47 + 120x119 + 36x35 + 24x23 + 12x11 = 2256 + 14280 + 1260 + 552 + 132 = 18 480
  • D = 57 360 / 18 480 = 3.10 (accept 3.1)
/ 3
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Lower D means lower biodiversity: the ungrazed meadow is dominated by one or a few species (eg tall grass) with the others rare (less even)
  • Grazing (plagioclimax management) prevents dominance by vigorous species so abundance is more evenly spread
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
  • A frame quadrat flattens tall plants, making percentage cover estimates inaccurate and hiding shorter plants
  • Point quadrat pins are lowered through the vegetation; each touch counts (10 pins = 10% each) without flattening
/ 2
05.5 (1 marks)
  • Subjective judgement varies between recorders; results are not quantitative so statistical analysis is not possible (but quick for many areas)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 394-395, 401-402; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.

Question 1010 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4 · Genn pp. 148-157 · BGC-07
[9 marks]10.1Describe how human activities have altered the natural equilibria of the carbon and nitrogen cycles, and evaluate the strategies that could counteract these changes.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by the residence time of a substance in a reservoir.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Carbon: combustion of fossil fuels and biomass moving carbon from long-term stores to the atmosphere; deforestation reducing photosynthesis; ploughing / drainage increasing aerobic decomposition; new anaerobic environments (paddies, landfill, reservoirs, livestock) releasing methane; marine pollution reducing phytoplankton; consequences: rising CO2 and CH4, climate change, ocean acidification, reduced soil organic matter
  • Nitrogen: Haber process doubling fixation; legume cultivation; drainage increasing nitrification and reducing denitrification; combustion NOx; sewage and fertiliser leaching; consequences: eutrophication, smogs, acid rain, N2O warming, NOx toxicity
  • Counter-strategies carbon: renewables / nuclear replacing fossil fuels, energy conservation, afforestation matched to deforestation and timber use, conserving peat bogs and forests, increasing soil organic matter (low tillage, mulching), CCS (pre / post combustion, storage)
  • Counter-strategies nitrogen: reduced combustion, catalytic converters / urea sprays, biological fixation instead of Haber, legumes and crop rotation, management of biological wastes as fertiliser, low-solubility fertilisers, timing, buffer strips, low tillage, nitrate vulnerable zones
  • Evaluation: some strategies well established and cheap (rotation, buffer strips), others expensive or unproven (CCS); scale of fossil fuel dependence; feedback and time lags; link to circular economy and low-temperature natural processes; judgement
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The average time an atom / molecule remains in the reservoir before being transferred out (volume / transfer rate)
/ 1

[25 marks]11.2Explain how feedback mechanisms and tipping points influence the rate of global climate change and discuss the implications for the strategies needed to control it.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Negative feedback: increased low-level cloud (albedo), increased photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle evaporation-precipitation
  • Positive feedback: melting permafrost (methane), reduced albedo of snow / ice / sea ice, methane hydrate release, ocean acidification and coral loss, forest and peat fires, soil decomposition, water vapour, cirrus clouds
  • Tipping points: self-sustaining processes; why control must precede them; examples
  • Implications: urgency and precautionary principle; need for deep cuts in greenhouse gases (energy, transport, buildings, agriculture, forestry, CCS) rather than only adaptation; geoengineering risks
  • Difficulty of prediction because feedbacks interact at different scales; models; international agreements and per capita footprints
  • Judgement on whether current strategies are adequate
