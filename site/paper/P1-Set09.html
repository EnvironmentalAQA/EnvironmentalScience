  • Only worthwhile where seawater is available and freshwater supplies are inadequate (eg Malta, Middle East)

Figure 1 shows a simplified energy budget for the Earth and its atmosphere. Values are percentages of incoming solar radiation.

Figure 1

[1 mark]07.1Use Figure 1 to calculate the planetary albedo of the Earth.
[6 marks]07.2Describe how each of the following wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation behaves in the atmosphere.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why the amount of infrared re-emitted to the surface (85%) is larger than the amount emitted to space (70%).
[1 mark]07.4Explain the term thermal stratification of the atmosphere.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (1 marks)
  • 25 + 5 = 30%
/ 1
07.2 (6 marks)
  • UV: short wavelength; mostly absorbed in the stratosphere by monatomic, diatomic and triatomic oxygen (ozone layer) which are formed and destroyed in a dynamic equilibrium; converted to chemical energy in these reactions; little reaches the surface
  • Visible light: transmitted through the atmosphere easily (some reflected by clouds / dust); absorbed by the Earth's surface and converted to heat; some absorbed by chlorophyll and converted to chemical energy in photosynthesis
  • IR: emitted by the warm surface (long-wave); absorbed by greenhouse gases (CO2, water vapour, methane) and converted to heat; the warm atmosphere re-emits IR to the surface and reduces conduction losses; some escapes to space
/ 6
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Greenhouse gases absorb most of the IR emitted by the surface and re-radiate it in all directions, including back down
  • The surface is repeatedly warmed and re-emits, so the total IR exchanged between surface and atmosphere exceeds the incoming solar energy; only the net loss (70%) balances the absorbed solar energy at equilibrium
/ 2
07.4 (1 marks)
  • The atmosphere forms layers (troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere) with different temperature gradients because energy is absorbed at different altitudes (eg UV heating the stratosphere, surface heating the troposphere)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 87-89; AQA spec 3.2.1.1.

  • Minimum ozone fell steeply from about 225 DU in 1979 to about 95 DU in the mid 1990s
  • Since then it has fluctuated around 100-115 DU with a slight recovery / no further decline
/ 2
08.2 (2 marks)
  • (225 - 95) / 225 x 100
  • = 57.8% (accept 58%)
/ 2
08.3 (4 marks)
  • Stratospheric temperatures over Antarctica are the lowest on Earth so stratospheric clouds / ice crystals form
  • Ice crystal surfaces allow reactions that convert the chlorine released from CFCs into chlorine molecules (Cl2) during the dark winter

  • When sunlight returns in spring (September) it splits the chlorine molecules into free radicals that destroy ozone; levels recover by December as the vortex breaks down

Question 1010 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 266-272 · NUT-07
[9 marks]10.1Explain how organic wastes and inorganic nutrients cause water pollution and evaluate the effectiveness of the methods used to control them.
[1 mark]10.2Give one reason why algae cause eutrophication problems more quickly than rooted plants when nutrients are added to a lake.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Organic nutrients (sewage, manure, silage effluent, paper mill / tannery / food processing effluent) are food for aerobic microbes whose respiration deoxygenates the water, killing fish and invertebrates; bacterial growths cover the bed, blocking light
  • Pathogens in sewage (cholera, typhoid, dysentery) spread via contaminated water; decay of organic matter releases inorganic nutrients
  • Inorganic nutrients: nitrates (fertiliser / manure runoff, leaching) and phosphates (sewage effluent, detergents, eroded soil) cause cultural eutrophication: algal blooms, shading of macrophytes, cyanobacterial toxins, night-time and end-of-summer deoxygenation
  • Human health: nitrites and methaemoglobinaemia; possible carcinogen via nitrosamines
  • Control of organic wastes: sewage treatment (pre-treatment, primary sedimentation removing >95% organic matter, aeration / activated sludge or trickling filters, anaerobic digestion of sludge with biogas)
  • Control of nutrients: tertiary phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate, denitrification in filter beds, micro-strainers / UV; Nitrate Vulnerable Zones, fertiliser timing / amount, low-solubility fertilisers, legumes, manure management; dredging sediments
  • Evaluation: sewage treatment has virtually eliminated deoxygenation and waterborne disease in developed countries but storm overflows and combined sewers still discharge untreated sewage; tertiary treatment is expensive so only used at sensitive sites; diffuse agricultural nitrate is hard to control and aquifers respond slowly; phosphate legacy in sediments
  • Judgement on point-source (sewage) control being more successful than diffuse-source (agriculture) control
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Algae / cyanobacteria absorb nutrients directly from the water (not the sediments) so they use dissolved nutrients more easily and grow rapidly
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 266-272; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
