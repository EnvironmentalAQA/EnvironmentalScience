Question 110 marks · Ionising radiation · spec 3.4.3.2.14 · Genn pp. 299-301 · RAD-01

Table 1 shows some radioactive isotopes, their half-lives and the type of radiation they emit.

Table 1

Iodine-1318 daysBeta / gammaFission in reactors
Strontium-9029 yearsBetaFission in reactors
Caesium-13730 yearsBeta / gammaFission in reactors

[1 mark]01.1Define the term half-life.
[2 marks]01.2A sample of iodine-131 has an activity of 6400 Bq. Calculate its activity after 32 days.
[3 marks]01.3Use Table 1 to explain why isotopes with half-lives of several decades, such as caesium-137, cause the greatest concern after a nuclear accident.
[3 marks]01.4Explain why plutonium-239 is relatively safe outside the body but very dangerous if inhaled or ingested.
[1 mark]01.5Explain what is meant by an activation product.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (1 marks)
  • The time taken for half of the atoms of a radioactive isotope to decay (activity halves)
/ 1
01.2 (2 marks)
  • 32 / 8 = 4 half-lives
  • 6400 / 24 = 400 Bq
/ 2
01.3 (3 marks)
  • Short half-life isotopes (I-131) release all their radiation quickly: dangerous but only briefly so short-term precautions (potassium iodide tablets, evacuation) are sufficient
  • Very long half-life isotopes (U-238) emit only small amounts of radiation in any period so the level of danger is low
  • Decades-long half-lives combine a high rate of radiation release with persistence for most or all of a human lifetime, so short-term protection is inadequate (land contaminated for generations)
/ 3
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Alpha particles have very low penetrating power and are absorbed by clothing / dead skin so external sources cause little exposure
  • Inside the body the alpha particles are absorbed in a very small mass of tissue so damage is concentrated (high RBE - weighting factor 20)
  • Long half-life means it continues to emit for the rest of the person's life / cannot be excreted quickly (contamination not just exposure)
/ 3
01.5 (1 marks)
  • A previously stable nucleus that becomes radioactive after absorbing a neutron (eg in reactor structures), later decaying to emit ionising radiation
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 299-301; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.14.

  • So animals behave naturally and nests are not deserted; monitoring continues 24 hours

Question 610 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.3 · Genn pp. 164-166 · SOIL-06
[5 marks]06.1Complete Table 1 to explain how each method reduces soil erosion.
Table 1

[2 marks]06.2Explain why long-term crops such as tea, coffee or orchards reduce erosion compared with annual arable crops.
[3 marks]06.3Describe three ways livestock can be managed to reduce soil erosion.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (5 marks)
  • Terracing: sloping land divided into narrow level fields held by retaining walls along contours; water slows as it crosses each terrace and infiltrates instead of running off
  • Tied ridging: on nearly flat land a criss-cross of intersecting ridges retains rainwater, increasing infiltration and preventing runoff
  • Windbreaks: hedgerows / tree rows reduce wind velocity (for up to 30 times their height) so the wind has less kinetic energy to carry particles; protect bare fields after harvest
  • Mulching: organic matter on the surface protects soil from raindrop impact and wind; decomposes to humus which binds particles; retains moisture
  • Strip cropping: strips of different crops harvested at different times (at 90° to the wind) so there is always a crop protecting part of the field; slows runoff
/ 5
06.2 (2 marks)
  • No frequent replanting / soil disturbance (ploughing) that breaks up soil structure and exposes particles
  • Permanent root systems and canopy cover protect the soil all year
/ 2
06.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Control stocking density so vegetation regrows faster than it is eaten
  • Restrict access to vulnerable areas such as river banks (fencing)
  • Move / remove livestock at high-risk times, eg after heavy rain when soil is wet
  • Rotational grazing to rest pastures
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 164-166; AQA spec 3.2.5.3.

[3 marks]07.1Explain why clay particles do not settle out in the sedimentation stage, and describe how they are removed in stage 4.
[2 marks]07.2Explain the purpose of aeration (stage 3).
[2 marks]07.3Explain why ozone or UV light may be used for sterilisation instead of chlorine when water comes from peaty upland sources.
[3 marks]07.4Describe how each of the following contaminants is removed from water.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • Clay particles are very small and carry electrostatic charges on their surfaces so they repel each other and stay suspended
  • Flocculants (aluminium sulfate / alum or polyelectrolytes) are added and mixed to neutralise the charges
  • The particles clump together (flocs) and settle in the clarifier tank
/ 3
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Increases dissolved oxygen and removes hydrogen sulfide (bad-egg smell) from anaerobic sources
  • Oxidises some dissolved metals (eg iron) so they become insoluble and can be removed; improves taste
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Peaty water contains organic matter which reacts with chlorine to produce toxic substances (eg trihalomethanes)
  • Ozone / UV kill pathogens without forming these by-products (though chlorine / chloramine keeps water sterile during distribution)
/ 2
07.4 (3 marks)
  • Ion exchange: toxic ions adsorb onto polymer resin beads or zeolite (also used to soften water by removing calcium / magnesium)
  • Reverse osmosis / distillation (desalination) - expensive, energy-intensive
  • Activated carbon: organic molecules causing odour / taste and pesticides adsorb onto the high-surface-area carbon particles
/ 3

Question 810 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 214-215 · REN-06

Figure 1 shows the water level inside and outside a tidal barrage over one 12.5-hour tidal cycle and the periods when electricity is generated.

Figure 1
[2 marks]08.1Use Figure 1 to explain when electricity is generated by the barrage.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why tidal power is intermittent but predictable.
[2 marks]08.3Explain the difference between spring and neap tides and why this affects output.
[2 marks]08.4Explain how a tidal lagoon can be used to generate electricity when there is no tidal flow.
[2 marks]08.5Give two coastal features that make a site suitable for tidal power.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • When there is a difference in level between the sea and the basin: on the ebb (about 6-8 hours) water flows out through the turbines
  • Also possible on the flood when the sea is higher than the basin; no generation when levels are equal (slack water)
/ 2
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Two periods of slack water in each 12.5-hour cycle (twice in about 25 hours) give no flow; the timing does not match demand
  • Tides depend on the positions of the Moon and Sun which are known, so output can be forecast years ahead and alternative supplies planned
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Spring tides: Sun and Moon aligned so their gravitational effects combine giving a large tidal range; neap: effects oppose giving a small range
  • Energy depends on the mass of water and height difference, so output is much greater at springs
/ 2
08.4 (2 marks)
  • When demand is low, surplus electricity pumps water into the lagoon above sea level
  • The stored water is released through the turbines later when demand is high (pumped storage)
/ 2
08.5 (2 marks)
  • Large tidal range (eg Severn Estuary)
  • Estuaries, bays or headlands / channels that focus the flow and increase velocity
/ 2

Question 1010 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.3-3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 183-196 · FN-07
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the extent to which new technologies could allow fossil fuels and nuclear fission to continue to supply a large proportion of the world's energy sustainably.
[1 mark]10.2State the approximate percentage of natural gas in a reservoir that can be recovered using its own pressure.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Fossil fuel technologies: secondary / tertiary recovery raising recovery from 20% to 60%; directional drilling and subsea wells; ROVs; fracking for tight oil and gas; tar sands / oil shales; coal gasification / liquefaction; enhanced gas recovery; methane hydrates; CCS (pre / post combustion, storage)
  • Limits: still finite (discoveries declining since the 1980s); rising energy and financial cost of unconventional sources; environmental impacts (aquifers, water use, tremors, land take, habitat damage); CO2 and other pollution; CCS unproven at scale and impractical for vehicles
  • Nuclear: new uranium extraction (polymer adsorption from seawater, phosphate mining, coal ash); molten salt reactors; plutonium breeder and thorium reactors increasing fuel use many-fold; fusion as a long-term prospect
  • Limits: cost overruns, decommissioning, waste, public opposition, proliferation, embodied energy of low-grade uranium; fusion not commercial
  • Sustainability criteria: maintaining supply without unacceptable environmental, economic or social impact; comparison with renewables and energy conservation; energy gap argument
  • Judgement: technologies can extend supplies and reduce some impacts but cannot make non-renewables sustainable indefinitely; role as transition / base-load alongside renewables
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • 80-90%
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 183-196; AQA spec 3.3.3-3.3.4.1.
