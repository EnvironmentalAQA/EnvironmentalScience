Question 210 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-06
[3 marks]02.1Describe three methods of reducing seabird by-catch in long-line fisheries.
[3 marks]02.2Explain how restricting fishing effort and restricting fishing methods reduce overfishing.
[2 marks]02.3Describe how captive rearing and release ('population seeding') can increase wild populations of lobsters or oysters.
[2 marks]02.4Explain why collecting data on planktonic fish eggs and larvae helps to prevent population collapse.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Sinkers / weighted lines so hooks sink quickly below the reach of albatrosses
  • Setting lines at night when birds are not feeding
  • Bird-scaring streamers / decoys
  • Curved hooks that catch tuna but not birds
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
  • Effort: limits on boat size, engine power, days at sea, number of licences; decommissioning payments to reduce fleet size where overfishing is unavoidable
  • Methods: bans or length limits on drift nets; bans on demersal trawling over sensitive seabed (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank deep-water reefs)
  • Shorter trawl tows so turtles / by-catch survive (over 99% survive under 10 minutes)
/ 3
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Eggs / larvae are reared in captivity through the planktonic stage when most die (predation, failure to find habitat)
  • Juveniles are released when survival chances are good, increasing recruitment to the adult population
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
  • Egg / larval survival varies greatly between years so adult numbers do not predict recruitment
  • Several poor years would lower the MSY; egg surveys allow catches to be reduced in advance rather than after a sudden collapse
/ 2

Question 310 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.2 · Genn pp. 6-8 · LIFE-05

Table 1 shows some events in the history of life on Earth.

Table 1
EventTime before present / billion years
Formation of the Earth4.6
First life (Archaea) on Earth3.5
First photosynthetic organisms2.7
Oxygen concentration in the atmosphere starts to rise2.45
End of the period of oxygen build-up (Proterozoic)0.54
[1 mark]03.1Use Table 1 to calculate how many million years passed between the appearance of photosynthetic organisms and the start of the rise in atmospheric oxygen.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why atmospheric oxygen did not start to rise as soon as photosynthesis began.
[3 marks]03.3Explain how the rise in atmospheric oxygen made life on the land surface possible.
[2 marks]03.4Explain how early photosynthetic organisms helped to prevent a long-term rise in the Earth's temperature.
[2 marks]03.5Give two other ways in which living organisms changed the conditions on Earth.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (1 marks)
  • 2.7 - 2.45 = 0.25 billion = 250 million years
/ 1
03.2 (2 marks)
  • For millions of years all the oxygen produced reacted with (dissolved) iron in the oceans
  • Only when all the iron had reacted did surplus dissolved oxygen build up in the oceans and then escape into the atmosphere
/ 2
03.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Oxygen in the atmosphere absorbed ultraviolet light
  • A dynamic equilibrium of reactions between O, O2 and O3 (the ozone layer) developed
  • Less UV reached the surface so organisms were no longer restricted to water (which had absorbed the UV)
  • Aerobic respiration became possible so more complex / larger organisms (plants and animals) evolved; many anaerobic Archaea and bacteria died out
/ 3
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Photoautotrophs absorbed carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, and some of the carbon was stored in geological sediments such as carbonate rocks and fossil fuels (carbon sequestration)
  • The lower CO2 concentration reduced the retention of heat, offsetting the increase in the brightness of the Sun (about 10% per billion years)
/ 2
03.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Biogeochemical cycles developed so small amounts of nutrient elements could support life for long periods without depletion
  • Transpiration by land plants returned water vapour to the atmosphere and increased rainfall in other areas
  • Regulation of atmospheric composition (eg oxygen / CO2 by photosynthesis and respiration)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 6-8; AQA spec 3.1.1.2.
Question 410 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4 · Genn pp. 384-389 · SUS-09

The Isle of Eigg (area 31 km2, population about 100) is not connected to the national grid. Table 1 shows its electricity sources.

Table 1
SourceCapacity / kW
Hydroelectric (3 generators)110
Solar PV50
Wind (4 turbines)24
Diesel generators (back-up)160
[3 marks]04.1Explain why Eigg uses a mix of three renewable resources rather than one, and why diesel back-up is still needed.
[3 marks]04.2Describe three other ways in which the Eigg community has increased its sustainability.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why small self-contained communities often find it easier to adopt sustainable lifestyles than large cities.
[2 marks]04.4Explain how large-scale DC electricity grids such as Desertec could increase the use of renewable energy.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Diversity: HEP (wet winters), solar (summer) and wind peak at different times so supply is more reliable - diverse systems resist change
  • No single source could meet demand all year; battery storage on the island grid smooths fluctuations
  • Calm, dry, dull periods still leave gaps so diesel generators provide security (fossil fuel use minimised, not yet eliminated)
/ 3
04.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Community ownership (Isle of Eigg Trust with Scottish Wildlife Trust) managing the island for people and wildlife
  • Wood biomass heating; insulation such as straw-bale housing; some electric vehicles
  • Habitat protection and creation: fencing rare plants from grazing, planting indigenous woodland (rowan, birch, willow); eagles and hen harriers nesting
  • Recyclables shipped off; garden / food waste composted; low-impact food production; beach litter collection
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Small groups can organise, agree and act quickly; decisions benefit people who see the results directly
  • Resources and wastes are local and visible so feedback is immediate, unlike cities supplied from far away
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Renewable energy is abundant but geographically and temporally variable; a continental grid moves electricity from areas of surplus (desert solar, offshore wind) to areas of demand
  • DC grids do not need every generator synchronised in phase as AC grids do, so coordination across many countries is easier
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 384-389; AQA spec 3.6.4.
Question 510 marks · Dynamic equilibria, feedback & tipping points · spec 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 370-372 · SUS-01
[1 mark]05.1State the Brundtland Commission (1987) definition of sustainable development.
[3 marks]05.2Explain why activities such as slash-and-burn agriculture, fishing and sewage discharge can be sustainable with a small human population but not with a large one.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why urban populations are often less aware of unsustainable exploitation than rural communities.
[4 marks]05.4Describe two negative feedback mechanisms in natural systems and explain how human responses can override them.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • Development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs
/ 1
05.2 (3 marks)
  • Impact depends on total population x per capita impact
  • With few people, natural processes (forest regrowth, fish recruitment, aerobic decomposition / dilution) recover faster than the resource is used or the waste added
  • Larger populations exceed the rate of natural recovery: forest and soil do not regenerate, catches exceed MSY, water deoxygenates
/ 3
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Urban areas are supported by resources brought in from elsewhere; when one area is depleted, exploitation simply moves (timber, fish, minerals, energy) so consumers see no shortage
  • Rural communities exploit local resources directly so the effects of over-exploitation become obvious quickly, encouraging cultural limits to need
/ 2
05.4 (4 marks)
  • Climate: warming increases evaporation, cloud and albedo; higher CO2 increases photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle: more evaporation gives more precipitation; populations: density-dependent factors return numbers to carrying capacity
  • In agriculture soil degradation, water shortage and loss of predators would reduce yields (negative feedback limiting the activity)
  • Humans respond with fertilisers, irrigation and pesticides to maintain yields, further damaging the natural systems that support production
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 370-372; AQA spec 3.6.1.

Question 710 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 33-35 · CBR-02

The Hawaiian goose (nene) was reduced by hunting, habitat loss and introduced predators to about 30 birds. A captive breeding programme started with just seven individuals and has increased the wild population to over 2000.

[4 marks]07.1Explain why starting the programme with seven individuals caused problems, and describe how these problems are managed.
[3 marks]07.2Explain three reasons, other than gene pool size, why species kept in captivity may fail to breed.
[3 marks]07.3Describe how artificial insemination and embryo transfer increase breeding success in captive mammals.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • Very small gene pool so individuals are closely related and inbreeding is likely
  • Harmful recessive genes are expressed when both parents carry them: some goslings had thin hair-like feathers with poor insulation so survival in cold weather was low
  • A stud book records family trees so breeding is arranged between the least related individuals (often coordinated by a specialist zoo)
  • Exchange of individuals / semen between zoos; avoid hybridisation with related species / subspecies
/ 4
07.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Breeding is triggered by stimuli (day length, light, temperature, food, body fat) which may be unknown / not provided
  • Population interactions: mixing breeding and non-breeding individuals causes conflict; some need a choice of partners, others pair for life
  • Breeding habitat needed: courtship display sites, nest sites, social groups (flamingos only breed in large groups - mirrors used)
  • Stress / disturbance
/ 3
07.3 (3 marks)
  • AI: semen collected (and can be stored / cryopreserved for years) and inserted into the female, so animals need not be transported or meet; avoids dangerous mating (elephants, rhinos) and partner rejection
  • Embryo transfer: a female of the endangered species (eg bongo) is treated with hormones to release many eggs, which are fertilised and implanted into surrogate females of a common related species (eland)
  • So many offspring are produced per cycle instead of one calf per year, overcoming the shortage of breeding females
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 33-35; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 810 marks · Energy & material cycles: natural vs human systems · spec 3.6.3 · Genn pp. 373-374 · SUS-04
[4 marks]08.1Explain how natural material cycles differ from linear human systems of material use.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why the use of toxic metals in electronic products is a particular problem for sustainability.
[4 marks]08.3Describe how the design of a product can move it from a linear to a cyclical system.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (4 marks)
  • Natural cycles use a small number of abundant elements (C, H, O, N, P) built into monomers and polymers (carbohydrates, proteins); wastes of one process are raw materials for another so nothing is depleted or accumulates
  • Natural wastes are biodegradable / non-toxic and are broken down by decomposers into raw materials
  • Human systems are linear: extract - process - manufacture - use - discard; minerals are dispersed after use or mixed so recovery is difficult
  • Human wastes are often persistent and toxic (organochlorines, PCBs, heavy metals in electronics) and build up; fossil fuel use is inherently linear and unsustainable
/ 4
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Metals are dispersed in small amounts within complex mixed products so they cannot easily be separated / recovered at end of life
  • They leach from landfill or are released by incineration; persistent, bioaccumulating
/ 2
08.3 (4 marks)
  • Design for durability, repair and re-use (replaceable parts, no built-in batteries, spare parts and support, no planned obsolescence)
  • Easy identification of materials (coding) and separation of components so they can be recycled
  • Separation of technical materials (metals, plastics) from biological materials (which can be composted / digested)
  • Use of recycled and recyclable / biodegradable materials; reduced packaging and material use (Cradle to Cradle)
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 373-374; AQA spec 3.6.3.
Question 910 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 67-71 · HAB-11

Figure 1 shows a simplified Antarctic food web.

[3 marks]09.1Explain why the sea around Antarctica is so productive despite the cold.
[3 marks]09.2Use Figure 1 to explain why fishing for krill and for Patagonian toothfish threatens other species.
[3 marks]09.3Explain the importance of Antarctica in controlling global natural cycles.
[1 mark]09.4Explain why the area of winter sea ice around Antarctica has increased in recent years even though air and sea are warming.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
  • Upwelling currents bring nutrients from the seabed to surface waters
  • 24-hour daylight in summer allows a huge phytoplankton bloom
  • Algae also grow under sea ice where krill shelter and feed; krill support most of the food web
/ 3
09.2 (3 marks)
  • Krill is the main food of baleen whales, penguins, seals and fish so over-harvesting removes food from most of the web
  • Toothfish overfishing removes the food of sperm whales and elephant seals
  • Long-lining for toothfish catches and drowns albatrosses on the hooks (by-catch)
/ 3
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Ice on land stores 70% of the Earth's fresh water, keeping sea levels low
  • High albedo of ice reflects sunlight, limiting warming (reduction in ice area causes further warming)
  • Carbon sequestration: carbon in algae eaten by krill sinks to the seabed in krill faeces
/ 3
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Stronger winds spreading ice; more snowfall from warmer, moister air freezing onto slush; fresh meltwater from land forming a surface layer that freezes more easily (long-term trend still expected to be a decline)
/ 1

Question 1015 marks · Impacts of agriculture, social/political factors & sustainable agriculture · spec 3.5.1.5 · Genn pp. 335-337 · AGI-07
[2 marks]10.1Explain why the potential to increase food supplies by cultivating new land is limited.
[2 marks]10.2Explain why importing food may reduce the availability of food in the area where it is produced.
[2 marks]10.3Explain why organic agriculture is more than simply 'not using pesticides and artificial fertilisers'.
[9 marks]10.4Evaluate the strategies that could be used to make agriculture more sustainable.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (2 marks)
  • About 10% of land is already cultivated and 25% grazed
  • Most remaining land is too dry, wet, cold, hot or steep, is covered in ice, or has no soil
/ 2
10.2 (2 marks)
  • Land becomes too expensive for local people to rent / is used to grow export crops for more affluent societies
  • So people with less money lose access to the land and food they used to produce
/ 2
10.3 (2 marks)
  • It uses natural processes to supply nutrients (legumes, recycled organic matter, soil biota), control pests and provide ecological services
  • So that pesticides and artificial fertilisers become unnecessary rather than just being banned
/ 2
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Unsustainable features: reliance on chemical pesticides (resistance, non-target impacts, bans), non-renewable rock phosphate, fossil-fuel-intensive nitrate manufacture, over-exploitation of rivers / groundwater, salinisation, loss of soil organic matter, greenhouse gases from fuel, rice and livestock, loss of species providing ecosystem services
  • Pest control: cultural methods (weeding, mulching, crop rotation, barrier crops, culling), biological control, predator habitats, polyculture / companion crops, integrated control, reduced antibiotic use

  • Biodiversity: conserve habitats of crop wild relatives, seed banks, retain hedgerows, ditches, ponds, woodland; agri-environment schemes
  • Evaluation: growing population (perhaps 12 billion) needs more food; lower trophic level diets; obstacles - cost, need for support, gaining acceptance of new techniques; learning from past land degradation (fertile crescent, US mid-west)
  • Judgement: no single strategy sufficient; combination of ecological principles and new technology; justified conclusion
