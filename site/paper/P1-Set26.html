Question 110 marks · Noise pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.13 · Genn pp. 293-295, 418 · NOISE-05

A motor-racing circuit has residents living 500 m away. The circuit operator wants to compare two designs of acoustic barrier: an earth baffle mound and a timber acoustic fence.

[5 marks]01.1Describe how you would carry out an investigation to compare the effectiveness of the two barriers.
[1 mark]01.2Explain why readings should be taken with the same wind direction.
[2 marks]01.3Explain the difference between the LA10 and LA90 measures of traffic noise.
[2 marks]01.4Outline two ways in which the residents' houses could be designed to reduce the noise reaching occupants.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (5 marks)
  • Use a calibrated sound meter (dB(A)) at fixed distances (eg 10, 50, 100, 250, 500 m) from the track behind each barrier and at a control site with no barrier
  • Standardise the noise source: same cars / same race or a standard test noise; same time of day, wind speed / direction, weather; same height of meter above ground
  • Take repeated readings at each distance and calculate means (and standard deviation) to assess variability
  • Use a measure such as LA10 or Leq over a set period rather than a single peak reading
  • Compare reduction in dB relative to the control at each distance; plot noise level against distance for each barrier; statistical test (t-test) to assess significance
/ 5
01.2 (1 marks)
  • Wind carries sound / affects how far it travels so different wind directions would change readings independently of the barrier
/ 1
01.3 (2 marks)
  • LA10 is the noise level exceeded for 10% of the period - a measure of the noisiest periods
  • LA90 is the level exceeded for 90% of the period - a measure of the quietest / background level
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Double / triple glazing
  • Acoustic insulation in walls / roof
  • Bedrooms on the side facing away from the track
  • Dense hedges / trees and earth mounds in gardens
/ 2

Question 410 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 200-202 · REN-02
[3 marks]04.1Explain how a ground-source heat pump can deliver more heat energy to a building than the electrical energy used to run it.
[3 marks]04.2Explain how Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) with thermal storage overcomes the intermittency of solar power.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why CSP can only be used in areas with little cloud, whereas PV can be used anywhere.
[2 marks]04.4Describe two environmental impacts of solar PV.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • A fluid in a buried ground loop absorbs low-temperature heat from the ground; at the expansion valve the fluid boils at low pressure absorbing this heat
  • A compressor raises the pressure so the gas condenses, releasing heat at a higher temperature inside the building
  • The electricity only moves heat rather than creating it, so up to four times as much heat is delivered as electricity used (less if the source is colder)
/ 3
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Parabolic reflectors focus sunlight onto tubes of oil, increasing the energy density and reaching high temperatures
  • The heat is transferred to molten salt (potassium nitrate) stored in large insulated tanks at up to 550 °C
  • The stored heat boils water to drive steam turbines whenever electricity is required, including at night
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Reflectors can only focus parallel rays of direct sunlight; cloud, dust and smoke scatter light so it cannot be concentrated
  • PV cells convert diffuse as well as direct light, though output is lower
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Manufacture: extraction / processing of silicon, metals and plastics; toxic wastes (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium)
  • Land take by large solar farms competing with other uses / affecting desert flora and fauna
  • Water for cleaning panels in arid areas
  • Disposal of old panels
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 200-202; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 510 marks · Principles of pollution control (CPA, CGM, new technologies) · spec 3.4.3.1, 3.4.3.3 · Genn pp. 244, 303-304 · PCON-04

Table 1 shows four general approaches to pollution control.

[4 marks]05.1Complete Table 1 by giving one example of each approach.
Table 1
ApproachExample
Production prevention
Prevention of release
Post-release remediation
Alternative processes
[2 marks]05.2Explain why prevention of production is generally preferable to post-release remediation.
[4 marks]05.3Describe how each of the following new technologies helps to control pollution.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • Production prevention: desulfurisation of fuels before combustion; low-temperature (fluidised bed) combustion to prevent NOx; smokeless fuels
  • Prevention of release: electrostatic precipitators / bag filters / cyclones for smoke; catalytic converters; flue gas desulfurisation; bund walls; landfill liners
  • Post-release remediation: oil spill clean-up (booms, skimmers, dispersants, bioremediation); phytoremediation of metal-contaminated land; liming of acidified lakes
  • Alternative processes: electric vehicles instead of petrol / diesel; renewable energy instead of fossil fuels; pyrethroids instead of organochlorines; fuel cells instead of internal combustion engines
/ 4
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Remediation is often incomplete / cannot recover all the pollutant (eg oil spills, dispersed pollutants), so some damage still occurs
  • Prevention avoids the damage entirely and is usually cheaper than clean-up / avoids polluter-pays costs; moving from post-production treatment to non-release is a circular economy principle
/ 2
05.3 (4 marks)
  • Satellites detect and track the extent / movement of oil slicks over large areas, quickly and repeatedly, so clean-up can be directed and polluters identified
  • GPS tracking monitors ship positions so navigation errors / collisions / groundings are reduced and offshore shipping routes are enforced
  • Polymers with a high surface area adsorb metal ions from contaminated water / effluent so they can be removed and recovered
  • Plants that absorb heavy metals (hyperaccumulators) are grown on contaminated land, harvested and disposed of / metals recovered, cleaning the soil
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 244, 303-304; AQA spec 3.4.3.1, 3.4.3.3.
Question 610 marks · The hydrosphere: hydrological cycle & unsustainable exploitation · spec 3.2.2.1-3.2.2.2 · Genn pp. 119-121 · HYD-05
[3 marks]06.1Explain why abstraction from an aquifer containing 'fossil' (palaeo) water, such as the High Plains / Ogallala Aquifer in the USA, cannot be sustainable.
[3 marks]06.2Describe how the GRACE satellites are used to monitor aquifer depletion and give one advantage over monitoring boreholes.
[3 marks]06.3Describe three measures being introduced on the North China Plain to reduce aquifer depletion.
[1 mark]06.4Suggest why large-scale afforestation on the North China Plain may reduce aquifer recharge even though trees reduce soil erosion.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • The water was recharged during or before the last ice age (10 000-20 000 years ago) when the climate was wetter
  • Current recharge is little or none so any abstraction reduces the stored volume
  • The large volume means shortages take a long time to appear, but eventually the supply runs out; ancient water is often saline, causing soil salinisation
/ 3
06.2 (3 marks)
  • Two satellites whose orbits are affected by the force of gravity
  • The mass of water in aquifers below changes gravity, so changes in the orbit / distance between the satellites indicate changes in groundwater mass
  • Advantage: the whole Earth is surveyed every 30 days, whereas borehole water-table measurements are slow and hard to coordinate over large areas
/ 3
06.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Low-pressure irrigation pipes to reduce leaks
  • Growing crops that need less water than wheat / maize
  • Charging consumers by volume used (metering)
  • Inter-basin transfer of water 450 km from the Yangtze
/ 3
06.4 (1 marks)
  • Trees intercept rainfall and transpire large volumes, reducing soil moisture and the water percolating down to the aquifer
/ 1

Question 815 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 103-104, 371-372 · CC-07
[2 marks]08.1Explain the difference between a negative and a positive feedback mechanism.
[4 marks]08.2Describe two negative feedback mechanisms that may reduce global warming.
[4 marks]08.3Explain how melting permafrost and melting methane hydrate act as positive feedback mechanisms.
[3 marks]08.4Give three other positive feedback mechanisms.
[2 marks]08.5Explain what is meant by a climate tipping point.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • Negative feedback: a change causes other changes that reduce the rate or size of the original change, re-establishing equilibrium
  • Positive feedback: a change causes other changes that increase the original change
/ 2
08.2 (4 marks)
  • Increased low-level cloud: higher temperatures increase evaporation and condensation, producing more cloud; clouds have a high albedo so more sunlight is reflected and warming is reduced
  • Increased photosynthesis: higher temperatures and CO2 increase photosynthesis, removing CO2; if stored in woody tissue atmospheric CO2 rises less
/ 4
08.3 (4 marks)
  • Permafrost: frozen waterlogged Arctic soils contain dead organic matter that decomposed anaerobically producing methane trapped in the frozen ground
  • Warming thaws the permafrost, releasing methane (a powerful greenhouse gas) and allowing further decomposition, causing more warming
  • Methane hydrate: methane from decomposed organic matter in deep sea sediments is held as a solid under high pressure and low temperature
  • If deep water warms the hydrate melts, releasing methane into the atmosphere and causing further warming
/ 4
08.4 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Reduced albedo as snow / ice melts
  • Ocean acidification reducing coral survival and carbon sequestration
  • Increased forest and peat fires releasing CO2
  • Faster decomposition of soil organic matter releasing CO2
  • Increased water vapour (a greenhouse gas) in warmer air
  • Increased cirrus (high) cloud which traps infrared
/ 3
08.5 (2 marks)
  • A point at which natural processes triggered by human-caused warming become self-sustaining / unstoppable
  • So that stopping the original human activity would no longer stop climate change (eg runaway permafrost melt, methane hydrate release, forest fires)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 103-104, 371-372; AQA spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1.

Question 1010 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 236-240 · PPRO-07
[9 marks]10.1Discuss how an understanding of the properties of pollutants can be used to predict their behaviour in the environment and to plan strategies to control them.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by bioaccumulation.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • State of matter: solids deposit near the source, gases disperse widely in the atmosphere; density (lead dust, hydrogen cyanide at Bhopal) controls how far particles/gases travel
  • Persistence / half-life: persistent pollutants (organochlorines, CFCs, heavy metals, radioactive wastes with long half-lives) travel further, accumulate and need containment rather than dilution; non-persistent pollutants (sewage, organophosphates) can be allowed to degrade
  • Toxicity, specificity, mode of action (enzyme inhibition, neurotoxins) determine acceptable concentrations and which species are at risk; LD50 / MDAF / MDNF used to set doses
  • Solubility in water (nitrates disperse and leach - control by nitrate vulnerable zones) vs liposolubility (DDT, mercury bioaccumulate and biomagnify - control by bans / restrictions)
  • Reactivity: reactive pollutants may degrade quickly or form secondary pollutants (ozone, acid rain, PANs) - controlling primary pollutants (catalytic converters, FGD) prevents secondary pollutants
  • Adsorption onto clay / sediments immobilises metals and caesium; can be used deliberately (activated carbon filters, polymer adsorption)
  • Mutagenic / carcinogenic / teratogenic action means no safe dose; exposure kept As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA)
  • Synergism (ozone + SO2; smoke + SO2) means pollutants must be considered together
  • Environmental features (temperature inversions, currents, pH, oxygen) interact with properties: Critical Pathway Analysis uses properties + environment to predict where pollutants go and to focus monitoring
  • Evaluation: properties of new chemicals can be predicted from similar ones (precautionary principle); understanding allows prevention rather than clean-up
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The increase in the amount / concentration of a substance within an (individual) organism over time, because intake exceeds excretion or breakdown
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 236-240; AQA spec 3.4.1.

[25 marks]11.2Mineral resources are non-renewable. Discuss how an understanding of geological processes and the development of new technologies can extend the period for which society can rely on mineral resources.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Why non-renewable: formed too slowly; geological concentration processes - hydrothermal, metamorphic, Proterozoic sediments, alluvial, evaporites, secondary enrichment, biological sediments
