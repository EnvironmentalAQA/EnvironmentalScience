Question 710 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 90-92 · CC-08
[6 marks]07.1Explain how climate change may affect the survival of species. Use named examples.
[2 marks]07.2Explain why a species living in an interdependent community may be unable to move to a new area even if suitable conditions exist there.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why polar regions warming faster than the equator affects the weather in the UK.
Show mark scheme
07.16 marks1 mark each

1 mark for each of the 6 points below.

  1. Direct effect of abiotic change: species near the edge of their range of tolerance (temperature, water) may die out locally, eg sundews if marshes dry, frogs if climate drier

    • beech (shallow roots) suffers in drought more than oak

  2. Changes in timing: earlier flowering / emergence may separate flowers from pollinators

    • plant toxins building up earlier may kill caterpillars
    • warmer winters disturb dormouse hibernation so they use fat reserves and starve

  3. Biotic changes: food supply, predators, disease vectors (mosquitoes / malaria spreading), pollinators and seed dispersers may change

  4. Distribution changes: species may colonise newly suitable areas (triton molluscs around the UK) but slow-growing species (trees) may disappear from old ranges faster than they colonise

    • suitable areas or biological corridors may not exist
    • human land use blocks movement

  5. Population fragmentation: rising seas or upward retreat to mountains isolate populations

    • divided gene pools increase inbreeding
    • small populations may not be viable
    • no repopulation from elsewhere

  6. Some species benefit (bats: warmer winters, more insects) but wetter stormier weather reduces feeding time

Also accept
  • Any other correct, relevant point of the same kind - wording never has to match these notes.
  • Any appropriate named example, including ones that are not listed here.
  • abiotic → non-living / physical factors
  • range of tolerance → the range of conditions in which a species can survive
  • Biotic → living factors / effects of other organisms

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

In the troposphere human activity enhances the greenhouse effect: fossil fuels and deforestation add CO2, rice, livestock and landfill add methane, fertilisers add nitrous oxide, and CFCs and tropospheric ozone are new absorbers. More outgoing infrared is absorbed and re-emitted, warming the surface, while smoke and aerosols scatter sunlight and land-use change alters albedo and evapotranspiration. In the stratosphere, CFCs, halons and HCFCs release chlorine and bromine that catalyse ozone destruction, shifting the O / O2 / O3 equilibrium so that less UV is absorbed there and more reaches the surface, cooling the stratosphere as well.

The consequences run through every system the atmosphere's energy drives: climate, thermohaline circulation, the hydrological cycle, sea level and species distributions, plus UV damage to organisms. Predicting them is hard because the processes are linked - one change alters others - and because feedback can amplify (albedo loss, methane release) or damp (cloud cover, photosynthesis) the effect, sometimes past tipping points. Time lags from ocean heat capacity and the long residence times of CO2 and CFCs, natural variability from solar output, volcanoes and orbital cycles, sparse historical data replaced by proxies, and models built on different assumptions all add uncertainty.

Some things are nevertheless well established: the physics of infrared absorption, the chemistry of ozone loss, and the success of the Montreal Protocol. What is uncertain is the scale and timing of regional impacts, which is exactly why the precautionary principle, not certainty, should guide action.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

Humans release greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels into the troposphere, which traps more heat and causes global warming. CFCs go into the stratosphere and destroy the ozone layer, so more UV reaches the Earth. It is hard to predict what will happen because the atmosphere is complicated and scientists do not know everything about it. There could be feedback effects that make it worse.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 gives one cause per layer; Level 3 lists several gases and processes and explains the mechanism (absorption and re-emission; catalytic destruction shifting an equilibrium).
  • 'Difficulties of prediction' is answered in Level 3 with specific reasons (linked processes, feedback, time lags, natural variability, proxy data, models); Level 1 says 'complicated'.
  • Level 3 evaluates - separating what is certain from what is not - and links this to the precautionary principle.
