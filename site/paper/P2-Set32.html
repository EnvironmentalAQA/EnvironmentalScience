Table 1 shows the numbers of breeding pairs of two seabird species on an island before and after a rat eradication programme.

[4 marks]01.1Explain why oceanic islands have many endemic species but low overall biodiversity.
[3 marks]01.2Use Table 1 to explain why introduced rats had such a severe effect on these birds.
[2 marks]01.3Explain how avian malaria threatens Hawaiian birds and how climate change worsens the threat.
[1 mark]01.4Give one other conservation measure for oceanic islands besides eradicating introduced species.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Isolation makes colonisation difficult: only marine species, seabirds, plants with floating seeds or chance arrivals (tortoises on logs, wind-blown birds) reach them
  • Whole taxa (terrestrial mammals, amphibians) may be absent so few original colonists
  • Colonists evolve in isolation under different selection pressures to fill the empty niches, producing unique species (Galapagos, Madagascar lemurs, Hawaii)
  • Endemics have small ranges and populations so are vulnerable to extinction
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • The birds evolved without mammal predators so nest on the ground / in burrows with no defence
  • Rats eat eggs and chicks; numbers fell to very low levels (45 and 12 pairs)
  • After eradication breeding success rose about 7-12 fold, confirming predation was the main limiting factor
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Introduced mosquitoes carry avian malaria to which endemic birds have no resistance; birds survive only above about 1500 m where it is too cold for malaria
  • Warming allows mosquitoes / malaria to spread to higher altitudes, shrinking the safe range
/ 2
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Control of developments and visitor numbers (biosecurity, quarantine of imports); protected areas; captive breeding of endemics

Question 310 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.1-3.1.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 10-15 · BIOV-01
[4 marks]03.1Explain what is meant by biomimetics and describe three examples.
[3 marks]03.2Explain why many plants produce chemicals that are useful as human medicines, and give two examples.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why the discovery of medicines is a strong argument for conserving whole habitats rather than individual species.
[1 mark]03.4Give one example of a wildlife species used in physiological research and the human health issue it helps to understand.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Copying the structural / functional adaptations of living organisms in the design of manufactured items
  • Aircraft wingtip fins copied from splayed wingtip feathers of soaring birds, reducing turbulence and fuel use
  • Humpback whale flipper tubercles copied on ship rudders and wind turbine blades (tighter turns, rotation at lower wind speeds)
  • Shark skin ridges reducing friction / bacterial adhesion (surface coatings, hospital surfaces)
  • Termite mound convection copied for natural ventilation of buildings; hollow strutted bird bones in lightweight roofs; gecko toe adhesion; burr hooks - Velcro; lotus leaf self-cleaning glass; spider silk for airbags / body armour; bat / dolphin echolocation - ultrasound scanners
/ 4
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Plants produce toxic chemicals (eg alkaloids) to defend themselves against herbivores; in controlled doses these affect human physiology
  • Examples: morphine / codeine from poppies; quinine from cinchona bark (malaria); aspirin from willow bark; taxol from yew (cancers); diosgenin from Mexican yam (contraceptives, cortisone)
  • Animal examples accepted: AZT from a marine sponge (HIV), shark liver lipids (tumours)
/ 3
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Only a very small proportion of species have been studied for medicinal substances so unknown species may contain valuable chemicals
  • Conserving the habitat protects all its species, including those not yet investigated (and their interdependent relationships)
/ 2
03.4 (1 marks)
  • Squid giant nerve cells - nerve function / heart disease, stroke; marsupials (kangaroo, wallaby pouch) - embryo development; hippo skin secretion - sunscreen / burns; sea urchin embryos - teratogen testing; armadillo - leprosy; marine sponge proteins - transplant rejection

Question 510 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.3 · Genn pp. 76-77 · LP-04
[3 marks]05.1Explain the difference between primary and secondary succession and why secondary succession is faster.
[3 marks]05.2Explain what is meant by deflected succession and a plagioclimax, using a named UK example.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why conservation of a plagioclimax requires continued human activity whereas conservation of a rainforest or coral reef does not.
[2 marks]05.4Explain why biodiversity tends to be low in abiotically extreme environments and high in stable ones.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Primary: succession on a newly created habitat with no life / no soil (bare rock, new pond, sand)
  • Secondary: succession restarting after an existing community is disturbed (tree felling, ploughing, fire, end of grazing)
  • Faster because soil already exists and many seeds / roots / organisms are still present so there are fewer delays for colonisation
/ 3
05.2 (3 marks)
  • Human activity (grazing, mowing, burning, coppicing) regularly removes the later successional species so the climatic climax cannot form
  • The stable community that develops under this repeated disturbance is a plagioclimax
  • Examples: chalk grassland / hay meadows maintained by grazing or mowing; lowland heath by burning; coppiced woodland; Machair grassland; Broads grazing marshes
/ 3
05.3 (2 marks)
  • If the traditional activity stops, secondary succession returns the habitat to scrub / woodland and the plagioclimax species are lost
  • Rainforest and coral reefs are climax communities so conservation aims to minimise human impact
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Extreme environments (deserts, polar) are dominated by abiotic factors; few species tolerate them; populations fluctuate and food webs are simple so changes in one food species have large effects
  • Stable environments allow many species to evolve inter-species relationships; populations are regulated by biotic factors; complex food webs give stability (rainforests, reefs)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 76-77; AQA spec 3.1.3.3.

[3 marks]08.1Use Table 1 to explain why liquid water cannot exist on the surface of Mars.
[3 marks]08.2Explain how the Earth's mass and its distance from the Sun created suitable conditions for life.
[2 marks]08.3Explain the importance of the Earth's magnetic field and its speed of rotation for living organisms.
[2 marks]08.4Explain how the tilt of the Earth's axis affects living organisms.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Mars has low mass so weak gravity and could not retain a dense atmosphere
  • Very low atmospheric pressure means water would boil / evaporate away even at low temperatures
  • Greater distance from the Sun gives low insolation and a mean temperature of -60 °C so water is frozen
/ 3
08.2 (3 marks)
  • Mass great enough for gravity to retain an atmosphere containing the elements needed (C, H, O, N as CO2, methane, ammonia, water vapour) and to maintain pressure so liquid water does not boil
  • Distance from the Sun (the 'Goldilocks zone') gives insolation producing temperatures at which water is liquid (0-35 °C over most of the surface) and proteins are not denatured
  • Too close (Venus) - too hot; too far (Mars) - too cold
/ 3
08.3 (2 marks)
  • The molten core produces a magnetosphere that deflects the solar wind / charged particles, preventing biologically damaging radiation reaching the surface
  • The 24-hour rotation limits heating by day and cooling by night, reducing temperature extremes (Venus' slow rotation gives extreme conditions)
/ 2
08.4 (2 marks)
  • The tilted axis causes seasonal variation in insolation as the Earth orbits the Sun
  • Producing seasons (temperature, day length) to which species' life cycles (breeding, migration, dormancy) are adapted; the Moon's gravity keeps the tilt stable and creates tides
/ 2

Question 910 marks · Dynamic equilibria, feedback & tipping points · spec 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 370-372 · SUS-01
[1 mark]09.1State the Brundtland Commission (1987) definition of sustainable development.
[3 marks]09.2Explain why activities such as slash-and-burn agriculture, fishing and sewage discharge can be sustainable with a small human population but not with a large one.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why urban populations are often less aware of unsustainable exploitation than rural communities.
[4 marks]09.4Describe two negative feedback mechanisms in natural systems and explain how human responses can override them.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (1 marks)
  • Development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs
/ 1
09.2 (3 marks)
  • Impact depends on total population x per capita impact
  • With few people, natural processes (forest regrowth, fish recruitment, aerobic decomposition / dilution) recover faster than the resource is used or the waste added
  • Larger populations exceed the rate of natural recovery: forest and soil do not regenerate, catches exceed MSY, water deoxygenates
/ 3
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Urban areas are supported by resources brought in from elsewhere; when one area is depleted, exploitation simply moves (timber, fish, minerals, energy) so consumers see no shortage
  • Rural communities exploit local resources directly so the effects of over-exploitation become obvious quickly, encouraging cultural limits to need
/ 2
09.4 (4 marks)
  • Climate: warming increases evaporation, cloud and albedo; higher CO2 increases photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle: more evaporation gives more precipitation; populations: density-dependent factors return numbers to carrying capacity
  • In agriculture soil degradation, water shortage and loss of predators would reduce yields (negative feedback limiting the activity)
  • Humans respond with fertilisers, irrigation and pesticides to maintain yields, further damaging the natural systems that support production
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 370-372; AQA spec 3.6.1.
