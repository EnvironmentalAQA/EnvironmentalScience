Question 110 marks · Thermal & oil pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.7 · Genn pp. 258-259 · WPOL-02
[3 marks]01.1Give three sources of oil pollution of the sea other than tanker accidents.
[3 marks]01.2Explain how an oil spill at sea can kill seabirds.
[2 marks]01.3Explain two ways in which an oil slick can harm organisms that live in the water beneath it.
[2 marks]01.4Explain why oil pollution is most serious when it occurs inshore or in an enclosed body of water.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Discharge of tank-washing water from tankers
  • Oil rig / drilling accidents / leakage during drilling
  • Pipeline leaks
  • Leakage from storage tanks / refinery spills
  • Waste lubricating oil from vehicles / industry washed into drains
  • Other ship accidents (fuel oil)
/ 3
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Oil sticks feathers together so they lose their insulating properties and birds die of hypothermia
  • Feathers lose buoyancy / waterproofing so heavily oiled birds drown
  • Birds swallow toxic oil when preening; time spent cleaning feathers reduces time for feeding themselves / their young
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • The surface film reduces the diffusion of oxygen from the atmosphere into the water, so aerobic organisms suffer from low dissolved oxygen
  • Toxic components (benzene, xylene) dissolve and poison plankton near the surface
  • Oil blocks light so algae / phytoplankton cannot photosynthesise
  • Oil masks odours / narcotises so animals cannot find mates or food by smell
  • Shore organisms (molluscs, crustaceans) are covered and asphyxiated
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Dispersal / dilution by currents and waves is prevented so oil stays concentrated; in open water most spills disperse in days or weeks
  • Inshore areas have more sensitive ecosystems (shores, reefs, mangroves, breeding sites) and more human activities (fishing, tourism) to be affected
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 258-259; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.7.

Question 310 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.2 · Genn pp. 228-232 · ECON-05
[6 marks]03.1Explain how each of the following building design features reduces energy use.
[2 marks]03.2Compare the embodied energy of concrete with alternative building materials.
[2 marks]03.3Describe how a heat exchanger allows a building to be ventilated with minimal heat loss.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (6 marks)
  • Orientation: south-facing windows (northern hemisphere) maximise passive solar gains; rooms needing warmth on the sunny side; fewer / smaller windows on the north side where losses are greatest; solar screens / brise soleil prevent summer overheating
  • Low SA:V (compact shapes, terraced / joined buildings, fewer storeys of small footprint) means less external surface per unit volume through which heat is lost
  • Earth sheltering: the ground is warmer than winter air and shields the building from wind, so conduction and wind-chill losses are reduced
/ 6
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Cement manufacture (heating limestone) is very energy intensive so concrete has high embodied energy
  • Limecrete has about half the embodied energy of concrete; rammed earth, straw bales and lime mortar are lower still
/ 2
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Warm stale air leaving and cold fresh air entering pass through the exchanger in opposite directions (counter-current) separated by thin conducting pipes / plates with a large surface area
  • Heat transfers from the outgoing to the incoming air so fresh air enters already warmed
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 228-232; AQA spec 3.3.4.2.2.

Question 610 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 401-402 · RM-08

A student estimated the population of ground beetles in a field using the Lincoln Index. 60 beetles were caught in pitfall traps, marked with a spot of paint and released. Three days later 45 beetles were caught, of which 9 were marked.

[2 marks]06.1Calculate the estimated population size.
[4 marks]06.2State four assumptions of the Lincoln Index method.
[2 marks]06.3Explain how the estimate would be affected if the paint made the beetles more likely to be eaten by birds.
[2 marks]06.4Suggest why the second sample was taken after three days rather than the next day or after a month.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (2 marks)
  • (60 x 45) / 9
  • = 300
/ 2
06.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Marked individuals mix freely / randomly with the rest of the population
  • No births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples
  • Marking does not affect survival or behaviour (eg make them more visible to predators)
  • Marks do not wear off / fall off
  • All individuals have the same chance of being caught (traps do not attract or repel marked animals)
/ 4
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Fewer marked beetles would survive to be recaptured
  • So the proportion marked in the second sample would be too low and the population would be overestimated
/ 2
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Enough time for marked beetles to mix with the population
  • Not so long that births / deaths / migration or loss of marks change the population
/ 2

Question 1010 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 196-217 · REN-08
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the potential contribution of renewable energy resources to future energy supplies in a country such as the UK.
[1 mark]10.2State the factor by which the kinetic energy of wind increases when wind velocity doubles.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Properties of renewables: intermittency, predictability, low energy density, storage, applicability, locational constraints, impacts, technology level, economics
  • Wind: large offshore resource in shallow seas, falling costs, HAWT / VAWT / direct drive developments; intermittent, needs storage and grid; land-use conflicts
  • Solar: PV costs falling rapidly, roof space, but low winter insolation in the UK; CSP unsuitable; heat pumps and photothermal for heating
  • HEP: most large sites used; low-head, helical and micro-hydro extend use; pumped storage vital
  • Tidal: large range (Severn, Mersey), predictable; barrages costly with high impact, lagoons and in-stream turbines lower impact
  • Wave: long Atlantic fetch, but technology immature
  • Biofuels: wastes, anaerobic digestion, energy crops vs food / habitat
  • Geothermal: limited to low-temperature schemes / heat pumps
  • Systems issues: storage (pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen, P2G, V2G), grid management, diversity of sources, energy conservation reducing demand, electrification of transport
  • Judgement: renewables could supply most electricity with storage and a diverse mix, but heat and transport are harder; transition needs investment and lifestyle change
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Eight times (energy proportional to velocity cubed)

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss the difficulties in monitoring and predicting global climate change and evaluate the methods used to overcome them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Nature of the problem: interconnected atmosphere-hydrosphere-biosphere systems; changes over different time and spatial scales; natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) masking or exaggerating anthropogenic change; time delay between cause and effect (ocean heat capacity)
  • Limitations of historic data: sparse global coverage, unsophisticated equipment, heat island bias, no atmospheric composition records
  • Proxy data and their reliability: dendrochronology, coral growth rings, pollen in dated sediments, ice cores (gas bubbles, isotope ratios, volcanic ash layers, compaction problems)
  • Modern monitoring: long-term data sets (Mauna Loa), electronic equipment, satellites (LEO vs geostationary), Argo floats, remote sensing of ice thickness / area / sea level
  • Feedback mechanisms and tipping points adding uncertainty; ecological uncertainty (species survival, distribution, fragmentation)
  • Computer models: how they are tested and refined; importance of accurate representative data; scenario-based predictions and ranges
  • Uncertainty over ENSO / Conveyor / jet stream responses
  • Evaluation and conclusion: confidence in the overall trend and human cause despite detailed uncertainty; role of peer review and international bodies (IPCC)
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 89-90, 100-104, 10; spec 3.2.1.2, 3.1.1.3.
[25 marks]11.2Discuss how knowledge of the properties of energy resources can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: energy density, renewability, abundance, location, intermittency / predictability, storage, transport, conversions, technology level, environmental impacts
  • Fossil fuels: choosing lower-impact fuels (gas over coal), desulfurisation, CCS, efficient combustion, siting power stations near cooling water and coal ports; new extraction technologies (directional drilling, secondary / tertiary recovery) and their impacts (fracking, tar sands)
  • Nuclear: high energy density reduces transport; waste properties (half-life) determine storage; thorium and fusion prospects
  • Renewables: matching resource to location (wind, HEP, solar, wave, tidal, geothermal, biofuel siting); intermittency managed by storage (pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen, P2G, V2G), grid management and diversity; low-impact designs (helical turbines, VAWT, tidal lagoons)
  • Energy conservation reduces demand: transport, buildings, industry (CHP, heat exchangers)
  • Impacts before and during use; life-cycle / embodied energy
  • Evaluation: no resource is impact-free; knowledge allows the best mix and mitigation; judgement
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
