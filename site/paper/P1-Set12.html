03.1 2 marks
1 mark for each point.

  • 1Random sampling might leave gaps / miss the pattern; systematic sampling avoids bias while covering the gradient

1 mark for each point.

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Properties of renewables: intermittency, predictability, low energy density, storage, applicability, locational constraints, impacts, technology level, economics
  • Wind: large offshore resource in shallow seas, falling costs, HAWT / VAWT / direct drive developments; intermittent, needs storage and grid; land-use conflicts
  • Solar: PV costs falling rapidly, roof space, but low winter insolation in the UK; CSP unsuitable; heat pumps and photothermal for heating

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

What renewables can contribute depends on their properties - intermittency, predictability, energy density, storage, applicability, location, impacts, technological maturity and cost - set against the UK's resources. Wind is the largest: shallow seas give a huge offshore resource, costs have fallen and direct-drive and vertical-axis designs keep improving, but it is intermittent, needs storage and grid connections and provokes land-use conflicts onshore. Solar PV costs are falling rapidly and roof space is plentiful, but winter insolation is low and concentrating solar is unsuitable; photothermal panels and heat pumps are better for heating. Most large HEP sites are used, though low-head, helical and micro-hydro extend it and pumped storage is vital for balancing. The Severn and Mersey have large, predictable tidal ranges, but barrages are costly and damaging while lagoons and in-stream turbines have lower impact; the long Atlantic fetch gives wave energy that the technology cannot yet harness reliably. Biofuels from wastes and anaerobic digestion are useful, but energy crops compete with food and habitat, and geothermal is limited to low-temperature schemes.

The system matters as much as the sources: storage in pumped HEP, batteries, hydrogen and power-to-gas, vehicle-to-grid, grid management, a diverse mix that smooths supply, and conservation to reduce demand. With these, renewables could supply most of the UK's electricity. Heat and transport are harder, because they need high temperatures or liquid fuels, so electrification and hydrogen would be required. The potential is large, but realising it needs sustained investment and changes in how energy is used.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

The UK could use lots of renewable energy. It is windy so wind turbines could be built, especially offshore. Solar panels can be put on roofs but it is not very sunny in winter. Tidal power could be used in the Severn estuary because it has big tides. Hydroelectric power can be used in Scotland. Renewables are intermittent so batteries would be needed. Renewables could provide a lot of the UK's energy in the future.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 mentions the main resources with one fact each; Level 3 assesses each against its properties, constraints and the state of the technology.
  • Level 3 treats storage, grid and demand as a system and distinguishes electricity from heat and transport - essential for judging 'future energy supplies'.
  • The conclusion in Level 3 is qualified (most electricity; heat and transport harder; needs investment); Level 1's is unqualified.
