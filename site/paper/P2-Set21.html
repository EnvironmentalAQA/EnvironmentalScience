Question 110 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 357-358 · AQUA-04
[4 marks]01.1Describe a polyculture system using several species of carp and explain how it increases total productivity.
[4 marks]01.2Explain how integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) and aquaponics reduce pollution while increasing food production.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why tuna aquaculture cannot yet replace tuna fishing.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Silver carp eat phytoplankton, bighead carp zooplankton, grass carp and tilapia vegetation, common carp bottom-feed on molluscs / insects, catfish or perch eat small fish
  • Species occupy different niches / trophic levels so they do not compete and all food sources are used
  • Bottom feeders re-suspend nutrients boosting plankton / plant growth
  • Predators control over-breeding species (tilapia) so remaining fish grow larger; total yield exceeds any single species
/ 4
01.2 (4 marks)
  • IMTA: fed species (salmon, shrimp) alongside inorganic extractive species (seaweeds absorbing ammonia and phosphate) and organic extractive species (filter-feeding mussels eating waste particles / plankton)
  • Wastes of one species become nutrients for another so less pollution and extra harvests
  • Aquaponics: aquaculture water rich in nutrients / organic matter feeds hydroponic salad crops (lettuce, herbs, watercress)
  • Plants remove the nutrients that would cause deoxygenation and cleaned water returns to the fish (tilapia, carp, catfish); rice-fish paddies similar
/ 4
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Bluefin tuna do not breed well in captivity so young fish are caught from the wild and fattened in sea cages
  • Production still depends on (and removes juveniles from) the wild population; carnivores need large fish inputs
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 357-358; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.

[2 marks]04.2Explain why clearance for cattle ranching or cash crops is usually unsustainable.
[3 marks]04.3Describe how each of the following threatens rainforest.

04.1 (3 marks)
  • Small clearings were farmed for a few years then abandoned as nutrients declined; the forest recolonised and soil fertility recovered before the area was cleared again
  • Population growth shortens the gap between clearances so the forest and soil do not recover
  • Clearings enlarge and degrade; less regrowth
/ 3
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Rainforest soils are thin and nutrient-poor (nutrients held in the biomass); once cleared, fertility falls in a few years and yields drop, so more land is cleared
  • Abandoned land may not be recolonised if no forest survives nearby; erosion of exposed soil

  • Reservoirs (eg Tucurui, 2850 km2) flood large areas, block migratory fish and river dolphins; decomposing vegetation produces acidic, deoxygenated water; fluctuating downstream flows
  • Droughts increase frequency, area and duration of fires (long fires boil sap and kill trees); cloud forests lose humidity as clouds form higher so frogs and epiphytes decline; some models predict replacement by savannah
/ 3
04.4 (2 marks)
  • A creditor country or conservation charity cancels / buys part of a debtor country's foreign debt
  • In return the country guarantees to protect an area of rainforest (eg USA - Guatemala, Brazil; WWF - Costa Rica, Philippines)

Question 510 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 33-35 · CBR-02

The Hawaiian goose (nene) was reduced by hunting, habitat loss and introduced predators to about 30 birds. A captive breeding programme started with just seven individuals and has increased the wild population to over 2000.

[4 marks]05.1Explain why starting the programme with seven individuals caused problems, and describe how these problems are managed.
[3 marks]05.2Explain three reasons, other than gene pool size, why species kept in captivity may fail to breed.
[3 marks]05.3Describe how artificial insemination and embryo transfer increase breeding success in captive mammals.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • Very small gene pool so individuals are closely related and inbreeding is likely
  • Harmful recessive genes are expressed when both parents carry them: some goslings had thin hair-like feathers with poor insulation so survival in cold weather was low
  • A stud book records family trees so breeding is arranged between the least related individuals (often coordinated by a specialist zoo)
  • Exchange of individuals / semen between zoos; avoid hybridisation with related species / subspecies
/ 4
05.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Breeding is triggered by stimuli (day length, light, temperature, food, body fat) which may be unknown / not provided
  • Population interactions: mixing breeding and non-breeding individuals causes conflict; some need a choice of partners, others pair for life
  • Breeding habitat needed: courtship display sites, nest sites, social groups (flamingos only breed in large groups - mirrors used)
  • Stress / disturbance
/ 3
05.3 (3 marks)
  • AI: semen collected (and can be stored / cryopreserved for years) and inserted into the female, so animals need not be transported or meet; avoids dangerous mating (elephants, rhinos) and partner rejection
  • Embryo transfer: a female of the endangered species (eg bongo) is treated with hormones to release many eggs, which are fertilised and implanted into surrogate females of a common related species (eland)
  • So many offspring are produced per cycle instead of one calf per year, overcoming the shortage of breeding females
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 33-35; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 610 marks · Manipulation of food species & agricultural energetics · spec 3.5.1.2 · Genn pp. 321-323 · FS-01
[6 marks]06.1Compare selective breeding, asexual reproduction and cross-breeding as methods of improving livestock or crops.
[4 marks]06.2Explain how artificial insemination and embryo transfer increase the rate at which desirable characteristics spread through a livestock population.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (6 marks)
  • Selective breeding: parents chosen for desirable genetic characteristics (Limousin muscle growth, Merino wool, Cheviot hardiness); combines genes but characteristics of offspring not fully predictable; many generations needed; risk of inbreeding
  • Asexual reproduction (cuttings, runners, tissue culture, cloning): offspring genetically identical to the parent so characteristics are predictable and survival high; but no variation so no improvement and vulnerability to the same disease; cloning of animals (nucleus transfer into an enucleated egg, surrogate) still developing
  • Cross-breeding: two breeds combined to give a mix of characteristics with hybrid vigour (heterosis) and lower inbreeding risk, eg Zebu (heat tolerant) x Ayrshire (high milk yield)
/ 6
06.2 (4 marks)
  • AI: semen from a male with desirable characteristics can fertilise many more females than natural mating; frozen semen stored and transported easily and used after the male dies
  • Embryo transfer: FSH stimulates a superior female to release many eggs, which are fertilised (IVF) with sperm from a superior male
  • Each embryo is implanted in a different surrogate female
  • So the donor produces many offspring per year rather than one per pregnancy
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 321-323; AQA spec 3.5.1.2.

Question 810 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.5 · Genn pp. 393-394, 400-402 · MON-01

A conservation team is planning to protect a population of an endangered antelope in a reserve.

[8 marks]08.1Explain why each of the following features of the population should be measured before a conservation strategy is decided.
[2 marks]08.2Describe how the population size of the antelope could be estimated.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (8 marks)
  • Size: shows whether the population is viable / how urgent action is; sets a baseline to judge success; needed to estimate carrying capacity and sustainable off-take
  • Distribution: shows which habitats / areas are used and whether the population is fragmented into non-viable isolated groups needing corridors; identifies key areas to protect
  • Survival rate (mortality): identifies the life stage and causes of death (predation, disease, poaching) so management targets the limiting factor
  • Age structure: many young indicates a growing population; few young / ageing population indicates breeding failure or high juvenile mortality even if adults are numerous; k-selected species need breeding adults protected
/ 8
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Direct counts from vehicles / aircraft / drones along transects, or camera traps with individual recognition
  • Sub-sample counts in known areas scaled up to the whole reserve; mark-release-recapture (Lincoln index) for smaller species
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 393-394, 400-402; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.5.

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how feedback mechanisms, tipping points and diversity influence the stability of natural systems, and how human activities affect them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Sustainability definitions; population and per capita impact; attitudes of rural and urban societies
  • Negative feedback: cloud cover and albedo, CO2 and photosynthesis, evaporation and precipitation, density-dependent population regulation
  • Positive feedback driven by human activities: permafrost, acidification, albedo decline, methane hydrates, fires, cirrus clouds, decomposition

  • Human responses that override feedback (fertilisers, irrigation, pesticides); links to climate change chapter; justified conclusion
