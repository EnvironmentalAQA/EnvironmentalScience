Question 115 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 108-109 · OZ-01
[3 marks]01.1Describe how ozone is formed and destroyed naturally in the stratosphere.
[6 marks]01.2Explain the properties of CFCs that made them useful, and the properties that cause ozone depletion (the Rowland-Molina hypothesis).
[3 marks]01.3Explain why increased UVB reaching the Earth's surface is harmful to living organisms. Give three effects.
[1 mark]01.4State what is meant by an ozone hole.
[1 mark]01.5Give one reason why concentration in ppm is not used to measure ozone depletion.
[1 mark]01.6Give one reason why the first evidence for ozone depletion came from ground-based measurements.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • UV light splits diatomic oxygen (O2) into two monatomic oxygen atoms (O)
  • Monatomic oxygen reacts with diatomic oxygen to form ozone (O3)
  • UV light also splits ozone into O2 and O; the reactions form a dynamic equilibrium which absorbs UV (converting it to chemical energy / heat)
/ 3
01.2 (6 marks)
  • Useful: boiling points near ambient temperature so easily liquefied under low pressure (aerosol propellants, fridge / air-conditioning coolants); dissolve grease but do not damage electrical components (solvents); non-flammable; non-toxic
  • Persistence: chemically stable / unreactive in the troposphere so they remain long enough to be carried up to the stratosphere
  • Dissociation by UV: in the stratosphere higher UV levels break the carbon-chlorine bonds releasing chlorine free radicals
  • Chlorine reacts with monatomic oxygen (and ozone), preventing ozone formation; chlorine is regenerated so one atom destroys many ozone molecules; bromine / iodine act similarly
/ 6
01.3 (3 marks)
  • UVB energy is absorbed by cells and breaks up biological molecules / DNA causing mutations, skin cancer, cataracts, sunburn (human health)
  • Damage to leaf tissue and reduced photosynthesis / crop yields (plants)
  • Damage to marine algae, phytoplankton, corals and planktonic larvae (marine organisms), reducing productivity and food webs
/ 3
01.4 (1 marks)
  • An area where the total ozone column falls below 220 Dobson Units (normal is about 300 DU) - a level never recorded before CFC pollution
/ 1
01.5 (1 marks)
  • Concentration varies with altitude and the stratosphere is not normally sampled; Dobson Units measure the total column thickness instead
/ 1
01.6 (1 marks)
  • The British Antarctic Survey at Halley detected higher UV at ground level, showing ozone above had decreased (satellites above the stratosphere cannot measure downward UV directly)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-109; AQA spec 3.2.1.3.
Question 210 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.1 · Genn pp. 158-161 · SOIL-02
[6 marks]02.1Explain the roles of three named groups of soil organisms in maintaining soil fertility.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why a soil pH between 5.5 and 7.0 is best for fertility.
[2 marks]02.3Explain how soil compaction by heavy machinery reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (6 marks)
  • Detritivores (worms, beetle larvae, millipedes, woodlice) physically break up dead organic matter, increasing its surface area, and release nutrients; worms create tunnels improving drainage and aeration
  • Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) secrete enzymes that break down dead organic matter, releasing nutrient ions (ammonification) and forming humus which binds particles into crumb peds
  • Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas to ammonium ions; nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite then nitrate for root absorption
  • Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with roots increasing phosphate uptake
/ 6
02.2 (2 marks)
  • This is the range of tolerance of most plants and soil biota; acidic soils leach nutrients and mobilise toxic aluminium, damaging root membranes
  • In alkaline soils phosphates become insoluble / unavailable
/ 2
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Interstitial spaces are reduced so aeration falls (aerobic processes such as nitrification and decomposition slow) and roots cannot penetrate
  • Infiltration falls so waterlogging is more likely and runoff / erosion increase
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 158-161; AQA spec 3.2.5.1.

Question 515 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 103-104, 371-372 · CC-07
[2 marks]05.1Explain the difference between a negative and a positive feedback mechanism.
[4 marks]05.2Describe two negative feedback mechanisms that may reduce global warming.
[4 marks]05.3Explain how melting permafrost and melting methane hydrate act as positive feedback mechanisms.
[3 marks]05.4Give three other positive feedback mechanisms.
[2 marks]05.5Explain what is meant by a climate tipping point.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • Negative feedback: a change causes other changes that reduce the rate or size of the original change, re-establishing equilibrium
  • Positive feedback: a change causes other changes that increase the original change
/ 2
05.2 (4 marks)
  • Increased low-level cloud: higher temperatures increase evaporation and condensation, producing more cloud; clouds have a high albedo so more sunlight is reflected and warming is reduced
  • Increased photosynthesis: higher temperatures and CO2 increase photosynthesis, removing CO2; if stored in woody tissue atmospheric CO2 rises less
/ 4
05.3 (4 marks)
  • Permafrost: frozen waterlogged Arctic soils contain dead organic matter that decomposed anaerobically producing methane trapped in the frozen ground
  • Warming thaws the permafrost, releasing methane (a powerful greenhouse gas) and allowing further decomposition, causing more warming
  • Methane hydrate: methane from decomposed organic matter in deep sea sediments is held as a solid under high pressure and low temperature
  • If deep water warms the hydrate melts, releasing methane into the atmosphere and causing further warming
/ 4
05.4 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Reduced albedo as snow / ice melts
  • Ocean acidification reducing coral survival and carbon sequestration
  • Increased forest and peat fires releasing CO2
  • Faster decomposition of soil organic matter releasing CO2
  • Increased water vapour (a greenhouse gas) in warmer air
  • Increased cirrus (high) cloud which traps infrared
/ 3
05.5 (2 marks)
  • A point at which natural processes triggered by human-caused warming become self-sustaining / unstoppable
  • So that stopping the original human activity would no longer stop climate change (eg runaway permafrost melt, methane hydrate release, forest fires)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 103-104, 371-372; AQA spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1.
Question 610 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 192-194 · FN-05

Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Public opposition following accidents (Three Mile Island 1979, Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011)
  • Very complex, expensive technology needing an advanced industrial infrastructure
  • Concerns about links between civil nuclear materials and weapons / terrorism
  • Uncertainty over permanent disposal of radioactive waste
  • Uncertain decommissioning costs
/ 3

  • Only 0.7% of natural uranium is fissile U-235; the remaining 99.3% is non-fissile U-238 (a 'fertile' fuel)
  • Neutron bombardment in the reactor converts U-238 to fissile plutonium-239 (via beta decays)
  • So much more energy is harnessed from the mined uranium; fast reactors need no moderator but are complex and costly

  • Disadvantages: thorium-232 is not fissile so must be bred into uranium-233 (slow breeding rate); U-233 is an alpha emitter so very hazardous to handle; less developed technology with high development costs

Show mark scheme
07.1 (1 marks)
  • B - Tropospheric ozone (formed by reactions of NOx in sunlight)
/ 1
07.2 (4 marks)
  • Primary pollutant: released directly by human activities, eg sulfur dioxide from burning coal / smelting sulfide ores; NOx from high-temperature combustion
  • Secondary pollutant: produced by chemical reactions between primary pollutants, often with non-pollutants (water, oxygen, sunlight)

10.1 (3 marks)
  • Together they form a dispersed layer in the stratosphere (the ozone layer / ozonosphere)
  • They absorb ultraviolet light, which drives a dynamic equilibrium of reactions that form and destroy ozone
  • The UV energy is converted to chemical / heat energy so most UV that passed the upper atmosphere never reaches the surface
/ 3
10.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Provides carbon, oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen (as N2, O2, CO2, H2O) for biological molecules
  • Delays the escape of infrared energy, keeping the surface warm
  • Distributes heat and water vapour by winds / ocean currents
  • Provides atmospheric pressure so liquid water can exist
  • Deflects damaging radiation of the solar wind in the upper atmosphere
/ 2
10.3 (1 marks)
  • Energy is absorbed at different altitudes (eg UV absorbed in the stratosphere, surface heating warms the troposphere) so temperature gradients change with altitude, producing layers
/ 1
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Troposphere: enhanced greenhouse effect - anthropogenic CO2 (fossil fuels, deforestation), methane (rice, livestock, landfill), N2O, CFCs, tropospheric ozone; more infrared absorbed and re-emitted; aerosols and smoke increase albedo / scatter light; land-use change alters surface albedo and evapotranspiration
  • Stratosphere: CFCs / halons / HCFCs release chlorine and bromine which catalyse ozone destruction, shifting the O / O2 / O3 equilibrium; less UV absorbed in the stratosphere, more reaches the surface; stratospheric cooling
  • Consequences: changes to climate, ocean currents (thermohaline), hydrological cycle, sea level, species distributions; increased UV damage to organisms
  • Difficulties of prediction: linked processes where one change alters others; feedback (positive and negative) and tipping points; time lags (ocean heat capacity, long residence times of CFCs and CO2); natural variability (solar output, volcanoes, Milankovitch cycles); incomplete understanding of atmospheric processes; limited historical data so proxies needed; models with different assumptions
  • Evaluation: some effects well understood (greenhouse physics, ozone chemistry proven, Montreal Protocol success) but scale and timing of regional impacts uncertain; precautionary principle
  • Justified conclusion

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss the difficulties in monitoring and predicting global climate change and evaluate the methods used to overcome them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Nature of the problem: interconnected atmosphere-hydrosphere-biosphere systems; changes over different time and spatial scales; natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) masking or exaggerating anthropogenic change; time delay between cause and effect (ocean heat capacity)
  • Limitations of historic data: sparse global coverage, unsophisticated equipment, heat island bias, no atmospheric composition records
  • Proxy data and their reliability: dendrochronology, coral growth rings, pollen in dated sediments, ice cores (gas bubbles, isotope ratios, volcanic ash layers, compaction problems)
  • Modern monitoring: long-term data sets (Mauna Loa), electronic equipment, satellites (LEO vs geostationary), Argo floats, remote sensing of ice thickness / area / sea level
  • Feedback mechanisms and tipping points adding uncertainty; ecological uncertainty (species survival, distribution, fragmentation)
  • Computer models: how they are tested and refined; importance of accurate representative data; scenario-based predictions and ranges
  • Uncertainty over ENSO / Conveyor / jet stream responses
  • Evaluation and conclusion: confidence in the overall trend and human cause despite detailed uncertainty; role of peer review and international bodies (IPCC)

[25 marks]11.2Explain how feedback mechanisms and tipping points influence the rate of global climate change and discuss the implications for the strategies needed to control it.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Negative feedback: increased low-level cloud (albedo), increased photosynthesis and sequestration; hydrological cycle evaporation-precipitation
  • Positive feedback: melting permafrost (methane), reduced albedo of snow / ice / sea ice, methane hydrate release, ocean acidification and coral loss, forest and peat fires, soil decomposition, water vapour, cirrus clouds

  • Judgement on whether current strategies are adequate
