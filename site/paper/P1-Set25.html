Question 210 marks · Pesticides · spec 3.4.3.2.8 · Genn pp. 263-264, 266 · PEST-03

A pesticide was applied to a field of oilseed rape. Table 1 shows the concentration of the pesticide in organisms sampled from a pond next to the field.

Table 1
OrganismTrophic levelPesticide concentration / mg kg-1
Pond water-0.002
Phytoplankton10.04
Water fleas (Daphnia)20.5
Small fish34.2
Heron438.0
[2 marks]02.1Calculate how many times more concentrated the pesticide is in the heron than in the pond water. Give your answer to two significant figures.
[1 mark]02.2Name the process shown by the data in Table 1.
[4 marks]02.3Explain the two properties of the pesticide that cause the pattern shown in Table 1.
[3 marks]02.4The pesticide was probably an organochlorine. Suggest why the use of organochlorines is now banned or heavily restricted in most countries, even though DDT is still used to control malaria mosquitoes in some houses.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (2 marks)
  • 38.0 / 0.002
  • = 19 000 (19 000 times / 1.9 x 104)
/ 2
02.2 (1 marks)
  • Biomagnification (accept bioaccumulation as part of the process only if biomagnification is also implied by increase up the food chain)
/ 1
02.3 (4 marks)
  • Persistence: the pesticide is not broken down (by light, microbes, enzymes) so it remains in the environment / organisms long enough to pass along the food chain
  • Liposolubility: the pesticide dissolves in lipids / fat so it is stored in body tissue and not excreted in (water-based) urine
  • Each organism eats many organisms from the level below and retains the pesticide so concentration rises at each trophic level
  • Herons have long lives so accumulate over time
/ 4
02.4 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:

  • They persist for decades so the damage continued long after use
  • Use in houses (on walls / nets) does not enter food chains / no runoff into water, so does not biomagnify
  • Benefit of preventing malaria deaths outweighs the risk (risk:benefit)
/ 3

Question 310 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 416-418 · RM-11

A student investigated the effect of pH on the germination of cress seeds.

[2 marks]03.1Explain why cress is a suitable species for this investigation but the results may not apply directly to natural ecosystems.
[2 marks]03.2Describe how the student should decide the range of pH values to test.
[4 marks]03.3Give three variables that should be standardised and explain how long the experiment should continue.
[2 marks]03.4The student wants to compare the effect of nutrient concentration on the growth of duckweed. Explain why leaf area is a better measure of growth than fresh mass.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Small seeds, genetically uniform, high and rapid germination rate so results are quick and reliable
  • Wild species have different ranges of tolerance so cress results cannot be applied directly
/ 2
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Preliminary study with widely spaced pH values to find where germination occurs
  • Final range from below the lowest pH giving germination to above the highest, with closer intervals
/ 2
03.3 (4 marks)
  • Temperature (constant, natural germination temperature)
  • Light, water volume, number of seeds per dish, seed source
  • Growth medium (filter paper; soil would alter pH); nutrients not needed for germination
  • Continue until it is clear that ungerminated seeds will not germinate, so that a delay in germination is not mistaken for failure
/ 4
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Surface water affects fresh mass and drying damages the plants; dry mass requires killing the plants so the same plants cannot be re-measured
  • Leaf area is a reasonable estimate of biomass that allows repeated measurement of the same plants over time
/ 2

Question 410 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.3-3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 183-196 · FN-07
[9 marks]04.1Evaluate the extent to which new technologies could allow fossil fuels and nuclear fission to continue to supply a large proportion of the world's energy sustainably.
[1 mark]04.2State the approximate percentage of natural gas in a reservoir that can be recovered using its own pressure.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Fossil fuel technologies: secondary / tertiary recovery raising recovery from 20% to 60%; directional drilling and subsea wells; ROVs; fracking for tight oil and gas; tar sands / oil shales; coal gasification / liquefaction; enhanced gas recovery; methane hydrates; CCS (pre / post combustion, storage)
  • Limits: still finite (discoveries declining since the 1980s); rising energy and financial cost of unconventional sources; environmental impacts (aquifers, water use, tremors, land take, habitat damage); CO2 and other pollution; CCS unproven at scale and impractical for vehicles
  • Nuclear: new uranium extraction (polymer adsorption from seawater, phosphate mining, coal ash); molten salt reactors; plutonium breeder and thorium reactors increasing fuel use many-fold; fusion as a long-term prospect
  • Limits: cost overruns, decommissioning, waste, public opposition, proliferation, embodied energy of low-grade uranium; fusion not commercial
  • Sustainability criteria: maintaining supply without unacceptable environmental, economic or social impact; comparison with renewables and energy conservation; energy gap argument
  • Judgement: technologies can extend supplies and reduce some impacts but cannot make non-renewables sustainable indefinitely; role as transition / base-load alongside renewables
/ 9
04.2 (1 marks)
  • 80-90%
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 183-196; AQA spec 3.3.3-3.3.4.1.
Question 510 marks · Importance & features of energy resources · spec 3.3.2-3.3.3 · Genn pp. 175-182 · ENF-06
[9 marks]05.1Discuss how the features of energy resources determine their suitability for different uses and their environmental sustainability.
[1 mark]05.2State one reason why nuclear power stations can be located where transport infrastructure is poor.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Features: renewable / non-renewable, depletable, abundance, energy density, locational constraints, intermittency and predictability, need for conversions, applicability, ease of storage and transport, technological development, political and economic influences, environmental impacts
  • Fossil fuels: high energy density, easy storage and transport, reach high temperatures, well-developed technologies and infrastructure - but finite, polluting (acid rain, smoke, CO2), politically concentrated (Middle East oil)
  • Nuclear: very high energy density, base load, few fuel deliveries - but complex, expensive, waste, public opposition
  • Renewables: low energy density, intermittent (wind, solar unpredictable; tidal predictable), locationally constrained, cannot be stored without conversion, need storage / grid, cannot reach fossil-fuel temperatures or replace liquid vehicle fuels yet - but renewable, low pollution, falling costs
  • Sustainability concepts: impacts before use (extraction, processing, equipment embodied energy) and as a consequence of use (pollution, habitat damage, depletion)
  • Economic and political factors: subsidies, guaranteed prices, planning, external costs not included in price, energy security
  • Judgement: a diverse mix matched to uses (electricity vs heat vs transport) with storage is most sustainable; transition must start before depletion
/ 9
05.2 (1 marks)
  • Very high energy density fuel means only a few tens of tonnes are needed each year, compared with 10 000 t of coal per day for a coal-fired station
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 175-182; AQA spec 3.3.2-3.3.3.

Question 910 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.4 · Genn pp. 156-157 · BGC-06
[3 marks]09.1Explain why phosphorus is more likely than nitrogen to be a limiting factor on biological productivity.
[2 marks]09.2Explain how mycorrhizal fungi increase phosphate uptake by plants.
[3 marks]09.3Describe how human activities have altered the phosphorus cycle.
[2 marks]09.4Explain two strategies for the sustainable management of phosphorus supplies.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
  • There is no gaseous / atmospheric reservoir of phosphorus (no equivalent of nitrogen fixation)
  • Most phosphorus compounds have low solubility so little is available in soil water
  • Phosphorus lost to marine sediments only returns through very slow processes: mountain building (uplift) and weathering of rocks
/ 3
09.2 (2 marks)
  • Fungal hyphae form a symbiotic relationship with plant roots and spread through a large volume of soil
  • Greatly increasing the surface area for absorbing (poorly mobile) phosphate ions, passed to the plant in exchange for carbohydrates
/ 2
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Mining of calcium phosphate rock (and past mining of guano deposits, eg Peru, Nauru, now largely exhausted)
  • Treated to make more soluble ammonium phosphate fertilisers, mobilising phosphorus into the environment
  • Fertiliser runoff / eroded soil / sewage effluent carry phosphates into water bodies causing eutrophication (with nitrates); harvesting removes phosphorus from farmland
/ 3
09.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Use biological wastes (manure, sewage sludge, compost) as fertiliser to return phosphates rather than mining rock
  • Breed crops that absorb phosphate more efficiently
  • Maintain conditions for mycorrhizal fungi (less tillage, fewer fungicides, organic matter)
  • Recover phosphate from sewage effluent by iron(III) sulfate precipitation; reduce soil erosion
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 156-157; AQA spec 3.2.4.4.
Question 1015 marks · Water treatment, conservation & new sources · spec 3.2.2.4-3.2.2.5 · Genn pp. 122-127 · HYD-13

Figure 1 shows the river level below a reservoir site during one year, before and after a river-regulation reservoir was built.

Figure 1
[2 marks]10.1Use Figure 1 to calculate the reduction in the annual range of river level produced by the reservoir.
[2 marks]10.2Explain how the reservoir produces the changes shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]10.3Explain why human activities in the catchment may have increased the extremes in river flow before the reservoir was built.
[9 marks]10.4Discuss the factors that should be considered when selecting a site for a new reservoir, and evaluate the alternative strategies for increasing the sustainability of water supplies.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (2 marks)
  • No reservoir range = 3.9 - 0.7 = 3.2 m; with reservoir = 2.7 - 1.7 = 1.0 m
  • Reduction = 2.2 m
/ 2
10.2 (2 marks)
  • After heavy rainfall surplus water is stored, reducing peak flows / flooding downstream
  • During dry periods the dam is opened to release water and maintain river levels
/ 2
10.3 (2 marks)
  • Deforestation / urbanisation reduce infiltration and interception
  • So runoff reaches the river faster after rain and less water is stored to maintain flow in dry periods
/ 2
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Site factors: topography - narrow exit from a large deep basin so a small dam holds a large volume; impermeable, strong bedrock without faults or seismic activity; large catchment with regular high rainfall and low evaporation; catchment land use with low pollution risk (pesticides, industry) and low soil erosion to avoid sedimentation; forest / vegetation in the flooded area decaying anaerobically releasing methane; access for workers and materials and distance to demand; value of the land lost (urban, conservation, agricultural) - loss-benefit analysis differs between countries
  • Alternatives for supply: rainwater collection, rivers, estuary barrages, desalination (energy-intensive so only where freshwater is inadequate), unexploited aquifers (N Africa, Kenya), inter-basin transfers (China, Australia, Wales-England), artificial aquifer recharge using surplus wet-season water, river-regulation reservoirs, afforestation to even out flow
  • Conservation strategies: low-water appliances, dual-flush toilets, xeriscaping, drip irrigation, grey-water re-use, leak repair (about 20% lost in the UK), metering
  • Treatment / pollution control allowing re-use of contaminated sources: sewage treatment, buffer strips, mine drainage control
  • Evaluation: reservoirs give large reliable supplies and flood control but flood land, cause habitat loss and methane; conservation is cheap and quick but limited by behaviour; transfers create conflicts between regions; desalination has high energy / carbon cost; combination needed as demand grows
  • Justified conclusion
/ 9
