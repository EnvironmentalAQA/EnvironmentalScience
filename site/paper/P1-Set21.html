[2 marks]01.4Give two examples of mineral deposits formed by biological sedimentation and state their origin.
Show mark scheme
01.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Igneous intrusions (batholiths) of magma cool within the crust, heating groundwater to produce superheated, pressurised water
  • 1The hot water dissolves minerals from the surrounding rock and the mineral-rich solutions travel along fissures / cracks away from the batholith
  • 1As the solutions cool the dissolved minerals crystallise in order of solubility (least soluble first) - fractional crystallisation
  • 1So different metals are separated and concentrated in veins, eg tin, copper, lead, silver, gold, arsenic (SW England batholith)

1 mark for each point.

  • 1As velocity falls, dense minerals (gold, tin ore) are deposited first while lighter material is carried further, concentrating the heavy minerals in particular places

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Control of greenhouse gases: energy conservation (transport, buildings, industry), renewable and nuclear energy replacing fossil fuels, CCS, reduced deforestation and afforestation (carbon sequestration), methane capture from landfill / digesters, HFC controls, changes in agriculture
  • Geoengineering: white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - largely untried, possible unpredictable consequences

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Strategies fall into control of greenhouse gases, geoengineering, international agreements and adaptation. Emissions are cut by energy conservation in transport, buildings and industry, by replacing fossil fuels with renewables and nuclear, by carbon capture and storage, by reducing deforestation and planting forests, and by capturing methane from landfill. Geoengineering ideas - white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - remain largely untried with unpredictable side effects. Adaptation, such as flood defences and managed retreat, reduces impacts but not the cause.

The Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement set targets, but they are weak or non-binding and participation is uneven; carbon trading and taxes exist but per capita footprints still differ enormously between countries. The evidence of success is limited: atmospheric CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 410 ppm and temperature by about 1 °C, and although emissions are falling in some developed countries they are rising in developing economies.

The difficulty is that fossil fuels underpin economies, so control demands lifestyle change, cost, and political agreement between nations with different priorities, while time delays and natural fluctuations mask the effect of any action. By contrast the Montreal Protocol succeeded because ozone was a substitution problem. The judgement must be partial success: the technology exists and regional reductions prove it works, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise, and tipping points make the delay dangerous.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Methods to reduce climate change include using renewable energy such as wind and solar instead of fossil fuels, saving energy, and planting trees to absorb carbon dioxide. International agreements like the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement have set targets for countries to reduce emissions. However carbon dioxide levels are still rising, so the methods have not been very successful so far. This is because countries rely on fossil fuels and do not always keep to the agreements. More needs to be done.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 covers the main strategies and reaches a conclusion, but with no quantitative evidence (280 to 410 ppm, 1 °C) and no mention of geoengineering or adaptation.
  • Level 3 explains why success is limited (economic dependence, time lags, natural variability, political disagreement) rather than just stating that it is.
  • The Montreal comparison and the tipping-point point give Level 3 the breadth and judgement the top band requires.
