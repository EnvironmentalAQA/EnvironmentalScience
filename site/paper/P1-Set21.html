Question 410 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 151-152 · BGC-03
[3 marks]04.1Explain why planting a new forest only sequesters carbon for a limited period, and how the storage can be extended.
[4 marks]04.2Compare pre-combustion and post-combustion methods of carbon capture.
[3 marks]04.3Describe two ways captured CO2 can be stored and give one concern about long-term storage.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Trees absorb CO2 by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood as they grow
  • Once mature there is no further net storage (photosynthesis balanced by respiration / decomposition), though the standing forest remains a reservoir
  • Harvesting the timber and keeping it as wood (eg house construction) then replanting produces additional storage as the new trees grow
/ 3
04.2 (4 marks)
  • Pre-combustion: the fuel is modified before use so no CO2 is released when burnt - eg gasification of coal to hydrogen and CO2 (CO2 stored, hydrogen distributed); oxy-fuel combustion in pure oxygen giving only CO2 and water vapour which is condensed out
  • Advantage: capture at a large central facility; suits small dispersed sources (vehicles) that cannot capture CO2
  • Post-combustion: CO2 removed from exhaust gases by solvents, membrane filtration, adsorption / desorption, cryogenic separation, graphene
  • Disadvantage: only about 20% of flue gas is CO2 so separation is costly / energy-intensive; none fully developed
/ 4
04.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Injected into depleted oil / gas fields or saline aquifers
  • Injected into oil reservoirs to provide pressure for secondary oil recovery
  • Reaction with fractured basalt to form solid carbonate minerals
  • Concern: leakage back to the atmosphere / monitoring over centuries; limited suitable geology; cost
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 151-152; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.
Question 510 marks · Thermal & oil pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.7 · Genn pp. 259-260 · WPOL-03

Table 1 shows features of oil tanker design and operation that reduce the risk of oil pollution.

[5 marks]05.1Complete Table 1 by explaining how each feature reduces oil pollution.
Table 1
FeatureHow it reduces oil pollution
Double hull
Inert gas system
Separate oil and ballast tanks
Twin engines / rudders
Recirculation of oil during unloading (crude oil washing)
[2 marks]05.2Explain how each of the following reduces the risk of tanker accidents.
[2 marks]05.3Describe how a bund wall prevents oil pollution from a storage tank.
[1 mark]05.4Explain the purpose of an oil interceptor on the drainage system of a car park.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (5 marks)
  • Double hull: a gap of up to 2 m between the hulls so damage to the outer hull in a collision / grounding does not release oil
  • Inert gas system: cooled engine exhaust gas (no oxygen) fills the space above the oil so an explosive oil vapour / air mixture cannot form
  • Separate ballast tanks: ballast water is not carried in oil tanks so no residual oil is discharged when ballast is pumped out
  • Twin engines / rudders: if one fails the ship can still be steered / propelled and is not carried onto rocks by currents or wind
  • Crude oil washing: sludges are mixed and removed with the cargo so tanks do not need washing with seawater that was then discharged; oily water is unloaded at the terminal
/ 5
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Offshore routes keep tankers further from the coast so if there is engine failure there is more time to deal with it before the ship is carried ashore
  • GPS gives accurate navigation; AIS lets ship positions be monitored and coordinated to avoid collisions and groundings
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • The tank stands on an impermeable base surrounded by a wall
  • The volume enclosed by the wall is greater than the tank volume so all the oil is contained if the tank splits / cannot overflow
/ 2
05.4 (1 marks)
  • Separates floating oil from drainage water so the oil is retained (for later treatment) and only water flows to the drains / river
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 259-260; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.7.

[1 mark]06.2Name the process shown by the data in Table 1.
[4 marks]06.3Explain the two properties of the pesticide that cause the pattern shown in Table 1.
[3 marks]06.4The pesticide was probably an organochlorine. Suggest why the use of organochlorines is now banned or heavily restricted in most countries, even though DDT is still used to control malaria mosquitoes in some houses.

06.1 (2 marks)
  • 38.0 / 0.002
  • = 19 000 (19 000 times / 1.9 x 104)

06.2 (1 marks)
  • Biomagnification (accept bioaccumulation as part of the process only if biomagnification is also implied by increase up the food chain)

Question 710 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 132-134 · MIN-01
[4 marks]07.1Describe how hydrothermal deposition produces exploitable concentrations of metal ores.
[2 marks]07.2Explain how Proterozoic marine sediments produced the world's major iron ore deposits.
[2 marks]07.3Explain how alluvial (physical sediment) processes separate and concentrate minerals such as gold and tin ore.
[2 marks]07.4Give two examples of mineral deposits formed by biological sedimentation and state their origin.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • Igneous intrusions (batholiths) of magma cool within the crust, heating groundwater to produce superheated, pressurised water
  • The hot water dissolves minerals from the surrounding rock and the mineral-rich solutions travel along fissures / cracks away from the batholith
  • As the solutions cool the dissolved minerals crystallise in order of solubility (least soluble first) - fractional crystallisation
  • So different metals are separated and concentrated in veins, eg tin, copper, lead, silver, gold, arsenic (SW England batholith)
/ 4
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Dissolved iron compounds in ancient seas were oxidised by the oxygen released by early photosynthetic organisms (2.5-1.8 billion years ago)
  • Iron oxides are insoluble so they were deposited as sediments (haematite, magnetite)
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Flowing water carries solid particles; the ability to carry them depends on the water velocity and the density of the solids
  • As velocity falls, dense minerals (gold, tin ore) are deposited first while lighter material is carried further, concentrating the heavy minerals in particular places
/ 2
07.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Limestone / chalk from the shells of marine organisms
  • Coal from terrestrial vegetation
  • Crude oil and natural gas from marine organisms (plankton)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 132-134; AQA spec 3.2.3.2.
Question 85 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 237-238 · PPRO-06
[1 mark]08.1Which of the following is a secondary pollutant? Tick one box.
  • A  Sulfur dioxide from a coal-fired power station
  • B  Tropospheric ozone
  • C  Carbon monoxide from a car exhaust
  • D  Nitrogen monoxide from a diesel engine
[4 marks]08.2Explain the difference between a primary pollutant and a secondary pollutant, using acid rain as an example.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (1 marks)
  • B - Tropospheric ozone (formed by reactions of NOx in sunlight)
/ 1
08.2 (4 marks)
  • Primary pollutant: released directly by human activities, eg sulfur dioxide from burning coal / smelting sulfide ores; NOx from high-temperature combustion
  • Secondary pollutant: produced by chemical reactions between primary pollutants, often with non-pollutants (water, oxygen, sunlight)
  • Sulfur dioxide is oxidised by ozone to sulfur trioxide which dissolves to form sulfuric acid; NOx dissolve to form nitric acid
  • The acids (sulfurous, sulfuric, nitric) are secondary pollutants; ozone itself is a secondary pollutant of NOx photochemistry

Question 1010 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 89-107 · CC-10
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the success of the methods and strategies used to reduce global climate change.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by a carbon footprint.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Control of greenhouse gases: energy conservation (transport, buildings, industry), renewable and nuclear energy replacing fossil fuels, CCS, reduced deforestation and afforestation (carbon sequestration), methane capture from landfill / digesters, HFC controls, changes in agriculture
  • Geoengineering: white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - largely untried, possible unpredictable consequences
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement - targets, but non-binding / uneven participation; carbon trading and taxes; comparison of per capita carbon footprints between countries
  • Evidence of limited success: global CO2 concentrations still rising (about 280 ppm pre-industrial to over 410 ppm); temperatures up about 1 °C; emissions in some developed countries falling but rising in developing economies
  • Reasons for difficulty: fossil fuels underpin economies, need for major lifestyle change, cost, time delays and natural fluctuations masking effects, political disagreement, developing countries' right to develop
  • Adaptation strategies (flood defences, drainage, managed retreat, infrastructure changes) reduce impacts but do not address the cause
  • Comparison with the Montreal Protocol's success for ozone
  • Judgement: partial success - technology exists and some regional reductions achieved, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise; tipping points make urgency greater
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The total greenhouse gas / CO2 emissions caused by an individual, organisation, product or country (often expressed per capita per year)

Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.

  • Evaluation: no resource is impact-free; knowledge allows the best mix and mitigation; judgement
