Question 410 marks · Ionising radiation · spec 3.4.3.2.14 · Genn pp. 301, 303 · RAD-02
[3 marks]04.1Explain how ionising radiation damages living cells.
[2 marks]04.2Distinguish between acute and chronic effects of radiation exposure.
[2 marks]04.3Distinguish between exposure and contamination.
[2 marks]04.4A worker receives an absorbed dose of 0.002 Gy from alpha radiation. Calculate the effective dose in sieverts.
[1 mark]04.5State what is measured by the becquerel.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Radiation absorbed by tissue produces free radicals (ions with unpaired electrons), mostly from water, which are highly reactive
  • Free radicals damage DNA / chromosomes in the nucleus causing mutations (cancer, inherited effects) - rapidly dividing cells (skin, gut, bone marrow) most affected
  • Damage to other cell parts can stop the cell functioning or kill it; low damage may be repaired by the intact nucleus
/ 3
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Acute: effects appearing quickly after a large dose over a short time (radiation sickness, damage to bone marrow / immune system / gut, haemorrhage, rapid death)
  • Chronic: effects appearing slowly, in proportion to the dose received over a long period (cancers, cataracts)
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Exposure: absorption of ionising radiation from a source the person is close enough to
  • Contamination: physically carrying radioactive material on or in the body, so exposure continues wherever the person goes; sealed sources can expose but not contaminate
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Sieverts = grays x radiation weighting factor (20 for alpha)
  • 0.002 x 20 = 0.04 Sv (40 mSv)
/ 2
04.5 (1 marks)
  • The activity of a source (number of decays per second)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 301, 303; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.14.

Question 810 marks · The hydrosphere: hydrological cycle & unsustainable exploitation · spec 3.2.2.1 · Genn pp. 116-117 · HYD-02
[6 marks]08.1Explain how each of the following human activities alters processes in the hydrological cycle.
[2 marks]08.2Describe two ways in which global climate change alters the hydrological cycle.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why the impact of industrial and domestic water use on the hydrological cycle is usually local.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (6 marks)
  • Deforestation: less interception so less evaporation from leaves; less transpiration so less water returned to the atmosphere and less precipitation downwind; more infiltration and surface runoff, higher river flows / flooding
  • Urban development: impermeable concrete / tarmac reduce infiltration and aquifer recharge; runoff is faster and greater, increasing river flooding downstream; drains carry water quickly to rivers
  • Agriculture: soil compaction by machinery / trampling reduces infiltration and soil moisture, increases runoff; irrigation increases evaporation; loss of soil biota (worms) reduces infiltration and water retention; drainage lowers water tables
/ 6
08.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Higher temperatures increase evaporation and change the amount, timing, type (rain vs snow) and location of precipitation
  • More rapid melting of snow and ice changes river regimes: more flooding after rain, lower flows in dry periods
  • Changed wind patterns move humid air to different areas
  • Sea level rise / salt incursion into coastal aquifers
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Most water abstracted is cleaned and returned to the river / sea so the main impact is on quality (pollution) not quantity
  • Effects are noticeable only where large urban centres draw on restricted local supplies (aquifers, small rivers)

Question 1010 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.5 · Genn pp. 245-254 · AIR-07
[9 marks]10.1Explain how the properties of smoke and of the gases involved in acid rain lead to their environmental effects, and evaluate the effectiveness of the methods used to control them.
[1 mark]10.2State one effect of tropospheric ozone on plants.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Smoke: solid particulates (PM10, PM5, PM1) from incomplete combustion; small particles stay suspended, penetrate lungs; toxic chemicals (acids, phenols, metals) carried on particles; high albedo cools climate; blocks light reducing photosynthesis; damages buildings
  • Smoke smogs form under temperature inversions in valleys (London 1952, 12 000 deaths); synergism with SO2
  • Controls: Clean Air Act 1956 (smokeless zones), smokeless fuels / gas / electricity, coal treatment, electrostatic precipitators, cyclones, bag filters, scrubbers, DPFs, better combustion; smoke smogs have largely been eliminated in UK cities
  • Acid rain gases: SO2 (coal combustion, smelting sulfide ores) oxidised by ozone to SO3 - sulfuric acid; NOx (high temperature combustion) - nitric acid; HCl from PVC incineration; reactive gases travel long distances (transboundary pollution)
  • Effects: pH denatures proteins / enzymes in exposed cells (gills, eggs, root hairs, stomata); dissolves exoskeletons; leaches Ca and Mg then mobilises toxic Al and Pb; corrodes metals, dissolves limestone; severity depends on bedrock (limestone neutralises)
  • Controls: fuel desulfurisation (coal washing, hydrodesulfurisation, low-sulfur ship fuels), dry and wet FGD, fluidised bed combustion, catalytic converters, urea sprays; switching from coal to gas / renewables
  • Evaluation: SO2 emissions in Europe have fallen dramatically and lake pH is recovering; NOx harder to control because of growing vehicle numbers; catalytic converters need lead-free fuel and warm engines; FGD is costly and produces wastes / gypsum; controlling primary pollutants also prevents secondary pollutants (ozone, PANs)
  • Judgement on relative success of controls for particulates vs SO2 vs NOx
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
Any 1 from:
  • Reduces growth rates of trees / crops
  • Damages leaf cuticles (allowing SO2 to cause more damage)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 245-254; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.5.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss the difficulties in monitoring and predicting global climate change and evaluate the methods used to overcome them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Nature of the problem: interconnected atmosphere-hydrosphere-biosphere systems; changes over different time and spatial scales; natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) masking or exaggerating anthropogenic change; time delay between cause and effect (ocean heat capacity)
  • Limitations of historic data: sparse global coverage, unsophisticated equipment, heat island bias, no atmospheric composition records
  • Proxy data and their reliability: dendrochronology, coral growth rings, pollen in dated sediments, ice cores (gas bubbles, isotope ratios, volcanic ash layers, compaction problems)
  • Modern monitoring: long-term data sets (Mauna Loa), electronic equipment, satellites (LEO vs geostationary), Argo floats, remote sensing of ice thickness / area / sea level
  • Feedback mechanisms and tipping points adding uncertainty; ecological uncertainty (species survival, distribution, fragmentation)
  • Computer models: how they are tested and refined; importance of accurate representative data; scenario-based predictions and ranges
  • Uncertainty over ENSO / Conveyor / jet stream responses
  • Evaluation and conclusion: confidence in the overall trend and human cause despite detailed uncertainty; role of peer review and international bodies (IPCC)
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
