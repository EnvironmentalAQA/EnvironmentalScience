Question 215 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-62 · HAB-14
[4 marks]02.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows a high biomass of life in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why growing coral reefs help to resist climate change.
[9 marks]02.3Discuss the importance of tropical coral reefs and evaluate the methods used to conserve them.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside the polyp cells
  • The algae absorb CO2 and excretory wastes from the polyp for photosynthesis and growth (efficient nutrient recycling)
  • They supply the polyp with oxygen and glucose - over 95% of its food energy
  • In return the algae gain a protected habitat and nutrients they could not absorb from the nutrient-deficient water
  • Polyps also feed on plankton captured by cilia and nematocysts
/ 4
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Corals deposit a skeleton of calcium carbonate (calcite)
  • Each carbonate molecule requires the absorption of a molecule of carbon dioxide, so reefs are a carbon sink
/ 2
02.3 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Importance: cover 1% of ocean area but support over a quarter of marine species; fisheries (fish, lobsters, conch); medicines (AZT, Ara-C, dolastatin, secosteroids, bryostatin, trabectedin, bone grafts); climate control as carbon sink; erosion protection of low-lying islands; tourism income
  • Threats: physical damage (anchors, divers, litter, coral extraction), collection of ornaments (CITES Appendix II), sedimentation from rivers and coastal development, pollution (oil, nutrients causing algal shading, sewage, pesticides, sunscreen), ocean acidification, bleaching from warming, overfishing (giant triton / crown of thorns starfish), introduced species (red lionfish), loss of mangroves and seagrass nurseries
  • Conservation: marine protected areas / national parks (Great Barrier Reef, Belize, Chagos), mooring buoys, diver education, No Take Zones which can increase catches elsewhere, sustainable ecotourism and fishing, protection of mangroves and seagrass, erosion and pollution control, planning control of ports and resorts
  • Fishing controls: closed seasons, minimum and maximum catchable sizes, bans on dynamite, trawling and small-mesh nets
  • Evaluation: local measures cannot control global threats (warming, acidification) which need international action; enforcement in remote areas is difficult; conflicts with local livelihoods; large vs small NTZs
  • Justified conclusion
/ 9
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 57-62; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

  • Wood biomass heating; insulation such as straw-bale housing; some electric vehicles
  • Habitat protection and creation: fencing rare plants from grazing, planting indigenous woodland (rowan, birch, willow); eagles and hen harriers nesting
  • Recyclables shipped off; garden / food waste composted; low-impact food production; beach litter collection
/ 3

  • Resources and wastes are local and visible so feedback is immediate, unlike cities supplied from far away
/ 2

  • DC grids do not need every generator synchronised in phase as AC grids do, so coordination across many countries is easier

[1 mark]05.1Which feature of the Earth prevents most atmospheric gases from escaping into space? Tick one box.
  • A  Its mass
  • B  Its magnetic field
  • C  Its distance from the Sun
  • D  Its speed of rotation
[2 marks]05.2Explain how the speed of rotation of the Earth and the tilt of its axis affect the conditions for life.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why the presence of liquid water on Earth depends on both the distance from the Sun and atmospheric pressure.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (1 marks)
  • Its mass
/ 1
05.2 (2 marks)
  • The 24-hour rotation period means the surface is not exposed to sunlight for long, so temperature extremes / excessive heating and cooling are reduced
  • The axis is tilted relative to the orbit so the amount of insolation at each latitude changes through the year, producing seasonal variations in conditions
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • The distance from the Sun gives surface temperatures between 0 and 35 °C in most areas: too close and water would boil / evaporate, too far and it would freeze
  • Atmospheric pressure (produced by the mass of gases held by gravity) is high enough to stop the water boiling / evaporating away; at much lower pressure there would be no liquid water

Question 610 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.1 · Genn pp. 28-29 · PRI-02
[8 marks]06.1Explain the meaning of each of the following terms and explain why such species are given conservation priority.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why the degree of population dispersal is considered when setting priorities.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (8 marks)
  • EDGE: Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered - few close relatives, often the last species of their genus / family (Bactrian camel, pygmy hippo, northern bald ibis); loss would remove a unique branch of evolution / genes
  • Endemic: found in only one area (often islands - Galapagos, Madagascar, Seychelles: Aldabra giant tortoise, red ruffed lemur); if lost locally there is no population elsewhere to recolonise
  • Keystone: disproportionately important for the structure of the community relative to its abundance - predation of dominant species, seed dispersal, habitat creation (jaguar, forest elephants keeping paths and water holes open, wolves, beavers); losing them causes loss of many other species
  • Flagship: high public profile (tiger, panda, elephant, orang-utan) raising funds and support that also protects the whole habitat and less charismatic species (fungi, bats, moths)
/ 8
06.2 (2 marks)
  • A fragmented population may consist of isolated groups each too small to be viable (resources, inbreeding) even if total numbers are large
  • A species confined to a single area (eg bonobos in one country) is vulnerable to any local change / conflict, unlike chimpanzees spread across many countries
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 28-29; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.1.

Question 910 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 314-318 · AGR-04

Table 1 shows the yield of a vegetable crop under different pest control methods over three years on one farm.

Table 1
MethodMean yield / t ha-1Pesticide applications per yearNon-target insect species recorded in field margins
Chemical insecticide only18.568
Crop rotation + beetle banks + hedgerows16.2031
Integrated control18.11-226
[4 marks]09.1Explain how crop rotation and beetle banks reduce pest damage.
[3 marks]09.2Use Table 1 to evaluate integrated control compared with the other two methods.
[3 marks]09.3Describe how the sterile male technique controls insect pests and explain why it is species-specific.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (4 marks)
  • Rotation: each crop has its own pests; growing a different crop each year (4-5 year cycle) means pests left at the end of a season die before that crop returns, so populations cannot build up early
  • Beetle banks / hedgerows provide habitat and overwintering sites for natural predators (ground beetles, ladybirds) that eat aphids
  • Rotation also evens out nutrient demands and includes legumes
/ 4
09.2 (3 marks)
  • Integrated control gives almost the same yield as chemical control (18.1 vs 18.5) with far fewer applications (1-2 vs 6)
  • It retains most of the biodiversity of cultural control (26 vs 31 species) whereas chemical control alone leaves only 8
  • Cultural control alone loses about 12% yield; integrated control combines cultural methods, resistant varieties and carefully timed specific non-persistent pesticides only when essential - best balance of yield, cost and impact
/ 3
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Large numbers of males are sterilised with gamma radiation and released
  • Females of many insects mate only once and store sperm, so mating with a sterile male produces no offspring; enough sterile males reduce fertile matings below replacement level (screw worm fly, Mediterranean fruit fly)
  • Only the target species' females mate with them so non-target species are unaffected; fails if sterilised males behave abnormally or re-colonisation occurs
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 314-318; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.

Table 1 shows the catch made by a shrimp trawler during one fishing trip.

Table 1
Component of catchMass / tonnes
Shrimps (target species)0.6
Crabs, sea urchins, molluscs and starfish5.9
Immature fish of commercial species1.9
[2 marks]10.1Calculate the percentage of the total catch that was by-catch.
[1 mark]10.2Explain why shrimp trawling produces such a high proportion of by-catch.
[3 marks]10.3Explain why k-selected species such as the common skate and the Greenland shark are especially vulnerable to overfishing.
[9 marks]10.4Evaluate the methods that can be used to reduce the environmental impacts of fishing.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (2 marks)
  • By-catch = 5.9 + 1.9 = 7.8 t; total = 8.4 t
  • 7.8 / 8.4 x 100 = 92.9% (accept 93%)
/ 2
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The nets have a very small mesh size so few non-target animals can escape
/ 1
10.3 (3 marks)
  • They breed at an old age (skate about 11 years; Greenland shark 100-150 years) and produce few young
  • They may reach a catchable size before they are sexually mature, so fishing can remove the whole breeding population before it has bred
  • Populations recover slowly, unlike r-selected species (herring, mackerel) which breed young and produce many young; they are caught as by-catch in demersal trawls so fishing continues even though they have no commercial value
/ 3
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Impacts: population decline of k-selected species, by-catch (immature target fish, over-quota fish, non-commercial species; drift nets - whales, dolphins, turtles; longlines - albatrosses; pair trawls - porpoises), ghost fishing, seabed damage by demersal trawls, coral and seagrass damage, dynamite fishing, food-web effects
  • Catch quotas: limit weight landed; work well for single-species pelagic shoals but in mixed fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead so the quota fails to protect them
  • Gear design: mesh size and square-mesh escape panels let small fish escape (diagonal mesh closes under drag); turtle exclusion devices; acoustic pingers for dolphins; curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting to protect albatrosses; biodegradable / radio-tracked traps to reduce ghost fishing
  • Restricted effort: limits on boat size, engine power and days at sea; decommissioning; restricted methods - drift-net bans, demersal trawling bans on sensitive seabeds (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank)
  • Evaluation: enforcement at sea is difficult; some methods reduce catch or raise costs; quotas need reliable stock data; effectiveness depends on species (single vs mixed shoals) and on international cooperation as fish migrate; as local stocks decline fishing moves to more distant waters
  • Justified conclusion weighing effectiveness of different methods
/ 9
