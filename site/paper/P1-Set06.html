Question 110 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 89-107 · CC-10
[9 marks]01.1Evaluate the success of the methods and strategies used to reduce global climate change.
[1 mark]01.2State what is meant by a carbon footprint.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Control of greenhouse gases: energy conservation (transport, buildings, industry), renewable and nuclear energy replacing fossil fuels, CCS, reduced deforestation and afforestation (carbon sequestration), methane capture from landfill / digesters, HFC controls, changes in agriculture
  • Geoengineering: white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - largely untried, possible unpredictable consequences
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement - targets, but non-binding / uneven participation; carbon trading and taxes; comparison of per capita carbon footprints between countries
  • Evidence of limited success: global CO2 concentrations still rising (about 280 ppm pre-industrial to over 410 ppm); temperatures up about 1 °C; emissions in some developed countries falling but rising in developing economies
  • Reasons for difficulty: fossil fuels underpin economies, need for major lifestyle change, cost, time delays and natural fluctuations masking effects, political disagreement, developing countries' right to develop
  • Adaptation strategies (flood defences, drainage, managed retreat, infrastructure changes) reduce impacts but do not address the cause
  • Comparison with the Montreal Protocol's success for ozone
  • Judgement: partial success - technology exists and some regional reductions achieved, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise; tipping points make urgency greater
/ 9
01.2 (1 marks)
  • The total greenhouse gas / CO2 emissions caused by an individual, organisation, product or country (often expressed per capita per year)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 89-107; AQA spec 3.2.1.2.

Question 410 marks · Pesticides · spec 3.4.3.2.8 · Genn pp. 265-266 · PEST-04
[9 marks]04.1Neonicotinoids are the most widely used group of insecticides. Explain how the properties of neonicotinoids affect their impact on bee populations and describe the methods used to reduce this impact.
[1 mark]04.2State one way in which pesticide use in agriculture may be reduced by using natural processes.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Neonicotinoids are neurotoxins similar to nicotine; they inhibit the neurotransmitter acetylcholine
  • High toxicity to insects but low toxicity to vertebrates because they cannot cross the vertebrate blood-brain barrier
  • Very toxic to bees: sub-lethal doses reduce navigation ability, foraging and immunity to disease; linked to colony decline
  • Water soluble and moderately persistent: remain in soil, absorbed by roots and translocated (systemic), so present in pollen and nectar
  • Persistence may allow build-up in aquifers / soil; broken down fastest in sunlight and by soil microbes
  • Synergism with some fungicides increases toxicity to bees
  • Exposure greatest when sprayed onto crops or when seed-coating dust is blown by wind
  • Control: seed coating rather than spraying; spraying when crops are not in flower / at night; still days to reduce drift
  • Restrictions on use (EU bans on outdoor use on flowering crops); use of alternative pest control (cultural, biological, integrated control)
  • Evaluation: reduced pesticide use but neonicotinoid alternatives (pyrethroids) also harm pollinators; trade-off with crop yields
/ 9
04.2 (1 marks)
Any 1 from:
  • Biological control (introduced predators / pathogens)
  • Predator habitats (hedgerows, beetle banks)
  • Crop rotation
  • Companion / barrier crops
  • Sterile male technique / pheromone traps
  • Genetic resistance
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 265-266; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.8.
Question 510 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 397-399 · RM-07
[6 marks]05.1Complete Table 1 to show a suitable sampling technique for each group of organisms and one limitation of the technique.
Table 1
OrganismsTechniqueOne limitation
Invertebrates active on the ground surface at night
Invertebrates living in leaf litter
Invertebrates on tree branches
[2 marks]05.2Explain why a light trap cannot be used to prove that a moth species is absent from a site.
[2 marks]05.3Explain how a motor-driven suction sampler can give a population density, whereas a pooter cannot.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (6 marks)
  • Ground surface: pitfall trap (container sunk level with the surface, cover, checked after 24 h) - only mobile surface animals; trapped predators eat others; preservative attracts / repels; escapes
  • Leaf litter: Tullgren funnel (sample on mesh under a light; heat / light drive invertebrates down into a container for a standard time) - only species repelled by heat / light; some die or stop moving; large ones cannot pass the mesh
  • Branches: beating tray (branch beaten a set number of times over a sheet) - hard to standardise vigour; some species not dislodged / fly away; high branches unreachable
/ 6
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Moths only fly as adults for part of the year and activity depends on weather / season, so a species may be present but not caught
  • Brightness / wavelength of the lamp affects attractiveness so catches vary between traps
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • The area of ground covered by the suction pipe is known so invertebrates per m2 can be calculated
  • A pooter is only a handling / collection device for individuals already found, not a sampling method
/ 2

Table 1 shows the mean daily solar energy received on a horizontal surface at three locations.

[1 mark]06.1Use Table 1 to calculate how many times more solar energy is received in December in the Sahara than in southern England.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why northern Norway receives more solar energy in June than in December.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why concentrating solar power systems using parabolic reflectors only work where there is no cloud.
[1 mark]06.4Explain why the low energy density of solar power is a problem for large-scale generation.
[9 marks]06.5Evaluate the extent to which new technologies could overcome the problems of using solar power as a large-scale energy resource.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (1 marks)
  • 4.2 / 0.7 = 6
/ 1
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Long summer days (almost 24 hours of daylight) so the total energy received is high, even though the Sun is low
  • In winter days are very short / the Sun does not rise, so light levels are extremely low
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Reflectors can only focus parallel rays of direct sunlight onto the absorber
  • Clouds, smoke and dust scatter the light so it cannot be focused and intensified
/ 2
06.4 (1 marks)
  • Very large areas of collectors are needed to harness significant amounts of energy (and the changing angle of incidence reduces it further)
/ 1
06.5 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Problems: intermittency (daily and seasonal cycles), unpredictable cloud cover, low energy density, changing angle of incidence, land requirements, water for cleaning in deserts, toxic wastes in manufacture (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium), efficiency loss at high temperature
  • Photothermal: evacuated tubes reduce conduction / convection losses; thermal stores (water, sand, concrete, molten salt) allow later use; passive solar architecture, brise soleil, light tubes; heat pumps deliver up to four times the energy used
  • Photovoltaic developments: multijunction cells absorbing more wavelengths, anti-reflective / moth-eye textured surfaces (smooth cells reflect 30%), PV-thermal hybrids cooling the cell, transparent PV in windows, heliostats keeping the optimum angle, self-cleaning nanohydrophobic surfaces
  • Concentrating solar power with molten-salt storage to 550 °C so steam turbines run when needed - overcomes intermittency
  • Integration: grid-connected solar farms, rooftop space avoiding land conflicts, batteries, pumped storage, links to energy storage and demand management
  • Evaluation: strong in sunny deserts / low latitudes, weaker in high latitudes in winter; cost falling; manufacturing impacts; no single technology solves all problems; justified conclusion

Question 910 marks · Noise pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.13 · Genn pp. 287 · NOISE-02
[3 marks]09.1Describe three effects of noise on human health other than hearing damage.
[3 marks]09.2Explain how noise can damage non-living objects.
[2 marks]09.3Explain how noise can reduce the breeding success of birds.
[2 marks]09.4Explain why military sonar is a threat to cetaceans.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Stress-related illness: ulcers, high blood pressure, heart disease
  • Behavioural changes: irritability, aggression, lack of concentration leading to accidents
  • Communication problems / educational underperformance (eg schools under flight paths)
  • Sleep disturbance
/ 3
09.2 (3 marks)
  • Acoustic fatigue: objects exposed to sound at their natural resonant frequency vibrate, causing stress cracking / structural failure (eg early jet engine cowlings)
  • Vibration damage: repetitive vibration from heavy vehicles damages buildings, bridges, underground pipes
  • Shock impacts: the force of a sudden very loud noise (sonic boom) breaks windows / dislodges masonry

  • Birds scared by sudden noise leave nests so predators take eggs / chicks; cliff-nesting seabirds knock eggs or chicks into the sea
/ 2

  • Whales / dolphins use hearing / echolocation to find food and communicate; loud sonar causes hearing damage if close to the source
  • Behavioural changes / disorientation possibly linked to mass strandings; reduced feeding success

Question 1010 marks · Importance & features of energy resources · spec 3.3.2-3.3.3 · Genn pp. 175-182 · ENF-06
[9 marks]10.1Discuss how the features of energy resources determine their suitability for different uses and their environmental sustainability.
[1 mark]10.2State one reason why nuclear power stations can be located where transport infrastructure is poor.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Features: renewable / non-renewable, depletable, abundance, energy density, locational constraints, intermittency and predictability, need for conversions, applicability, ease of storage and transport, technological development, political and economic influences, environmental impacts
  • Fossil fuels: high energy density, easy storage and transport, reach high temperatures, well-developed technologies and infrastructure - but finite, polluting (acid rain, smoke, CO2), politically concentrated (Middle East oil)
  • Nuclear: very high energy density, base load, few fuel deliveries - but complex, expensive, waste, public opposition
  • Renewables: low energy density, intermittent (wind, solar unpredictable; tidal predictable), locationally constrained, cannot be stored without conversion, need storage / grid, cannot reach fossil-fuel temperatures or replace liquid vehicle fuels yet - but renewable, low pollution, falling costs
  • Sustainability concepts: impacts before use (extraction, processing, equipment embodied energy) and as a consequence of use (pollution, habitat damage, depletion)
  • Economic and political factors: subsidies, guaranteed prices, planning, external costs not included in price, energy security
  • Judgement: a diverse mix matched to uses (electricity vs heat vs transport) with storage is most sustainable; transition must start before depletion
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Very high energy density fuel means only a few tens of tonnes are needed each year, compared with 10 000 t of coal per day for a coal-fired station
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 175-182; AQA spec 3.3.2-3.3.3.
