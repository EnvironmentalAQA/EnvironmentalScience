Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

In the troposphere human activity enhances the greenhouse effect: fossil fuels and deforestation add CO2, rice, livestock and landfill add methane, fertilisers add nitrous oxide, and CFCs and tropospheric ozone are new absorbers. More outgoing infrared is absorbed and re-emitted, warming the surface, while smoke and aerosols scatter sunlight and land-use change alters albedo and evapotranspiration. In the stratosphere, CFCs, halons and HCFCs release chlorine and bromine that catalyse ozone destruction, shifting the O / O2 / O3 equilibrium so that less UV is absorbed there and more reaches the surface, cooling the stratosphere as well.

The consequences run through every system the atmosphere's energy drives: climate, thermohaline circulation, the hydrological cycle, sea level and species distributions, plus UV damage to organisms. Predicting them is hard because the processes are linked - one change alters others - and because feedback can amplify (albedo loss, methane release) or damp (cloud cover, photosynthesis) the effect, sometimes past tipping points. Time lags from ocean heat capacity and the long residence times of CO2 and CFCs, natural variability from solar output, volcanoes and orbital cycles, sparse historical data replaced by proxies, and models built on different assumptions all add uncertainty.

Some things are nevertheless well established: the physics of infrared absorption, the chemistry of ozone loss, and the success of the Montreal Protocol. What is uncertain is the scale and timing of regional impacts, which is exactly why the precautionary principle, not certainty, should guide action.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

Humans release greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels into the troposphere, which traps more heat and causes global warming. CFCs go into the stratosphere and destroy the ozone layer, so more UV reaches the Earth. It is hard to predict what will happen because the atmosphere is complicated and scientists do not know everything about it. There could be feedback effects that make it worse.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 gives one cause per layer; Level 3 lists several gases and processes and explains the mechanism (absorption and re-emission; catalytic destruction shifting an equilibrium).
  • 'Difficulties of prediction' is answered in Level 3 with specific reasons (linked processes, feedback, time lags, natural variability, proxy data, models); Level 1 says 'complicated'.
  • Level 3 evaluates - separating what is certain from what is not - and links this to the precautionary principle.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Solar power's problems are intrinsic: it is intermittent with daily and seasonal cycles, cloud makes it unpredictable and scatters light so it cannot be focused, its low energy density demands large collector areas, the angle of incidence keeps changing, cleaning in deserts uses scarce water, manufacturing PV produces toxic wastes such as silicon tetrachloride and cadmium, and PV efficiency falls as cells heat up.

New technology addresses most of these. Evacuated-tube photothermal panels cut conduction and convection losses, and thermal stores of water, sand, concrete or molten salt hold heat for later; passive solar design, brise soleil and light tubes reduce demand; heat pumps deliver up to four times the energy used to run them. Multijunction PV cells absorb more wavelengths, moth-eye anti-reflective surfaces stop the 30% of light a smooth cell reflects, PV-thermal hybrids cool the cell and use the heat, transparent PV turns windows into generators, heliostats keep panels at the optimum angle and nanohydrophobic surfaces self-clean. Concentrating solar power with molten salt stored at 550 °C runs steam turbines when electricity is wanted, directly overcoming intermittency, and grid-connected farms, rooftops that avoid land conflicts, batteries and pumped storage integrate the output.

Even so, no technology changes the fact that winter insolation at high latitudes is tiny, that CSP needs cloudless skies, or that manufacturing has impacts. Solar can dominate supply in sunny, low-latitude regions and contribute strongly elsewhere in summer, but overcoming its problems fully depends on storage and a diverse energy mix rather than on solar technology alone.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (2 marks)

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Strategies fall into control of greenhouse gases, geoengineering, international agreements and adaptation. Emissions are cut by energy conservation in transport, buildings and industry, by replacing fossil fuels with renewables and nuclear, by carbon capture and storage, by reducing deforestation and planting forests, and by capturing methane from landfill. Geoengineering ideas - white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - remain largely untried with unpredictable side effects. Adaptation, such as flood defences and managed retreat, reduces impacts but not the cause.

The Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement set targets, but they are weak or non-binding and participation is uneven; carbon trading and taxes exist but per capita footprints still differ enormously between countries. The evidence of success is limited: atmospheric CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 410 ppm and temperature by about 1 °C, and although emissions are falling in some developed countries they are rising in developing economies.

The difficulty is that fossil fuels underpin economies, so control demands lifestyle change, cost, and political agreement between nations with different priorities, while time delays and natural fluctuations mask the effect of any action. By contrast the Montreal Protocol succeeded because ozone was a substitution problem. The judgement must be partial success: the technology exists and regional reductions prove it works, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise, and tipping points make the delay dangerous.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Methods to reduce climate change include using renewable energy such as wind and solar instead of fossil fuels, saving energy, and planting trees to absorb carbon dioxide. International agreements like the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement have set targets for countries to reduce emissions. However carbon dioxide levels are still rising, so the methods have not been very successful so far. This is because countries rely on fossil fuels and do not always keep to the agreements. More needs to be done.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 covers the main strategies and reaches a conclusion, but with no quantitative evidence (280 to 410 ppm, 1 °C) and no mention of geoengineering or adaptation.
  • Level 3 explains why success is limited (economic dependence, time lags, natural variability, political disagreement) rather than just stating that it is.
  • The Montreal comparison and the tipping-point point give Level 3 the breadth and judgement the top band requires.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Whether a resource suits a use depends on its features: renewable or not, abundance, energy density, locational constraints, intermittency and predictability, the conversions needed, ease of storage and transport, how developed the technology is and its impacts. Fossil fuels dominate because they combine high energy density, easy storage and transport, high temperatures for industry and mature infrastructure - which is why petrol and diesel still monopolise transport. But they are finite, cause acid rain, smoke and CO2, and are politically concentrated. Nuclear fuel has the highest energy density and gives reliable base load with few deliveries, yet the technology is complex and expensive and produces long-lived waste against public opposition.

Renewables have the opposite profile: low energy density needing large collectors, intermittency (wind and solar unpredictable, tidal predictable), strong locational constraints and no direct storage, so they need conversion, storage and grid links and cannot yet reach fossil-fuel temperatures or replace liquid fuels. Sustainability must count impacts before use - extraction, processing, embodied energy of equipment - as well as pollution, habitat damage and depletion during use, and economics and politics (subsidies, planning, external costs left out of prices, energy security) shape what is actually chosen.

No single resource is suitable for everything. Electricity, heat and transport have different requirements, so the most sustainable system is a diverse mix matched to each use with storage, and the transition must begin before depletion forces it.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (2 marks)

Energy resources have different features. Fossil fuels are non-renewable and will run out but they have a high energy density. Renewable energy like wind and solar will not run out but they are intermittent because the wind does not always blow. Nuclear power produces radioactive waste. Renewable energy is more sustainable because it does not produce carbon dioxide. So we should use more renewable energy.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 mentions two or three features; Level 3 works through the full set and links each to a specific use (transport, base load, industry).
  • Level 3 includes sustainability concepts (impacts before and during use, external costs) and political factors that Level 1 omits entirely.
  • The conclusion in Level 3 is a reasoned position (a diverse mix matched to uses); Level 1's 'use more renewables' does not follow from a discussion.
