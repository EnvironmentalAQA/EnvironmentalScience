Question 110 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.2 · Genn pp. 228-231 · ECON-04

Table 1 shows the rate of heat loss through 1 m2 of different glazing systems for a 1 °C temperature difference (U-value).

Table 1
GlazingHeat loss / W m-2 K-1
Single glazing5.8
Double glazing, air filled2.8
Double glazing, argon filled, low-e glass1.6
Triple glazing, krypton filled, low-e glass0.8
Double glazing, vacuum (0.2 mm gap)0.74
[3 marks]01.1A house has 20 m2 of single glazing. The average temperature difference between inside and outside over the heating season is 12 °C. Calculate the reduction in the rate of heat loss if the windows are replaced with argon-filled low-e double glazing.
[2 marks]01.2Explain why the gap between the panes should be as large as possible but not too large.
[2 marks]01.3Explain how low-emissivity glass and inert gas filling each reduce heat loss.
[2 marks]01.4Explain why vacuum double glazing has a very narrow gap and where it is used.
[1 mark]01.5State one reason why insulation thickness has an optimum value.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • Single: 5.8 x 20 x 12 = 1392 W
  • Argon: 1.6 x 20 x 12 = 384 W
  • Reduction = 1008 W (accept 1010)
/ 3
01.2 (2 marks)
  • A wider gap of trapped gas reduces conduction because gas is a poor conductor
  • If the gap is too wide convection currents start in the gas, carrying heat across; the optimum depends on the gas and window size
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Low-e coating reflects long-wavelength infrared back into the room, reducing radiation losses
  • Argon / krypton / xenon have lower thermal conductivity than air so less conduction across the gap
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Panes must be held apart by small spacers which cannot be long, so the gap is only about 0.2 mm
  • Used where there is not room for thick gas-filled multiple glazing (eg historic buildings)
/ 2
01.5 (1 marks)
  • Each extra layer saves less energy (diminishing returns) while cost rises, so beyond the optimum the cost of insulation exceeds the value of energy saved
/ 1

Question 310 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.4 · Genn pp. 156-157 · BGC-06
[3 marks]03.1Explain why phosphorus is more likely than nitrogen to be a limiting factor on biological productivity.
[2 marks]03.2Explain how mycorrhizal fungi increase phosphate uptake by plants.
[3 marks]03.3Describe how human activities have altered the phosphorus cycle.
[2 marks]03.4Explain two strategies for the sustainable management of phosphorus supplies.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • There is no gaseous / atmospheric reservoir of phosphorus (no equivalent of nitrogen fixation)
  • Most phosphorus compounds have low solubility so little is available in soil water
  • Phosphorus lost to marine sediments only returns through very slow processes: mountain building (uplift) and weathering of rocks
/ 3
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Fungal hyphae form a symbiotic relationship with plant roots and spread through a large volume of soil
  • Greatly increasing the surface area for absorbing (poorly mobile) phosphate ions, passed to the plant in exchange for carbohydrates
/ 2
03.3 (3 marks)
  • Mining of calcium phosphate rock (and past mining of guano deposits, eg Peru, Nauru, now largely exhausted)
  • Treated to make more soluble ammonium phosphate fertilisers, mobilising phosphorus into the environment
  • Fertiliser runoff / eroded soil / sewage effluent carry phosphates into water bodies causing eutrophication (with nitrates); harvesting removes phosphorus from farmland
/ 3
03.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Use biological wastes (manure, sewage sludge, compost) as fertiliser to return phosphates rather than mining rock
  • Breed crops that absorb phosphate more efficiently
  • Maintain conditions for mycorrhizal fungi (less tillage, fewer fungicides, organic matter)
  • Recover phosphate from sewage effluent by iron(III) sulfate precipitation; reduce soil erosion
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 156-157; AQA spec 3.2.4.4.

Figure 1
[2 marks]04.1Use Figure 1 to suggest the minimum number of traps needed to produce a reliable mean. Give a reason for your answer.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why the number of samples needed depends on the degree of scatter of the results.
[3 marks]04.3The student wants to compare woodlouse numbers in a woodland and in a grassland. Describe how the sampling should be standardised.

04.1 (2 marks)
  • About 40-50 traps
  • Because the running mean stops changing / fluctuating with fewer than this the mean is unreliable
/ 2
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Widely scattered results (large standard deviation) mean a single sample may not be representative
  • More samples are needed so that the effect of variability is averaged out and the mean is representative / significance can be assessed
/ 2
04.3 (3 marks)

  • Traps emptied at the same time of day / same weather conditions in both habitats

  • Other variables that could affect results (eg rainfall, temperature) monitored or controlled
/ 3

  • Flying insect activity changes with weather over minutes to weeks (temperature, wind) and diurnally
  • Samples on different occasions are needed to produce a mean that represents the typical value or to identify trends
  • The interval between samples should match the rate at which the factor changes, found from a preliminary study
/ 3

  • Numbers fell from about 210 pairs in 1945 to a minimum of about 25 in 1965 (steepest fall 1955-1960)
  • Numbers then recovered / rose steadily after 1970, reaching about 190 by 1985 (not yet back to 1945 level)

Question 610 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 239-240 · PPRO-02
[3 marks]06.1Explain the difference between a mutagen, a carcinogen and a teratogen.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why a birth abnormality caused by a teratogen cannot be inherited by future generations, whereas one caused by a mutagen may be.
[2 marks]06.3Distinguish between somatic and gonadic effects of a mutagen.
[3 marks]06.4Explain what is meant by synergism and give an example.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Mutagen: an agent that changes the chemical structure / base sequence of DNA (causes mutations), eg ionising radiation, UV, PCBs, cadmium, asbestos
  • Carcinogen: a mutagen that causes cancer / uncontrolled cell division producing a tumour
  • Teratogen: an agent that causes birth abnormalities by preventing normal gene expression (inhibiting proteins / enzymes) without changing DNA structure, eg mercury, 2,4,5-T
/ 3
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Teratogens do not change the DNA structure, so the abnormality is not in the genes and cannot be passed on
  • Mutagens change DNA; if the mutation is in a gamete / egg / sperm (gonadic effect) it can be inherited
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Somatic: mutation in a general body cell, affecting the individual only (eg cancer / tumour)
  • Gonadic: mutation in cells of the ovaries or testes / gametes / embryo, which may cause abnormalities in offspring
/ 2
06.4 (3 marks)
  • Two or more pollutants whose effects interact to produce a different (usually more serious) effect
  • The pollutants themselves do not react to form a new pollutant (that would be a secondary pollutant)
  • Example: ozone damages leaf cuticles so sulfur dioxide can cause more damage to the exposed cells; smoke and sulfur dioxide in smogs; neonicotinoids with fungicides
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 239-240; AQA spec 3.4.1.
Question 710 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 187-188 · FN-01

Figure 1 shows the percentage of the oil in a reservoir that can be recovered using different recovery techniques.

Figure 1
[2 marks]07.1Describe how primary oil recovery works and explain why it recovers only about 20% of the oil.
[2 marks]07.2Describe secondary recovery and explain how it can be linked to carbon capture and storage.
[3 marks]07.3Describe three tertiary (enhanced) recovery techniques.
[3 marks]07.4Explain three advantages of directional drilling.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Natural pressure of gas above the oil (or dissolved in it) or water beneath forces oil up the production well; pump-jacks ('nodding donkeys') increase flow
  • Pressure falls as oil is removed so the remaining oil no longer flows to the well
/ 2
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Water or natural gas is pumped down an injection well to maintain the pressure and flow of oil (recovery to about 40%)
  • Captured CO2 can be pumped down instead, increasing oil recovery while storing the CO2 underground
/ 2
07.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Steam injection (from combustion or solar parabolic concentrators) to heat the oil and reduce its viscosity
  • Controlled underground combustion to heat the oil
  • Detergents / solvents to reduce surface tension so oil flows more easily
  • Bacteria that partially digest heavy oil into lighter oils and produce CO2 to maintain pressure
/ 3
07.4 (3 marks)
  • Many wells can be drilled from a single platform, reducing land take / number of rigs
  • Deposits under locations where rigs cannot be placed (urban areas, sensitive habitats) can be reached
  • Drilling can follow softer strata (quicker) and target multiple small reservoirs up to 10 km away, increasing total recovery

Question 810 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 214-215 · REN-06

Figure 1 shows the water level inside and outside a tidal barrage over one 12.5-hour tidal cycle and the periods when electricity is generated.

Figure 1
[2 marks]08.1Use Figure 1 to explain when electricity is generated by the barrage.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why tidal power is intermittent but predictable.
[2 marks]08.3Explain the difference between spring and neap tides and why this affects output.
[2 marks]08.4Explain how a tidal lagoon can be used to generate electricity when there is no tidal flow.
[2 marks]08.5Give two coastal features that make a site suitable for tidal power.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • When there is a difference in level between the sea and the basin: on the ebb (about 6-8 hours) water flows out through the turbines
  • Also possible on the flood when the sea is higher than the basin; no generation when levels are equal (slack water)
/ 2
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Two periods of slack water in each 12.5-hour cycle (twice in about 25 hours) give no flow; the timing does not match demand
  • Tides depend on the positions of the Moon and Sun which are known, so output can be forecast years ahead and alternative supplies planned
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Spring tides: Sun and Moon aligned so their gravitational effects combine giving a large tidal range; neap: effects oppose giving a small range
  • Energy depends on the mass of water and height difference, so output is much greater at springs
/ 2
08.4 (2 marks)
  • When demand is low, surplus electricity pumps water into the lagoon above sea level
  • The stored water is released through the turbines later when demand is high (pumped storage)
/ 2
08.5 (2 marks)
  • Large tidal range (eg Severn Estuary)
  • Estuaries, bays or headlands / channels that focus the flow and increase velocity
/ 2

Question 1010 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3, 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 108-113, 105-106 · OZ-04
[9 marks]10.1Ozone depletion is often described as an environmental problem that has been tackled successfully. Compare the effectiveness of the methods used to restore the ozone layer with the effectiveness of attempts to tackle global climate change, and explain the reasons for the difference.
[1 mark]10.2Name the international agreement of 1985 that preceded the Montreal Protocol and state its main weakness.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Stages of scientific method illustrated by ozone: identification (Antarctic UV data), hypothesis (Rowland-Molina 1974), data collection / analysis (Dobson units, satellites, balloons confirming ClO), proposed solutions, enactment (Vienna 1985, Montreal 1987)
  • Evidence of success: ozone hole area and depth stabilised and beginning to shrink; CFC concentrations in the atmosphere falling; UV levels not rising further; global ozone loss limited to about 4%
  • Reasons for success: international recognition of serious consequences (skin cancer); agreement of nearly every country; legally binding targets with a fund for developing countries; relatively few producers / products; alternatives (HCFCs, HFCs, pump sprays, hydrocarbons) already available and cheap; no lifestyle change needed
  • Limits: persistence of CFCs means recovery takes decades; some ODSs still used; HFC replacements are greenhouse gases
  • Climate change contrast: greenhouse gases come from almost every human activity (energy, transport, agriculture, industry) in every country; fossil fuels underpin economies so control requires major lifestyle and economic change
  • Agreements (Kyoto, Paris) have weaker / non-binding targets, disputes between developed and developing nations; emissions still rising; natural fluctuations, time delays and tipping points make cause-effect less clear to the public
  • Judgement: ozone control was a technical substitution problem; climate change is a systemic one, so the ozone success is only partly transferable
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer - no legally binding goals
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-113, 105-106; AQA spec 3.2.1.3, 3.2.1.2.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Soil is often described as a non-renewable resource. Discuss the causes and consequences of soil degradation and evaluate the strategies that can be used to manage soils sustainably.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Features of fertile soil: water, nutrients, air, organic matter, pH, biota, texture, structure, depth; slow rate of soil formation vs rapid erosion (dynamic equilibrium broken)
  • Causes of degradation: vegetation removal, ploughing vulnerable soils, overgrazing, reduced organic matter and biota, cultivating steep slopes, compaction, salinisation from irrigation, nutrient depletion, pollution

  • USLE as a tool for estimating loss and testing management
  • Management: long-term crops, low / zero tillage, contour ploughing, tied ridging, terracing, stone rows, windbreaks, multicropping / strip cropping, mulching, organic matter, livestock control, drainage / pH / nutrient management, conservation of soil biota
  • Links to sustainable agriculture, carbon storage, hydrological cycle, circular economy
  • Evaluation and judgement on which strategies are most effective in different environments

[25 marks]11.2Discuss the difficulties in monitoring and predicting global climate change and evaluate the methods used to overcome them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Nature of the problem: interconnected atmosphere-hydrosphere-biosphere systems; changes over different time and spatial scales; natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) masking or exaggerating anthropogenic change; time delay between cause and effect (ocean heat capacity)
  • Limitations of historic data: sparse global coverage, unsophisticated equipment, heat island bias, no atmospheric composition records
  • Proxy data and their reliability: dendrochronology, coral growth rings, pollen in dated sediments, ice cores (gas bubbles, isotope ratios, volcanic ash layers, compaction problems)
  • Modern monitoring: long-term data sets (Mauna Loa), electronic equipment, satellites (LEO vs geostationary), Argo floats, remote sensing of ice thickness / area / sea level
  • Feedback mechanisms and tipping points adding uncertainty; ecological uncertainty (species survival, distribution, fragmentation)
  • Computer models: how they are tested and refined; importance of accurate representative data; scenario-based predictions and ranges
  • Uncertainty over ENSO / Conveyor / jet stream responses
  • Evaluation and conclusion: confidence in the overall trend and human cause despite detailed uncertainty; role of peer review and international bodies (IPCC)
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
