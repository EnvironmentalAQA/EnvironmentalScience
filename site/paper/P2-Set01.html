Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

New technologies have transformed what can be monitored and how often. Image databases of unique markings - tiger stripes, whale-shark spots, dolphin fin scars - let individuals be recognised, giving territory size, movements, lifespan and social structure without repeated capture, though only for species with unique features. Motion-sensitive and infrared cameras and CCTV record activity day and night with far less disturbance than observers, especially at nests.

Genetics adds detection where nothing can be seen: eDNA in water reveals great crested newts or invasive carp from shed cells, and DNA databases identify which regional population timber, ivory or fish came from, supporting enforcement. Bat detectors and sonograms give presence, abundance and activity of species heard but not seen. Position monitoring uses radio, GPS and acoustic transmitters, or tiny geolocator tags for birds too small to carry a transmitter, while data recorders on drones, AUVs, balloons, aircraft, satellites or the animals themselves collect abiotic data. Satellites map land use and deforestation in visible light, vegetation density and temperature in infrared, and wind, waves, sea level and oil slicks with radar that works through cloud.

The gains are coverage, continuity and reduced disturbance. The limits are cost, battery life and weight, the need to calibrate sensors and ground-truth remote data, ethical questions about tagging, and the fact that many species still require traditional methods and indirect evidence. New technology therefore complements rather than replaces fieldwork, but for rare, remote or nocturnal species it has made effective conservation possible where it was not before.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Other species supply resources we use directly: wood, fibres such as cotton and wool, oils, fuels and food. Because most farmed species were domesticated long ago and are often kept where they are poorly adapted, wild species such as the drought-resistant morama bean, the yeheb tree that grows on poor soils, perennial maize that needs no ploughing, and bison that graze grassland too poor for cattle offer new, better-adapted food sources. Wild species also carry genetic resources: crop wild relatives in Vavilov centres hold genes for disease resistance and salt tolerance that inbred commercial varieties lack.

Biomimetics copies adaptations into technology - humpback flipper tubercles in turbine blades, shark skin in friction-reducing coatings, termite-mound convection in naturally ventilated buildings, hollow bird bones in lightweight roofs. Medicines such as taxol, quinine, AZT and diosgenin came from wild species, and physiological research on marsupials, hippo skin and sponge proteins is helping with foetal development, burns and transplant rejection. Since only a tiny fraction of species has been studied, the unknown future value argues for conserving whole habitats.

Beyond resources, other species provide services we could not replace: regulation of atmospheric gases, evapotranspiration driving rainfall, the carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles run by microbes, soil formation by decomposers, and pollination, seed dispersal and habitat provision. These are unnoticed precisely because they are in dynamic equilibrium, so their economic value is underestimated. On balance the resource and service arguments alone justify conservation, but they are strongest when combined with the intrinsic and aesthetic case, because many species with no known use still sustain the systems we depend on.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Tropical coral reefs cover about one per cent of the oceans yet support over a quarter of marine species, a diversity built on the symbiosis between polyps and zooxanthellae that recycles nutrients in nutrient-poor water. They matter to people for fisheries of fish, lobsters and conch; for medicines such as AZT, Ara-C, dolastatin, secosteroids, bryostatin and trabectedin discovered in reef organisms; as a carbon sink, since every carbonate unit in coral skeleton absorbs a molecule of carbon dioxide; for dissipating wave energy that would erode low-lying islands; and for tourism income.

They are also unusually vulnerable because they need narrow abiotic conditions. Anchors, divers, litter and coral extraction kill polyps; ornaments are collected; sediment from rivers and coastal development smothers them; oil, sewage and nutrients cause algal shading; ocean acidification hinders skeleton formation; warming causes bleaching; overfishing of giant tritons lets crown-of-thorns starfish destroy reefs; introduced lionfish eat native fish; and loss of mangroves and seagrass removes the nurseries and sediment traps reefs depend on.

Marine protected areas such as the Great Barrier Reef, Belize and Chagos combine mooring buoys, diver education, no-take zones that raise catches outside them, sustainable tourism and protection of linked habitats, while fishing controls set closed seasons, minimum and maximum sizes and ban dynamite, trawling and small mesh. These work well against local threats where they are enforced, and no-take zones have measurable benefits, but they cannot touch warming and acidification, which need international action, enforcement in remote areas is weak, and restrictions conflict with local livelihoods. Local conservation buys time; only global climate action can secure the reefs.

  • Manipulation of food species: population density, monocultures, selective breeding, asexual reproduction, cross-breeding, AI / embryo transfer, GM; energetics - intensive vs extensive, energy subsidies, ratios, FCR, food chain losses
  • Impacts: habitat loss / change, reduced biodiversity, introduced species, pesticide and nutrient pollution, GHGs, hydrological changes, soil erosion; positive: plagioclimax habitats
  • Social, economic and political influences shaping methods
  • Sustainable strategies and evaluation; conclusion
