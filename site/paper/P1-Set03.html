Question 310 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.3-3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 183-196 · FN-07

03.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Fossil fuel technologies: secondary / tertiary recovery raising recovery from 20% to 60%; directional drilling and subsea wells; ROVs; fracking for tight oil and gas; tar sands / oil shales; coal gasification / liquefaction; enhanced gas recovery; methane hydrates; CCS (pre / post combustion, storage)

Table 1 shows four general approaches to pollution control.

[4 marks]05.1Complete Table 1 by giving one example of each approach.
Table 1
ApproachExample
Production prevention
Prevention of release
Post-release remediation
Alternative processes
[2 marks]05.2Explain why prevention of production is generally preferable to post-release remediation.
[4 marks]05.3Describe how each of the following new technologies helps to control pollution.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • Production prevention: desulfurisation of fuels before combustion; low-temperature (fluidised bed) combustion to prevent NOx; smokeless fuels
  • Prevention of release: electrostatic precipitators / bag filters / cyclones for smoke; catalytic converters; flue gas desulfurisation; bund walls; landfill liners
  • Post-release remediation: oil spill clean-up (booms, skimmers, dispersants, bioremediation); phytoremediation of metal-contaminated land; liming of acidified lakes
  • Alternative processes: electric vehicles instead of petrol / diesel; renewable energy instead of fossil fuels; pyrethroids instead of organochlorines; fuel cells instead of internal combustion engines
/ 4
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Remediation is often incomplete / cannot recover all the pollutant (eg oil spills, dispersed pollutants), so some damage still occurs
  • Prevention avoids the damage entirely and is usually cheaper than clean-up / avoids polluter-pays costs; moving from post-production treatment to non-release is a circular economy principle
/ 2
05.3 (4 marks)
  • Satellites detect and track the extent / movement of oil slicks over large areas, quickly and repeatedly, so clean-up can be directed and polluters identified
  • GPS tracking monitors ship positions so navigation errors / collisions / groundings are reduced and offshore shipping routes are enforced
  • Polymers with a high surface area adsorb metal ions from contaminated water / effluent so they can be removed and recovered
  • Plants that absorb heavy metals (hyperaccumulators) are grown on contaminated land, harvested and disposed of / metals recovered, cleaning the soil

Question 615 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 108-109 · OZ-01
[3 marks]06.1Describe how ozone is formed and destroyed naturally in the stratosphere.
[6 marks]06.2Explain the properties of CFCs that made them useful, and the properties that cause ozone depletion (the Rowland-Molina hypothesis).
[3 marks]06.3Explain why increased UVB reaching the Earth's surface is harmful to living organisms. Give three effects.
[1 mark]06.4State what is meant by an ozone hole.
[1 mark]06.5Give one reason why concentration in ppm is not used to measure ozone depletion.
[1 mark]06.6Give one reason why the first evidence for ozone depletion came from ground-based measurements.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • UV light splits diatomic oxygen (O2) into two monatomic oxygen atoms (O)
  • Monatomic oxygen reacts with diatomic oxygen to form ozone (O3)
  • UV light also splits ozone into O2 and O; the reactions form a dynamic equilibrium which absorbs UV (converting it to chemical energy / heat)
/ 3
06.2 (6 marks)
  • Useful: boiling points near ambient temperature so easily liquefied under low pressure (aerosol propellants, fridge / air-conditioning coolants); dissolve grease but do not damage electrical components (solvents); non-flammable; non-toxic
  • Persistence: chemically stable / unreactive in the troposphere so they remain long enough to be carried up to the stratosphere
  • Dissociation by UV: in the stratosphere higher UV levels break the carbon-chlorine bonds releasing chlorine free radicals
  • Chlorine reacts with monatomic oxygen (and ozone), preventing ozone formation; chlorine is regenerated so one atom destroys many ozone molecules; bromine / iodine act similarly
/ 6
06.3 (3 marks)
  • UVB energy is absorbed by cells and breaks up biological molecules / DNA causing mutations, skin cancer, cataracts, sunburn (human health)
  • Damage to leaf tissue and reduced photosynthesis / crop yields (plants)
  • Damage to marine algae, phytoplankton, corals and planktonic larvae (marine organisms), reducing productivity and food webs
/ 3
06.4 (1 marks)
  • An area where the total ozone column falls below 220 Dobson Units (normal is about 300 DU) - a level never recorded before CFC pollution
/ 1
06.5 (1 marks)
  • Concentration varies with altitude and the stratosphere is not normally sampled; Dobson Units measure the total column thickness instead
/ 1
06.6 (1 marks)
  • The British Antarctic Survey at Halley detected higher UV at ground level, showing ozone above had decreased (satellites above the stratosphere cannot measure downward UV directly)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-109; AQA spec 3.2.1.3.
Question 715 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 103-104, 371-372 · CC-07
[2 marks]07.1Explain the difference between a negative and a positive feedback mechanism.
[4 marks]07.2Describe two negative feedback mechanisms that may reduce global warming.
[4 marks]07.3Explain how melting permafrost and melting methane hydrate act as positive feedback mechanisms.
[3 marks]07.4Give three other positive feedback mechanisms.
[2 marks]07.5Explain what is meant by a climate tipping point.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Negative feedback: a change causes other changes that reduce the rate or size of the original change, re-establishing equilibrium
  • Positive feedback: a change causes other changes that increase the original change
/ 2
07.2 (4 marks)
  • Increased low-level cloud: higher temperatures increase evaporation and condensation, producing more cloud; clouds have a high albedo so more sunlight is reflected and warming is reduced
  • Increased photosynthesis: higher temperatures and CO2 increase photosynthesis, removing CO2; if stored in woody tissue atmospheric CO2 rises less
/ 4
07.3 (4 marks)
  • Permafrost: frozen waterlogged Arctic soils contain dead organic matter that decomposed anaerobically producing methane trapped in the frozen ground
  • Warming thaws the permafrost, releasing methane (a powerful greenhouse gas) and allowing further decomposition, causing more warming
  • Methane hydrate: methane from decomposed organic matter in deep sea sediments is held as a solid under high pressure and low temperature
  • If deep water warms the hydrate melts, releasing methane into the atmosphere and causing further warming
/ 4
07.4 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Reduced albedo as snow / ice melts
  • Ocean acidification reducing coral survival and carbon sequestration
  • Increased forest and peat fires releasing CO2
  • Faster decomposition of soil organic matter releasing CO2
  • Increased water vapour (a greenhouse gas) in warmer air
  • Increased cirrus (high) cloud which traps infrared
/ 3
07.5 (2 marks)
  • A point at which natural processes triggered by human-caused warming become self-sustaining / unstoppable
  • So that stopping the original human activity would no longer stop climate change (eg runaway permafrost melt, methane hydrate release, forest fires)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 103-104, 371-372; AQA spec 3.2.1.2, 3.6.1.

Question 910 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 269-272 · NUT-03

Figure 1 is a flow diagram of the main stages in a sewage treatment works using activated sludge treatment.

Figure 1
StageProcess
Pre-treatmentScreens and grit traps
Primary treatmentPrimary sedimentation
Secondary treatmentAeration tank then secondary sedimentation
Tertiary treatmentPhosphate stripping / micro-strainers / UV
Sludge treatmentAnaerobic digestion
[2 marks]09.1Explain the purpose of screens and grit traps.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why primary sedimentation is described as the most important process.
[3 marks]09.3Describe what happens in the aeration tank and explain why the sludge from the secondary sedimentation tank is returned to it.
[3 marks]09.4Explain why tertiary treatment may be needed before effluent is discharged, and describe one tertiary process.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • Screens (metal grills) trap floating / suspended non-biodegradable items (paper, plastic) which are landfilled or incinerated
  • Grit traps: the channel widens so flow slows and stones / road grit sink out because there is not enough kinetic energy to carry them
/ 2
09.2 (2 marks)
  • The effluent stands in large tanks so the faecal / organic solids sink and are removed as sludge
  • This removes over 95% of the organic matter from the fluid so the risk of deoxygenation of the river is greatly reduced
/ 2
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Aerobic bacteria digest / break down the remaining dissolved and suspended organic matter
  • Air (or pure oxygen) is mixed in by paddle wheels or air stones so oxygen shortage does not limit the aerobic process
  • The suspended bacteria collected in the secondary sedimentation tank are the 'activated sludge'; returning them maintains a large bacterial population so digestion is rapid
/ 3
09.4 (3 marks)
  • Effluent from secondary treatment may still contain dissolved inorganic nutrients (phosphates, nitrates) that cause eutrophication, and some bacteria
  • Needed where the discharge site is ecologically sensitive or used by many people (bathing water, drinking water abstraction)
  • Phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate; micro-strainers (very fine sieves) then UV light or chlorine to kill remaining bacteria
/ 3

  • In winter days are very short / the Sun does not rise, so light levels are extremely low

  • Problems: intermittency (daily and seasonal cycles), unpredictable cloud cover, low energy density, changing angle of incidence, land requirements, water for cleaning in deserts, toxic wastes in manufacture (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium), efficiency loss at high temperature
