Question 310 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.2 · Genn pp. 82-85 · LP-02
[8 marks]03.1Define each of the following ecological terms and give an example.
[2 marks]03.2Explain the difference between an ecosystem and a biome.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (8 marks)
  • Species: a group of closely related organisms that naturally interbreed to produce fertile offspring and are reproductively isolated from other groups (Passer domesticus, house sparrow); the only 'real' taxon
  • Niche: the role a species plays in its habitat - how it uses resources and relates to other species (tawny owl: nocturnal carnivore nesting in tree holes eating small mammals); two species cannot share exactly the same niche in one habitat
  • Community: all the populations of all species (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, protists, archaea) living in an area
  • Biome: a large geographical region with a specific climate and characteristic community, including all areas where it occurs (all tropical rainforests, tundra, savannah)
/ 8
03.2 (2 marks)
  • An ecosystem is the biotic community plus abiotic factors of one area, relatively self-contained (a particular coral reef)
  • A biome includes all separate ecosystems with the same climate and community type (all coral reefs); each reef is a different ecosystem within the same biome
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 82-85; AQA spec 3.1.3.2.
Question 410 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 33-35 · CBR-02

The Hawaiian goose (nene) was reduced by hunting, habitat loss and introduced predators to about 30 birds. A captive breeding programme started with just seven individuals and has increased the wild population to over 2000.

[4 marks]04.1Explain why starting the programme with seven individuals caused problems, and describe how these problems are managed.
[3 marks]04.2Explain three reasons, other than gene pool size, why species kept in captivity may fail to breed.
[3 marks]04.3Describe how artificial insemination and embryo transfer increase breeding success in captive mammals.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Very small gene pool so individuals are closely related and inbreeding is likely
  • Harmful recessive genes are expressed when both parents carry them: some goslings had thin hair-like feathers with poor insulation so survival in cold weather was low
  • A stud book records family trees so breeding is arranged between the least related individuals (often coordinated by a specialist zoo)
  • Exchange of individuals / semen between zoos; avoid hybridisation with related species / subspecies
/ 4
04.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Breeding is triggered by stimuli (day length, light, temperature, food, body fat) which may be unknown / not provided
  • Population interactions: mixing breeding and non-breeding individuals causes conflict; some need a choice of partners, others pair for life
  • Breeding habitat needed: courtship display sites, nest sites, social groups (flamingos only breed in large groups - mirrors used)
  • Stress / disturbance
/ 3
04.3 (3 marks)
  • AI: semen collected (and can be stored / cryopreserved for years) and inserted into the female, so animals need not be transported or meet; avoids dangerous mating (elephants, rhinos) and partner rejection
  • Embryo transfer: a female of the endangered species (eg bongo) is treated with hormones to release many eggs, which are fertilised and implanted into surrogate females of a common related species (eland)
  • So many offspring are produced per cycle instead of one calf per year, overcoming the shortage of breeding females
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 33-35; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3.
Question 510 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.1 · Genn pp. 338-339 · FISH-01

Figure 1 shows the seasonal changes in light intensity, nutrient concentration and phytoplankton biomass in the surface water of a temperate ocean in the northern hemisphere.

Figure 1
[4 marks]05.1Use Figure 1 to explain the spring peak and summer decline in phytoplankton biomass.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why tropical open oceans have low productivity all year despite high light and temperature.
[3 marks]05.3Explain why areas of upwelling and coastal waters are the most productive parts of the sea.
[1 mark]05.4Explain why phosphate is often the limiting nutrient in the sea.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • In winter nutrients are high (storms disturb sediments, deep mixing) but light is too low for photosynthesis
  • In spring light rises while nutrients are still high so growth is rapid - the spring bloom (peak about May)
  • The bloom uses up nutrients (falling to 10 by July); dead plankton sink out of the photic zone carrying nutrients to the seabed
  • So nutrients become limiting in summer despite high light; a smaller autumn bloom follows when storms return nutrients while light is still adequate
/ 4
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Warm surface water is stable (no seasonal mixing / storms) so nutrients lost to deep water in sinking dead organisms are not replaced
  • Nutrient (especially phosphate) concentrations in the photic zone remain very low - the limiting factor
/ 2
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Upwelling (offshore winds, seamounts, currents deflected upwards) brings cold nutrient-rich deep water into the photic zone
  • Coastal waters receive nutrients washed off the land in rivers and storms disturb shallow sediments
  • Nutrients plus light in the shallow photic zone allow high algal productivity supporting rich food webs and fisheries (eg Peru anchovy)
/ 3
05.4 (1 marks)
  • Phosphates have low solubility and no atmospheric reservoir; they are only added by river runoff or upwelling and are lost to sediments
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 338-339; AQA spec 3.5.2.1.

Question 710 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 22-23 · HUM-02
[6 marks]07.1Explain how each of the following changes in abiotic factors caused by human activities can make a habitat unsuitable for species living there.
[2 marks]07.2Explain how increased water turbidity caused by ploughing, mining or dredging harms aquatic organisms.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why species that were already rare are most likely to be lost when abiotic conditions change.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Drainage of wetlands / lowering water tables by groundwater abstraction dries out wetlands; HEP schemes alter river levels flooding sandbank nests of turtles / birds; species with narrow tolerance (specialists) affected first; sundews lost when drained soil becomes aerobic / nitrogen-rich and taller competitors invade
  • Hot water discharges and organic wastes (sewage) reduce dissolved oxygen, killing aerobic organisms such as fish and insect larvae
  • Acid mine drainage and acid rain gases lower pH, denaturing proteins of exposed tissues (fish eggs, gills) and dissolving calcium exoskeletons (crayfish)
/ 6
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Reduces light penetration so submerged plants cannot photosynthesise
  • Blocks the gills of filter feeders such as bivalve molluscs; sediment smothers the bed
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Rare species often have very specialised habitat requirements / narrow ranges of tolerance
  • A small change moves conditions outside their range while generalists survive; small populations cannot recover
