Question 110 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 395-396, 415-416 · RM-06

A student compared the invertebrates in a stream above and below a sewage outfall using kick sampling.

[3 marks]01.1Describe how kick sampling is carried out so that the samples from the two sites are comparable.
[3 marks]01.2Give three limitations of kick sampling.
[2 marks]01.3Explain how a Surber sampler improves on kick sampling.
[2 marks]01.4Explain why the invertebrates found give a better indication of long-term sewage pollution than a single measurement of dissolved oxygen.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • Net held with its straight edge on the river bed facing the current (open to the flow)
  • The bed in front is disturbed by kicking / scraping a boot for a standard number of kicks and time (eg 30 s)
  • Same substrate type, water depth and flow rate at both sites; same net mesh; repeat samples; invertebrates identified with a key and counted
/ 3
01.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Difficult to standardise the vigour / area kicked
  • Buried organisms or those fixed to stones are not dislodged
  • Some organisms swim and avoid the net
  • Flow rate varies and affects capture
  • Fragile organisms destroyed
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • A fixed area of bed is enclosed by a frame so the sampled area is standardised; side nets reduce escapes
  • But only works in fast enough flow and is hard to set on rough substrates / deep water
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Pollutant / oxygen levels fluctuate (eg with flow, time of day) so one reading may miss a pollution event
  • Sensitive taxa (stonefly, mayfly larvae) disappear and tolerant ones (midge larvae, hoglouse) remain, integrating conditions over weeks / months (biotic index)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 395-396, 415-416; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.

Question 310 marks · Dynamic equilibria, feedback & tipping points · spec 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 371-372 · SUS-02
[3 marks]03.1Explain why natural systems regulated by negative feedback rarely reach tipping points, and give an example of a natural tipping point in the geological past.
[4 marks]03.2Explain why diverse systems are more resistant to change than simple systems, using a natural and a human example.
[3 marks]03.3Give three positive feedback mechanisms by which human-induced warming may become self-sustaining.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • Negative feedback responds to any change by re-establishing the previous equilibrium
  • Only large unusual events overwhelm the feedback so a new equilibrium forms
  • Example: methane hydrate release at the end of the Permian causing climate change and mass extinction
/ 3
03.2 (4 marks)
  • In a diverse system each component is relatively less important so the loss / change of one has a small effect; alternative food sources and relationships buffer the change
  • Natural: coral reefs and tropical rainforests with complex food webs are stable; simple polar / desert systems fluctuate
  • Human: agroecosystems focus on a few crops and remove detritivores, pollinators and predators so are vulnerable to pests, disease, weather
  • Diverse technical systems: a society using a range of renewable energy resources is more secure than one relying on a single source
/ 4
03.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Melting permafrost releasing methane
  • Reduced albedo as ice / snow melt
  • Methane hydrate release
  • Forest and peat fires
  • Faster soil decomposition
  • Ocean acidification reducing sequestration
  • Cirrus cloud formation
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 371-372; AQA spec 3.6.1.
Question 410 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 352-355 · AQUA-01
[3 marks]04.1Explain why carnivorous species such as salmon dominate aquaculture in MEDCs while herbivores such as carp and tilapia dominate in LEDCs.
[3 marks]04.2Describe how all-female rainbow trout are produced and explain why this is done.
[4 marks]04.3Explain how each of the following abiotic factors is controlled in an intensive salmon farm.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • MEDCs: market demand and higher prices for the flavour of carnivorous fish (salmon, trout) justify high inputs (fishmeal, cooler climates suit them)
  • LEDCs: herbivores / omnivores feed on natural vegetation, phytoplankton and wastes so need low inputs; warm climates suit tilapia
  • Herbivores are more energy-efficient (lower trophic level) so more food per unit area
/ 3
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Some genetically female (XX) fish are treated with testosterone so they become functional males producing only X-carrying sperm
  • Crossed with normal females (XX) all offspring are XX females
  • Female trout meat has a better flavour; in tilapia the reverse (all males) because males grow larger / more efficiently
/ 3
04.3 (4 marks)
  • Salmon need high oxygen: water sprays / weirs aerate; wastes removed to prevent deoxygenation
  • Constant circular flow keeps fish swimming the same way - fewer collisions / injuries, more muscular fish, higher stocking density
  • Artificial lighting delays maturation (salmon stop growing at maturity) so larger fish; controls smoltification
  • Cool sites (Scotland, Norway) because warm water holds less oxygen although warmth increases growth
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 352-355; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.

Question 610 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 30-31 · PRI-03
[4 marks]06.1Describe four ways in which the Wildlife and Countryside Act (1981) protects wildlife in the UK.
[4 marks]06.2Explain the difference between CITES Appendix I and Appendix II, giving an example species for each.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why controlling international trade can be more effective than trying to stop collection of a species in its own country.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Designation of protected areas (SSSIs, MCZs)
  • Protection of wild birds and their nests (except some pests / game birds)
  • Protection of named mammals such as otters, dormice, red squirrels, badgers and their setts
  • Uprooting wild plants generally illegal
  • Bats must not be disturbed; roof timber treatments must be non-toxic to bats
/ 4
06.2 (4 marks)
  • Appendix I: species threatened with extinction - all international (commercial) trade banned, except movement for conservation breeding
  • Examples: great apes, big cats, rhinos, blue whale
  • Appendix II: species that may become threatened if trade is not closely controlled - trade permitted under licence from countries where the species is well protected
  • Examples: Honduras mahogany, hippopotamus, green iguana, great white / basking shark, Venus fly trap
/ 4
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Collection may be hard to police in remote areas / poor countries, but if the animals or products cannot be exported / imported there is no market
  • Removing the profit removes the incentive to collect
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 30-31; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.2.
Question 710 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.4, 3.1.3.1 · Genn pp. 43-46 · CBR-06
[6 marks]07.1Describe how each of the following abiotic factors can be managed to increase biodiversity in a nature reserve.
[4 marks]07.2Explain how biotic factors can be managed to increase the survival of a species. Give four examples.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Light: selective felling to create clearings for plants needing high light while retaining dense canopy for shade species; clearings also give warm sand / soil for lizard eggs; leave dead wood; woodland floor plants (bluebells) grow before the canopy leafs
  • Dissolved oxygen: narrowing a slow river increases flow and turbulence so more oxygen dissolves, suiting trout and mayfly larvae; shallow water warms quickly for egg development
  • Nutrients: removing fertile topsoil / not fertilising allows plants of nutrient-poor soils (eg sundew on acidic nitrate-deficient soils) that cannot compete on fertile ground; dung patches add local nutrients
/ 6
07.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Food: wildflower grasslands for seed-eating birds; providing food species
  • Control of predation: island nest sites unreachable by foxes; trapping / culling / eradicating introduced rats (Scilly Isles - Manx shearwater and storm petrel breeding success increased)
  • Control of competitors: removing rhododendron so wildflowers get light
  • Pollination: a range of flowering plants with different flowering times so pollinators have food all season
  • Seed dispersal species (hippos, elephants, monkeys) conserved; control of introduced pathogens
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 43-46; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4, 3.1.3.1.
Question 810 marks · Energy & material cycles: natural vs human systems · spec 3.6.3 · Genn pp. 373-374 · SUS-04
[4 marks]08.1Explain how natural material cycles differ from linear human systems of material use.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why the use of toxic metals in electronic products is a particular problem for sustainability.
[4 marks]08.3Describe how the design of a product can move it from a linear to a cyclical system.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (4 marks)
  • Natural cycles use a small number of abundant elements (C, H, O, N, P) built into monomers and polymers (carbohydrates, proteins); wastes of one process are raw materials for another so nothing is depleted or accumulates
  • Natural wastes are biodegradable / non-toxic and are broken down by decomposers into raw materials
  • Human systems are linear: extract - process - manufacture - use - discard; minerals are dispersed after use or mixed so recovery is difficult
  • Human wastes are often persistent and toxic (organochlorines, PCBs, heavy metals in electronics) and build up; fossil fuel use is inherently linear and unsustainable
/ 4
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Metals are dispersed in small amounts within complex mixed products so they cannot easily be separated / recovered at end of life
  • They leach from landfill or are released by incineration; persistent, bioaccumulating
/ 2
08.3 (4 marks)
  • Design for durability, repair and re-use (replaceable parts, no built-in batteries, spare parts and support, no planned obsolescence)
  • Easy identification of materials (coding) and separation of components so they can be recycled
  • Separation of technical materials (metals, plastics) from biological materials (which can be composted / digested)
  • Use of recycled and recyclable / biodegradable materials; reduced packaging and material use (Cradle to Cradle)
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 373-374; AQA spec 3.6.3.
Question 910 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.3 · Genn pp. 73-76 · LP-03

A new pond was dug in a nature reserve. Table 1 shows the vegetation recorded at intervals over 60 years.

Table 1
Years after creationDominant vegetationWater depth at centre / m
1Single-celled algae, floating duckweed2.0
5Submerged pondweeds; reeds at margins1.8
15Reeds and reedmace over most of pond; water lilies1.0
30Marsh plants, sedges; willow and alder at edges0.2
60Willow / alder carr; oak seedlings0 (soil waterlogged)
[2 marks]09.1Name the type of sere shown in Table 1 and identify the pioneer species.
[5 marks]09.2Explain the changes shown in Table 1.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why a lithosere and a hydrosere in the same region end with the same climax community.
[1 mark]09.4Describe the early stages of a lithosere.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • Hydrosere
  • Single-celled algae (and floating plants) - arrive on birds / insects / from soil
/ 2
09.2 (5 marks)
  • Pioneers and later plants grow and die; dead organic matter plus sediment washed in fill the pond so water becomes shallower
  • As depth falls rooted plants (pondweeds then emergent reeds with leaves above water) can anchor and colonise; emergents shade submerged plants which die out
  • Sediment / soil accumulates and becomes able to support larger plants; water-tolerant trees (willow, alder) colonise the waterlogged soil
  • Transpiration by trees and further sediment dry the soil and make it aerobic so oak can colonise; the denser canopy shades out smaller plants
  • Each community changes abiotic conditions (depth, light, nutrients, oxygen), making it suitable for the next colonisers which out-compete them; conditions become less extreme and biotic factors increasingly important
/ 5
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Starting conditions (bare rock vs open water) differ but each stage makes conditions less extreme and builds soil / organic matter
  • The final community is controlled by the regional climate (temperature, water availability), which is the same for both - climatic climax (eg oak woodland in the UK)
/ 2
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Lichens and algae colonise bare rock (extreme temperatures, no soil, little water); rock fragments and dead organic matter accumulate; mosses, then grasses and ferns as thin soil forms; wind-dispersed trees (birch) then animal-dispersed (oak, beech)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 73-76; AQA spec 3.1.3.3.
