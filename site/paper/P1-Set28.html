Question 110 marks · Noise pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.13 · Genn pp. 286-295 · NOISE-06
[9 marks]01.1Using named examples, explain how the sources of noise pollution can be controlled by changes to design, operation and location.
[1 mark]01.2State what is meant by the Leq 57 dB contour used around UK airports.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Principles: dB is logarithmic (10 dB = 10x; 3 dB = 2x) so small reductions in dB are large reductions in intensity; effects on humans (hearing, stress, behaviour) and wildlife (breeding birds, bats, cetaceans)
  • Aircraft design: high bypass ratio engines, chevron nozzles, hush kits, acoustic liners, fairings, composites, blended wing
  • Aircraft operation: take-off angle, flight paths avoiding urban areas, constant descent, reduced reverse thrust, night restrictions, Chapter noise limits, fines and noise charges, quota count
  • Airport design / location: away from population centres, taxi and engine-test areas away from housing, acoustic walls, baffle mounds, land-use restrictions, free acoustic insulation
  • Railway: track polishing, sound-absorbing ballast and suspension, pantograph fairings, wheel lubrication, composite brakes
  • Road: porous / rubber asphalt, acoustic fences, embankments, tree planting, aerodynamics, engine insulation, traffic management and routing, double glazing
  • Industrial: silencers on compressors, pressing instead of stamping, nylon rollers, drilling instead of pile-driving, bubble curtains, timing of blasting, acoustic mats / curtains, remote operation, ear protection
  • Domestic: acoustic absorbers in appliances, volume limiters, selection of quieter appliances, ear defenders
  • Evaluation: which approaches are most effective / cost-effective; conflicts (airports near cities for convenience); legislation and monitoring (Leq 57 dB contours) needed to enforce
/ 9
01.2 (1 marks)
  • The area within which the average (equivalent continuous) sound level between 07:00 and 23:00 in summer exceeds 57 dB - taken as the onset of significant community annoyance
/ 1

Question 210 marks · Scientific methodologies & sampling design · spec 3.7.1 · Genn pp. 390-393 · RM-01

A student investigated whether the number of earthworms per m2 changes with distance from a hedge across an arable field.

[2 marks]02.1Explain why systematic sampling along a transect is more appropriate than random sampling for this investigation.
[4 marks]02.2Describe how a preliminary study could be used to decide the distance between sampling sites and the number of samples at each site.
[4 marks]02.3Describe how earthworms could be extracted from a 0.5 m x 0.5 m sample area and give two limitations of the method.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (2 marks)
  • There is an environmental gradient (distance from the hedge / changing conditions) so samples must be taken at regular intervals along it to detect the change
  • Random sampling might leave gaps / miss the pattern; systematic sampling avoids bias while covering the gradient
/ 2
02.2 (4 marks)
  • Sample at close intervals (eg every 1 m) first and see at what spacing the variations are still detected without unnecessary data; wider intervals (eg 4 m) may miss peaks and troughs
  • Take increasing numbers of samples at one site and calculate the running mean; the number needed is where the mean stops fluctuating / stabilises
  • Calculate standard deviation to assess scatter: more variable data need more samples
  • Test the method (extraction technique, timing) for reliability before the main study
/ 4
02.3 (4 marks)
  • Remove surface vegetation; pour an irritant solution (dilute mustard / detergent in water) onto the area until saturated (and around it), keep adding as it infiltrates
  • Collect worms that come to the surface for a standardised time; count / identify / weigh
  • Limitations: solution may not percolate impermeable soils or reach deep worms; worms may move sideways or downwards; some may die or not respond; slow-moving worms missed
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 390-393; AQA spec 3.7.1.
Question 310 marks · Principles of pollution control (CPA, CGM, new technologies) · spec 3.4.3.1 · Genn pp. 243-245 · PCON-02
[6 marks]03.1Explain how the control of emission location and emission timing can reduce the severity of pollution. Use examples.
[2 marks]03.2Explain the polluter pays principle and suggest why it encourages pollution prevention.
[2 marks]03.3Explain the precautionary principle as applied to the release of a new chemical.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (6 marks)
  • Location: discharge into the sea / large water bodies where currents dilute and disperse the pollutant
  • Location: chimneys / emissions sited downwind of urban areas so pollutants are not carried over populations; avoiding valleys and enclosed water bodies where pollutants concentrate
  • Location: not discharging wastes onto permeable rock above an aquifer / not siting landfill on aquifers
  • Timing: restrict polluting activities (industrial combustion, vehicle use) during temperature inversions when dispersal is poor
  • Timing: discharge liquid effluent when river flow is high / on an ebb tide so it is diluted and carried away
  • Timing: pesticide spraying at night / on still days; noisy operations restricted to daytime; night-flight restrictions
/ 6
03.2 (2 marks)
  • The organisation / person that causes the pollution is responsible for (paying for) the damage caused
  • If the cost of preventing the pollution is less than the cost of the damage / fines, there is a financial incentive to prevent it; works best when penalties are high and escape is unlikely
/ 2
03.3 (2 marks)
  • It is assumed that the waste will cause pollution / harm until research shows it is unlikely to
  • So release is not permitted until it has been tested; lack of knowledge of a problem is not an excuse / does not reduce responsibility
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 243-245; AQA spec 3.4.3.1.
Question 410 marks · Fluctuations in supply/demand & energy storage · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 222-224 · EST-05
[2 marks]04.1Explain why compressed air energy storage with heat recovery can achieve over 90% efficiency.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why large-volume hot water stores lose heat more slowly per unit of stored energy than small ones.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why molten salt rather than water is used to store heat from CSP plants.
[2 marks]04.4Explain how high thermal mass building materials reduce energy use.
[2 marks]04.5Describe one other energy storage technology.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • Surplus energy drives a pump compressing air stored in underground caverns (salt mines); releasing it drives machinery / turbines
  • Compression produces a lot of heat; storing this heat (hot oil / molten salt) and returning it when the air expands recovers energy that would otherwise be lost
/ 2
04.2 (2 marks)
  • As volume increases the surface area : volume ratio decreases
  • Heat is lost through the surface so there is less surface per unit of stored heat; very large stores can carry summer solar heat into winter (inter-seasonal storage)
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Potassium nitrate has a much higher boiling point so it stores heat at up to 550 °C without pressurisation
  • Water could only reach such temperatures under high pressure, increasing costs; high temperature needed to raise steam for turbines
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Materials with high specific heat capacity (concrete, water) warm and cool slowly
  • They absorb heat in hot weather reducing overheating / air conditioning, and release it as the weather cools, delaying the need for heating
/ 2
04.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Flywheels: rotating mass stores kinetic energy for short-term smoothing / generation
  • Supercapacitors: electrochemical capacitors with 10-100 times the energy density of conventional capacitors; possible future large-scale electricity storage
  • Thermal storage in molten salt / high-volume tanks; hydrogen / P2G; batteries
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 222-224; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.

Question 610 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.1 · Genn pp. 225-227 · ECON-02
[6 marks]06.1Explain how each of the following features of vehicle design reduces energy use.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why solid steel wheels on rails lose less energy than pneumatic tyres on roads.
[2 marks]06.3Describe two ways combustion efficiency in an internal combustion engine can be improved.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (6 marks)
  • Aerodynamic shapes / fairings reduce friction with the air (air resistance) so less energy is needed to propel the vehicle, especially at speed; hydrodynamics for ships
  • Reduced mass (plastics, carbon-fibre composites, high-strength steel alloys, aluminium engine blocks, lighter batteries and magnets, rounded shapes) means less energy to accelerate and less rolling resistance, so lower fuel consumption
  • Conventional friction brakes convert kinetic energy to waste heat; regenerative braking uses a generator to convert it to electricity stored in a battery (or returned to the rail grid) which then propels the vehicle, so less fuel is needed to accelerate again
/ 6
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Pneumatic tyres deform as they rotate; the continual change in shape and air movement inside generates frictional heat (more if under-inflated)
  • Steel wheels do not deform so rolling resistance is very low
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • More valves per cylinder to remove exhaust gases fully so they do not mix with fresh fuel
  • Better temperature control (thermostatic fan)
  • Electronic ignition control / regular servicing to keep spark timing correct
  • Turbochargers supplying more oxygen
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 225-227; AQA spec 3.3.4.2.1.
Question 710 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.2.2.3 · Genn pp. 96-97 · CC-04
[2 marks]07.1Describe the two processes that drive the North Atlantic Conveyor.
[4 marks]07.2Explain how global climate change could weaken the North Atlantic Conveyor and describe the possible consequence for the climate of the UK.
[3 marks]07.3Explain why the effects of climate change on UK temperatures are difficult to predict.
[1 mark]07.4Describe how deep ocean currents are monitored.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Friction with the prevailing south-westerly winds drags surface water north-eastwards from the tropical Atlantic
  • Water in the NE Atlantic cools, becomes denser (and more saline as ice forms) and sinks, drawing warm surface water in to replace it; the cold water returns southwards at depth (thermohaline circulation)
/ 2
07.2 (4 marks)
  • Higher temperatures melt land ice on Greenland which flows into the North Atlantic
  • The fresh water dilutes the seawater, reducing its salinity and density
  • Less dense water sinks less easily so the flow rate of the current is reduced
  • Less heat is transported to NW Europe so the UK could become colder (like Moscow / Churchill at the same latitude) even while global temperatures rise
/ 4
07.3 (3 marks)
  • Several interconnected systems act at once over different timescales: jet stream changes may raise temperatures, a slowing Conveyor may lower them, greenhouse gases raise them
  • Natural fluctuations (solar output, orbit) can hide or exaggerate anthropogenic change; individual events do not show trends
  • Time delays between cause and effect (oceans take a long time to warm because of volume and heat capacity); incomplete understanding of the processes
/ 3
07.4 (1 marks)
  • Argo floats sink to a set depth for about 10 days, then surface and transmit temperature, salinity and position data; the sequence of positions shows current direction and speed (surface currents also by satellites and buoys)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 96-97; AQA spec 3.2.1.2, 3.2.2.3.

Question 1010 marks · Pesticides · spec 3.4.3.2.8 · Genn pp. 265-266 · PEST-04
[9 marks]10.1Neonicotinoids are the most widely used group of insecticides. Explain how the properties of neonicotinoids affect their impact on bee populations and describe the methods used to reduce this impact.
[1 mark]10.2State one way in which pesticide use in agriculture may be reduced by using natural processes.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Neonicotinoids are neurotoxins similar to nicotine; they inhibit the neurotransmitter acetylcholine
  • High toxicity to insects but low toxicity to vertebrates because they cannot cross the vertebrate blood-brain barrier
  • Very toxic to bees: sub-lethal doses reduce navigation ability, foraging and immunity to disease; linked to colony decline
  • Water soluble and moderately persistent: remain in soil, absorbed by roots and translocated (systemic), so present in pollen and nectar
  • Persistence may allow build-up in aquifers / soil; broken down fastest in sunlight and by soil microbes
  • Synergism with some fungicides increases toxicity to bees
  • Exposure greatest when sprayed onto crops or when seed-coating dust is blown by wind
  • Control: seed coating rather than spraying; spraying when crops are not in flower / at night; still days to reduce drift
  • Restrictions on use (EU bans on outdoor use on flowering crops); use of alternative pest control (cultural, biological, integrated control)
  • Evaluation: reduced pesticide use but neonicotinoid alternatives (pyrethroids) also harm pollinators; trade-off with crop yields
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
Any 1 from:
  • Biological control (introduced predators / pathogens)
  • Predator habitats (hedgerows, beetle banks)
  • Crop rotation
  • Companion / barrier crops
  • Sterile male technique / pheromone traps
  • Genetic resistance
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 265-266; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.8.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Mineral resources are non-renewable. Discuss how an understanding of geological processes and the development of new technologies can extend the period for which society can rely on mineral resources.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Why non-renewable: formed too slowly; geological concentration processes - hydrothermal, metamorphic, Proterozoic sediments, alluvial, evaporites, secondary enrichment, biological sediments
  • Understanding processes guides exploration (batholith margins, river gravels, ancient seas) and remote sensing / geophysical techniques
  • Reserves, resource, stock; Lasky's principle; cut-off ore grade and its drivers
  • Extraction technologies: mechanisation, bioleaching, phytomining, polymer and bacterial adsorption, seabed nodules
  • Reducing demand: recycling and its difficulties, Cradle to Cradle, design for disassembly, linear vs circular material flows, dispersal of metals in use
  • Environmental limits: impacts of mining and their control; energy costs rising with lower grades; conflicts
  • Judgement: technology can extend supplies substantially but only within energy and environmental constraints; circular economy essential

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how knowledge of the properties of pollutants can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: state of matter, density, persistence, toxicity, specificity, reactivity, adsorption, solubility, bioaccumulation / biomagnification, synergism, mutagenic / carcinogenic / teratogenic action, mobility
  • How properties predict behaviour: dispersal (gases vs solids, density - Bhopal), how far and how long (persistence, mobility), where they concentrate (adsorption on sediments, food chains)
  • Environmental features interacting with properties: temperature inversions, currents, pH, oxygen, light, adsorbent materials
  • Control based on properties: persistent / liposoluble pesticides banned (organochlorines) and replaced by non-persistent / specific ones; systemic pesticides and seed coatings
  • Heavy metals: lead uses discontinued, storage at high pH to reduce solubility; mercury chemical form; radioactive waste categorised by half-life / activity and contained (encapsulation, sealed storage)
  • Reactive primary pollutants controlled to prevent secondary pollutants (catalytic converters, FGD, urea sprays); oil properties (floats, insoluble) exploited by booms and skimmers; density in cyclone separators
  • Principles: CPA and CGM based on properties; emission location / timing; ALARA and BATNEEC; polluter pays; precautionary principle
  • New technologies: satellite oil-spill monitoring, GPS, polymer adsorption, phytoremediation, bioremediation
  • Evaluation: prevention better than cure; limits of remediation; cost-effectiveness; contrast success of ozone (Montreal) with climate change; judgement
