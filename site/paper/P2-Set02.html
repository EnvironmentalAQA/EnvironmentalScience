Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Fishing's impacts go beyond depleting target species: k-selected species that breed late and produce few young collapse and recover slowly; by-catch of immature target fish, over-quota fish and non-commercial species is thrown back dead, with drift nets killing whales, dolphins and turtles, longlines drowning albatrosses and pair trawls porpoises; lost gear keeps ghost fishing; demersal trawls destroy sea fans, corals and seagrass; and removing any species alters its competitors, prey and predators.

Catch quotas limit the weight landed and work well for single-species pelagic shoals where fishing stops when the quota is reached, but in mixed demersal fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead, so the quota does not protect them. Gear design tackles by-catch directly: mesh sizes and square-mesh escape panels let small fish out because, unlike diagonal mesh, they do not close under drag; turtle exclusion devices, acoustic pingers for dolphins, curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting protect turtles, cetaceans and albatrosses; biodegradable and radio-tracked traps limit ghost fishing. Restricting effort - boat size, engine power, days at sea, decommissioning - reduces total pressure, and banning drift nets or demersal trawling on sensitive seabeds such as Rockall Bank protects habitats.

Effectiveness depends on enforcement at sea, which is difficult, on reliable stock data for quotas, on the species and fishery type, and on international cooperation because fish migrate; some measures cut catches or raise costs, and as local stocks fall fleets simply move further away. Gear modifications are the most effective per unit cost because they reduce damage without reducing legal catch; quotas and effort limits only work where they are enforced.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Fishing damages the environment by overfishing, catching by-catch such as dolphins and damaging the seabed with trawling. Quotas limit how many fish can be caught so stocks can recover. Bigger mesh sizes let small fish escape so they can grow and breed. Turtle exclusion devices let turtles escape from nets. Some fishing methods can be banned in sensitive areas. No-take zones let fish breed. These methods help but they are hard to enforce at sea and some fishermen ignore them.

What makes the difference

  • Level 3 evaluates each method on its own terms and then compares them; Level 2's evaluation is a single general sentence.
  • Named examples (Rockall Bank, albatross measures, ghost-fishing controls) give Level 3 the range the top band expects.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Natural systems have remained sustainable because they run on low energy-density renewable energy, mainly sunlight driving the hydrological, carbon and nitrogen cycles; their reactions occur at low temperatures because enzymes lower activation energies; materials cycle so the waste of one process is the raw material of the next; only a few abundant elements are used; and wastes are biodegradable and non-toxic. Negative feedback keeps them in dynamic equilibrium and high diversity makes them resistant to change.

Human systems contrast on every point: non-renewable fossil fuels, high-temperature processes such as the Haber process and incineration, linear use that disperses minerals and mixes materials that cannot be separated, and persistent toxins like PCBs and the metals in electronics. We also override negative feedback - when degraded soil reduces yields we add fertiliser, irrigation and pesticides - and trigger positive feedback such as permafrost melting.

Copying natural principles is already possible in many areas: renewable energy and heat pumps, bioleaching at low temperature, recycling and design for disassembly, industrial symbiosis, legumes and organic matter instead of manufactured nitrate, and retaining hedgerows for pest predators - the basis of the circular economy. The limits are population size and affluence, the attitude of urban consumers who never see depleted resources, cost, and the need for international cooperation. So the principles can make individual activities far more sustainable, but only a large-scale, cooperative shift in lifestyle would make the whole human system sustainable.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Natural systems use renewable energy from the Sun and recycle materials, so nothing runs out and there is no waste. Humans use fossil fuels which will run out and produce waste that is toxic. Natural processes happen at low temperatures but the Haber process needs high temperatures. Humans could copy natural systems by using renewable energy like solar and wind, recycling more and using less fertiliser. This would make human activities more sustainable. However it would be expensive and people would have to change their lifestyle.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 states the contrast correctly but does not explain the mechanisms (enzymes, negative feedback, diversity, dispersal of minerals) that Level 3 uses.
  • Level 3 applies the principles to specific technologies (bioleaching, heat pumps, industrial symbiosis, legumes); Level 2 stays at 'recycle more'.
  • 'Evaluate the extent' - Level 3 distinguishes individual activities from the whole system in its conclusion; Level 2's evaluation is a single sentence about cost.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Present agriculture relies on inputs that cannot last: chemical pesticides to which pests evolve resistance and which harm non-target species, rock phosphate that is non-renewable, nitrate fertiliser made with fossil-fuel energy, and irrigation that over-draws rivers and groundwater and salinises soil, while ploughing loses soil organic matter and livestock, rice and machinery release greenhouse gases. Each can be replaced by strategies based on ecological principles. Pests are controlled culturally by weeding, mulching, rotation, barrier crops and culling, by biological control and predator habitats, by polyculture and companion crops, and by integrated control that uses chemicals only as a last resort. Nutrients come from recycled organic matter, rotation with legumes and conserved soil biota, so artificial fertiliser, especially nitrate, can be reduced. Water is saved with low water-use crops, reservoirs and aquifer recharge and drip irrigation; soil carbon is kept by low tillage and less machinery, with renewable energy for what remains; methane falls with rice varieties that allow earlier drainage and with ground, carbohydrate-rich cattle feed. Habitats of crop wild relatives, seed banks, hedgerows, ditches, ponds and woodland and agri-environment schemes protect the biodiversity that supplies these services.

Food demand will rise with a population that may reach 12 billion, there is little new land, and imports can reduce food availability where it is grown, so yield cannot be ignored. Some strategies cost more or need farmers to accept new techniques, and past land degradation from the fertile crescent to the American mid-west shows what happens when they are not adopted. No single strategy suffices; a combination of ecological principles with new technology is the only route to feeding more people without exhausting the resources food depends on.

  • Level 3's evaluation considers population growth, land limits, cost, adoption and historical evidence; Level 1's is a single clause.
  • Note that Level 3 avoids defining organic farming as 'not using chemicals' - the textbook stresses it is the positive use of natural processes.

Model answers: what a Level 5 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 5 (23 marks)

Biodiversity is lost through direct exploitation, habitat destruction, introduced species, pollution and changes to abiotic factors, so its conservation needs methods that address each cause, at scales from a single species to international law. Their effectiveness varies with the threat, the species and the resources available.

Deciding what to conserve comes first. The IUCN Red List uses population size, area, decline rate and probability of extinction to prioritise species, and endemic, keystone, flagship and evolutionarily distinct species receive weight because their loss has wider consequences. This is effective at directing scarce resources, but it favours well-studied vertebrates over invertebrates and plants.

Legislation and agreements protect species and habitats. In the UK the Wildlife and Countryside Act, SSSIs, NNRs and Marine Conservation Zones, and in Europe SACs, SPAs and the Habitats Directive, restrict damaging activities; internationally CITES controls trade in threatened species, the IWC regulates whaling, the Ramsar Convention protects wetlands and the Rio Convention on Biological Diversity commits nations to conservation. Their effectiveness depends on enforcement: CITES has reduced trade in ivory and rhino horn but poaching continues where enforcement is weak; the IWC moratorium allowed some whale populations to recover but Japan and Norway continued whaling; designations protect against development but not against pollution or climate change crossing boundaries.

In-situ habitat conservation and management are the most fundamental methods because they keep species with the community and abiotic conditions they depend on. Management uses ecological understanding: maintaining plagioclimaxes such as chalk grassland and heathland by grazing or burning, coppicing and pollarding woodland, controlling water levels, light and grazing, designing reserves with the right area, shape, biological corridors and buffer zones, and creating new habitats such as wetlands in worked-out gravel pits. Case studies show it works - coppiced woodland restored dormice and butterflies, wetland creation increased bitterns - but reserves are often small and fragmented and need permanent funding.

Ex-situ methods rescue species for which in-situ protection has failed. Captive breeding faces problems of space, diet, breeding triggers, social groups and small gene pools, addressed with studbooks, artificial insemination, embryo transfer and cryopreservation; seed banks such as the Millennium Seed Bank store plant genetic diversity cheaply. The Arabian oryx, California condor and pool frog show captive breeding can bring species back, but only where protected habitat exists for hard or soft release with post-release monitoring, and it is expensive and works for few species.

Ecological monitoring - image databases, eDNA, tracking, satellites - measures whether any of this is working and has improved the targeting of effort. Overall, legislation is effective when enforced, habitat management is the most effective and widely applicable method, and captive breeding is a last resort that succeeds only in combination with habitat protection. The Living Planet Index continues to fall, however, which shows that conservation methods are winning individual battles while the drivers of loss - population, consumption, land-use change and climate change - remain unaddressed.

What makes the difference
  • The essay is structured by type of method and evaluates the effectiveness of each in turn, with named examples for both successes and failures.
  • It draws on several specification areas (biodiversity, conservation legislation, habitat management, captive breeding, ecological processes, monitoring) - the synoptic breadth Level 5 requires.
  • The conclusion ranks the methods and then steps back to the bigger picture (Living Planet Index, underlying drivers) - a supported judgement rather than a summary.
  • A Level 3 essay would describe the same methods accurately but with fewer examples, little evaluation of enforcement or cost, and a conclusion that all methods are important.
