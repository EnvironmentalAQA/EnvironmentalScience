Question 115 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-62 · HAB-14
[4 marks]01.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows a high biomass of life in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[2 marks]01.2Explain why growing coral reefs help to resist climate change.
[9 marks]01.3Discuss the importance of tropical coral reefs and evaluate the methods used to conserve them.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside the polyp cells
  • The algae absorb CO2 and excretory wastes from the polyp for photosynthesis and growth (efficient nutrient recycling)
  • They supply the polyp with oxygen and glucose - over 95% of its food energy
  • In return the algae gain a protected habitat and nutrients they could not absorb from the nutrient-deficient water
  • Polyps also feed on plankton captured by cilia and nematocysts
/ 4
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Corals deposit a skeleton of calcium carbonate (calcite)
  • Each carbonate molecule requires the absorption of a molecule of carbon dioxide, so reefs are a carbon sink
/ 2
01.3 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Importance: cover 1% of ocean area but support over a quarter of marine species; fisheries (fish, lobsters, conch); medicines (AZT, Ara-C, dolastatin, secosteroids, bryostatin, trabectedin, bone grafts); climate control as carbon sink; erosion protection of low-lying islands; tourism income
  • Threats: physical damage (anchors, divers, litter, coral extraction), collection of ornaments (CITES Appendix II), sedimentation from rivers and coastal development, pollution (oil, nutrients causing algal shading, sewage, pesticides, sunscreen), ocean acidification, bleaching from warming, overfishing (giant triton / crown of thorns starfish), introduced species (red lionfish), loss of mangroves and seagrass nurseries
  • Conservation: marine protected areas / national parks (Great Barrier Reef, Belize, Chagos), mooring buoys, diver education, No Take Zones which can increase catches elsewhere, sustainable ecotourism and fishing, protection of mangroves and seagrass, erosion and pollution control, planning control of ports and resorts
  • Fishing controls: closed seasons, minimum and maximum catchable sizes, bans on dynamite, trawling and small-mesh nets
  • Evaluation: local measures cannot control global threats (warming, acidification) which need international action; enforcement in remote areas is difficult; conflicts with local livelihoods; large vs small NTZs
  • Justified conclusion
/ 9
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 57-62; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

Question 315 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 341-345 · FISH-07

Table 1 shows the catch made by a shrimp trawler during one fishing trip.

Table 1

Shrimps (target species)0.6
Crabs, sea urchins, molluscs and starfish5.9
Immature fish of commercial species1.9
[2 marks]03.1Calculate the percentage of the total catch that was by-catch.
[1 mark]03.2Explain why shrimp trawling produces such a high proportion of by-catch.
[3 marks]03.3Explain why k-selected species such as the common skate and the Greenland shark are especially vulnerable to overfishing.
[9 marks]03.4Evaluate the methods that can be used to reduce the environmental impacts of fishing.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • By-catch = 5.9 + 1.9 = 7.8 t; total = 8.4 t
  • 7.8 / 8.4 x 100 = 92.9% (accept 93%)
/ 2
03.2 (1 marks)
  • The nets have a very small mesh size so few non-target animals can escape
/ 1
03.3 (3 marks)
  • They breed at an old age (skate about 11 years; Greenland shark 100-150 years) and produce few young
  • They may reach a catchable size before they are sexually mature, so fishing can remove the whole breeding population before it has bred
  • Populations recover slowly, unlike r-selected species (herring, mackerel) which breed young and produce many young; they are caught as by-catch in demersal trawls so fishing continues even though they have no commercial value
/ 3
03.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Impacts: population decline of k-selected species, by-catch (immature target fish, over-quota fish, non-commercial species; drift nets - whales, dolphins, turtles; longlines - albatrosses; pair trawls - porpoises), ghost fishing, seabed damage by demersal trawls, coral and seagrass damage, dynamite fishing, food-web effects
  • Catch quotas: limit weight landed; work well for single-species pelagic shoals but in mixed fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead so the quota fails to protect them
  • Gear design: mesh size and square-mesh escape panels let small fish escape (diagonal mesh closes under drag); turtle exclusion devices; acoustic pingers for dolphins; curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting to protect albatrosses; biodegradable / radio-tracked traps to reduce ghost fishing
  • Restricted effort: limits on boat size, engine power and days at sea; decommissioning; restricted methods - drift-net bans, demersal trawling bans on sensitive seabeds (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank)
  • Evaluation: enforcement at sea is difficult; some methods reduce catch or raise costs; quotas need reliable stock data; effectiveness depends on species (single vs mixed shoals) and on international cooperation as fish migrate; as local stocks decline fishing moves to more distant waters
  • Justified conclusion weighing effectiveness of different methods
/ 9
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 341-345; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.

05.1 (4 marks)

  • Natural processes occur at low temperatures because enzymes lower the activation energy of reactions; human processes need high temperatures and pressures

  • Anaerobic digestion and composting treat wastes; enzymes in industry (biological washing powders, biofuels from algae / microorganisms)

Question 65 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.1 · Genn pp. 5-7 · LIFE-04
[1 mark]06.1Which feature of the Earth prevents most atmospheric gases from escaping into space? Tick one box.
  • A  Its mass
  • B  Its magnetic field
  • C  Its distance from the Sun
  • D  Its speed of rotation
[2 marks]06.2Explain how the speed of rotation of the Earth and the tilt of its axis affect the conditions for life.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why the presence of liquid water on Earth depends on both the distance from the Sun and atmospheric pressure.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (1 marks)
  • Its mass
/ 1
06.2 (2 marks)
  • The 24-hour rotation period means the surface is not exposed to sunlight for long, so temperature extremes / excessive heating and cooling are reduced
  • The axis is tilted relative to the orbit so the amount of insolation at each latitude changes through the year, producing seasonal variations in conditions
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • The distance from the Sun gives surface temperatures between 0 and 35 °C in most areas: too close and water would boil / evaporate, too far and it would freeze
  • Atmospheric pressure (produced by the mass of gases held by gravity) is high enough to stop the water boiling / evaporating away; at much lower pressure there would be no liquid water
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 5-7; AQA spec 3.1.1.1.

Question 810 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 369, 386-387 · FOR-05

The Rio Bravo Conservation and Management Area in Belize exploits mahogany under a sustainable management plan while conserving a rainforest of over 200 tree, 400 bird and 70 mammal species.

[5 marks]08.1Describe five features of the timber harvesting at Rio Bravo that make it sustainable.
[3 marks]08.2Explain why involving local communities is essential to the success of the reserve.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why selective logging is better for wildlife than clear-felling, and give one disadvantage.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (5 marks)
  • Harvest rate below the maximum sustainable yield; only economically valuable species (mahogany) selectively logged, not clear-felled
  • Trees felled only where other mahoganies stand upwind so seeds recolonise the clearing; large seed trees and small trees left to grow
  • Buffer zones with no harvesting or machinery near rivers / wetlands
  • Restricted machinery and planned track routes re-used to avoid damaging roots of retained / rare / fruit trees
  • Each felled tree tagged and tracked to the retailer for FSC accreditation; rangers control illegal logging; tree nurseries replant over-exploited and ecologically important species
/ 5
08.2 (3 marks)
  • The reserve removed traditional uses (fuelwood, timber, farming clearings, hunting, medicines) so people would otherwise resent / ignore it
  • Employment as rangers, forestry and tourism workers; ecotourists use local restaurants, produce and souvenirs; offcuts given to local carvers
  • Benefits give the community an incentive to protect the forest; carbon sequestration payments from companies fund management
/ 3
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Removing individual trees leaves small clearings so remaining populations survive nearby and the forest structure / microclimate is kept; timber resource used over a longer period
  • Disadvantage: more labour-intensive and expensive; more tracks needed per tree
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 369, 386-387; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.
Question 910 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.3 · Genn pp. 73-76 · LP-03

A new pond was dug in a nature reserve. Table 1 shows the vegetation recorded at intervals over 60 years.

Table 1
Years after creationDominant vegetationWater depth at centre / m
1Single-celled algae, floating duckweed2.0
5Submerged pondweeds; reeds at margins1.8
15Reeds and reedmace over most of pond; water lilies1.0
30Marsh plants, sedges; willow and alder at edges0.2
60Willow / alder carr; oak seedlings0 (soil waterlogged)
[2 marks]09.1Name the type of sere shown in Table 1 and identify the pioneer species.
[5 marks]09.2Explain the changes shown in Table 1.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why a lithosere and a hydrosere in the same region end with the same climax community.
[1 mark]09.4Describe the early stages of a lithosere.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • Hydrosere
  • Single-celled algae (and floating plants) - arrive on birds / insects / from soil
/ 2
09.2 (5 marks)
  • Pioneers and later plants grow and die; dead organic matter plus sediment washed in fill the pond so water becomes shallower
  • As depth falls rooted plants (pondweeds then emergent reeds with leaves above water) can anchor and colonise; emergents shade submerged plants which die out
  • Sediment / soil accumulates and becomes able to support larger plants; water-tolerant trees (willow, alder) colonise the waterlogged soil
  • Transpiration by trees and further sediment dry the soil and make it aerobic so oak can colonise; the denser canopy shades out smaller plants
  • Each community changes abiotic conditions (depth, light, nutrients, oxygen), making it suitable for the next colonisers which out-compete them; conditions become less extreme and biotic factors increasingly important
/ 5
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Starting conditions (bare rock vs open water) differ but each stage makes conditions less extreme and builds soil / organic matter
  • The final community is controlled by the regional climate (temperature, water availability), which is the same for both - climatic climax (eg oak woodland in the UK)
/ 2
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Lichens and algae colonise bare rock (extreme temperatures, no soil, little water); rock fragments and dead organic matter accumulate; mosses, then grasses and ferns as thin soil forms; wind-dispersed trees (birch) then animal-dispersed (oak, beech)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 73-76; AQA spec 3.1.3.3.

  • Most remaining land is too dry, wet, cold, hot or steep, is covered in ice, or has no soil

  • So people with less money lose access to the land and food they used to produce

Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Unsustainable features: reliance on chemical pesticides (resistance, non-target impacts, bans), non-renewable rock phosphate, fossil-fuel-intensive nitrate manufacture, over-exploitation of rivers / groundwater, salinisation, loss of soil organic matter, greenhouse gases from fuel, rice and livestock, loss of species providing ecosystem services
