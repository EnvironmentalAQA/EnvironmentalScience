Question 215 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-62 · HAB-14
[4 marks]02.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows a high biomass of life in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why growing coral reefs help to resist climate change.
[9 marks]02.3Discuss the importance of tropical coral reefs and evaluate the methods used to conserve them.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside the polyp cells
  • The algae absorb CO2 and excretory wastes from the polyp for photosynthesis and growth (efficient nutrient recycling)
  • They supply the polyp with oxygen and glucose - over 95% of its food energy
  • In return the algae gain a protected habitat and nutrients they could not absorb from the nutrient-deficient water
  • Polyps also feed on plankton captured by cilia and nematocysts
/ 4
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Corals deposit a skeleton of calcium carbonate (calcite)
  • Each carbonate molecule requires the absorption of a molecule of carbon dioxide, so reefs are a carbon sink
/ 2
02.3 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Importance: cover 1% of ocean area but support over a quarter of marine species; fisheries (fish, lobsters, conch); medicines (AZT, Ara-C, dolastatin, secosteroids, bryostatin, trabectedin, bone grafts); climate control as carbon sink; erosion protection of low-lying islands; tourism income
  • Threats: physical damage (anchors, divers, litter, coral extraction), collection of ornaments (CITES Appendix II), sedimentation from rivers and coastal development, pollution (oil, nutrients causing algal shading, sewage, pesticides, sunscreen), ocean acidification, bleaching from warming, overfishing (giant triton / crown of thorns starfish), introduced species (red lionfish), loss of mangroves and seagrass nurseries
  • Conservation: marine protected areas / national parks (Great Barrier Reef, Belize, Chagos), mooring buoys, diver education, No Take Zones which can increase catches elsewhere, sustainable ecotourism and fishing, protection of mangroves and seagrass, erosion and pollution control, planning control of ports and resorts
  • Fishing controls: closed seasons, minimum and maximum catchable sizes, bans on dynamite, trawling and small-mesh nets
  • Evaluation: local measures cannot control global threats (warming, acidification) which need international action; enforcement in remote areas is difficult; conflicts with local livelihoods; large vs small NTZs
  • Justified conclusion

Question 410 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 36-37 · CBR-03
[3 marks]04.1Explain the difference between hard and soft release and state the types of species for which each is suitable.
[5 marks]04.2Describe five features of a suitable release site.
[2 marks]04.3Explain the importance of post-release monitoring.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Hard release: individuals released with no post-release support - suitable for species whose behaviour is instinctive and need no learned skills (insects, fish, amphibians, reptiles)
  • Soft release: gradual release into larger areas with food provided while they learn to find food, avoid predators - needed for mammals and birds
  • Problems faced: finding / recognising food and poisonous foods, hunting skills (captive-born predators never learned), avoiding predators, acceptance into wild social groups

Any 5 from:
  • Large enough suitable habitat for a viable population
  • Reliable food supply
  • Low predation risk / original threat removed
  • Suitable breeding sites
  • Water
  • Support of the local human population
  • Official / legal protection of habitat and species
/ 5
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Tracking (radio / GPS tags, rings) shows survival, dispersal and breeding of released animals so the success of the project can be assessed
  • Identifies causes of failure so future releases can be improved / extra support given

Question 510 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 18-19 · BIOV-07
[2 marks]05.1Explain why the ecosystem services provided by other species are often given a low conservation priority.
[3 marks]05.2Explain how soil organisms provide services that humans depend on.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why pollination by insects is more useful than wind pollination to plants growing in forests.
[3 marks]05.4Explain how seed dispersal by animals increases the survival of a plant's offspring.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • The processes (eg regulation of atmospheric gases by photosynthesis and respiration) act in a dynamic equilibrium and cancel each other out
  • Because there is no overall change their actions go unnoticed / their importance is not appreciated until they are lost
/ 2
05.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Invertebrates, fungi and bacteria break down / decompose dead organic matter, releasing nutrients for plant growth
  • The organic matter and humus produced hold the soil together (reducing erosion) and regulate the water cycle, giving more even river flow and less flooding
  • Organic acids from decomposition aid the weathering of rock, producing more soil and releasing more nutrients
  • Microbes carry out the processes of the carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles so wastes do not build up and nutrients are not depleted
/ 3
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Trees reduce wind velocity so wind pollination would be ineffective / unreliable over long distances
  • Insects search over long distances for flowers so dispersed populations can be pollinated, and less pollen needs to be produced (energy saving); specialised flowers reduce pollen being wasted on other species
/ 2
05.4 (3 marks)
  • Dispersal is not haphazard: animals live in the same habitat as the plant so are likely to carry seeds to locations where they can survive
  • Seeds can be carried longer distances than by wind and can be larger, containing more nutrients for the seedling
  • Seeds passing through the gut are deposited in faecal material which acts as a fertiliser, and passage through the intestine may stimulate germination
/ 3

Question 610 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 394-395, 401-402 · RM-05

A student used 0.5 m x 0.5 m grid quadrats to survey ground flora in two meadows, one grazed and one ungrazed. Table 1 shows the results for the grazed meadow (10 quadrats).

Table 1
SpeciesNumber of individuals
Ribwort plantain48
Perennial ryegrass120
White clover36
Daisy24
Creeping buttercup12
[2 marks]06.1Explain why a 0.5 m x 0.5 m quadrat was chosen rather than a 10 cm x 10 cm or 100 m x 100 m quadrat.
[3 marks]06.2Calculate Simpson's Index of Biodiversity (D) for the grazed meadow using D = N(N-1) / Σn(n-1). Show your working.
[2 marks]06.3The ungrazed meadow had D = 1.8 with the same number of species. Explain what this shows.
[2 marks]06.4Explain why a point quadrat would be better than a frame quadrat in tall vegetation.
[1 mark]06.5Give one limitation of using the DAFOR abundance scale.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (2 marks)
  • Quadrat size depends on the size / spacing of the organisms: 0.5 m x 0.5 m is typical for ground flora
  • 10 cm x 10 cm is for lichens (too small to contain a representative sample of plants); 100 m x 100 m is for trees (impractical, too much work)
/ 2
06.2 (3 marks)
  • N = 240; N(N-1) = 240 x 239 = 57 360
  • Σn(n-1) = 48x47 + 120x119 + 36x35 + 24x23 + 12x11 = 2256 + 14280 + 1260 + 552 + 132 = 18 480
  • D = 57 360 / 18 480 = 3.10 (accept 3.1)
/ 3
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Lower D means lower biodiversity: the ungrazed meadow is dominated by one or a few species (eg tall grass) with the others rare (less even)
  • Grazing (plagioclimax management) prevents dominance by vigorous species so abundance is more evenly spread
/ 2
06.4 (2 marks)
  • A frame quadrat flattens tall plants, making percentage cover estimates inaccurate and hiding shorter plants
  • Point quadrat pins are lowered through the vegetation; each touch counts (10 pins = 10% each) without flattening
/ 2
06.5 (1 marks)
  • Subjective judgement varies between recorders; results are not quantitative so statistical analysis is not possible (but quick for many areas)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 394-395, 401-402; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.

[4 marks]07.2Explain how hydroponic cultivation maximises productivity and give two disadvantages.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Organic advantages: often waste products, locally available and cheap; add humus improving structure and water retention; increase soil biota; slow release reduces leaching
  • Organic disadvantages: nutrient composition cannot be controlled; released slowly so needs long-term planning; bulky with high water content so expensive to transport; cannot be applied to a growing crop; odour / pathogens
  • Inorganic advantages: composition controlled to match crop needs; nutrients released rapidly; concentrated and easy to apply
  • Inorganic disadvantages: high energy (fossil fuel) manufacture (Haber process); no organic matter so humus and biota decline; some toxic to worms; high solubility so leached causing eutrophication; raw materials (phosphate rock) finite
/ 6
07.2 (4 marks)
  • Crops grown in a circulating nutrient solution (inert medium) in greenhouses; nutrient concentrations monitored and adjusted so nutrients are never limiting; abiotic factors controlled
  • Roots are small (all in contact with nutrients) so more growth goes into the harvested crop; no soil-borne pathogens or weeds; crop stays fresh with roots
  • Disadvantages: high inputs of nutrients and energy (heating, lighting, pumping); high level of technical knowledge; only economic for high-value crops

Table 1 shows the catch made by a shrimp trawler during one fishing trip.

Table 1

Crabs, sea urchins, molluscs and starfish5.9
Immature fish of commercial species1.9
[2 marks]10.1Calculate the percentage of the total catch that was by-catch.
[1 mark]10.2Explain why shrimp trawling produces such a high proportion of by-catch.
[3 marks]10.3Explain why k-selected species such as the common skate and the Greenland shark are especially vulnerable to overfishing.
[9 marks]10.4Evaluate the methods that can be used to reduce the environmental impacts of fishing.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (2 marks)
  • By-catch = 5.9 + 1.9 = 7.8 t; total = 8.4 t
  • 7.8 / 8.4 x 100 = 92.9% (accept 93%)
/ 2
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The nets have a very small mesh size so few non-target animals can escape
/ 1
10.3 (3 marks)
  • They breed at an old age (skate about 11 years; Greenland shark 100-150 years) and produce few young
  • They may reach a catchable size before they are sexually mature, so fishing can remove the whole breeding population before it has bred
  • Populations recover slowly, unlike r-selected species (herring, mackerel) which breed young and produce many young; they are caught as by-catch in demersal trawls so fishing continues even though they have no commercial value
/ 3
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Impacts: population decline of k-selected species, by-catch (immature target fish, over-quota fish, non-commercial species; drift nets - whales, dolphins, turtles; longlines - albatrosses; pair trawls - porpoises), ghost fishing, seabed damage by demersal trawls, coral and seagrass damage, dynamite fishing, food-web effects
  • Catch quotas: limit weight landed; work well for single-species pelagic shoals but in mixed fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead so the quota fails to protect them
  • Gear design: mesh size and square-mesh escape panels let small fish escape (diagonal mesh closes under drag); turtle exclusion devices; acoustic pingers for dolphins; curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting to protect albatrosses; biodegradable / radio-tracked traps to reduce ghost fishing
  • Restricted effort: limits on boat size, engine power and days at sea; decommissioning; restricted methods - drift-net bans, demersal trawling bans on sensitive seabeds (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank)
  • Evaluation: enforcement at sea is difficult; some methods reduce catch or raise costs; quotas need reliable stock data; effectiveness depends on species (single vs mixed shoals) and on international cooperation as fish migrate; as local stocks decline fishing moves to more distant waters
  • Justified conclusion weighing effectiveness of different methods
