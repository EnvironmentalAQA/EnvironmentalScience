01.18 marks2 marks per point

2 marks for each of the 4 points below; award 1 mark where a point is only partly made.

  1. Normal diagonal mesh closes as the net fills / stretches so small fish cannot escape

    • square-mesh panels do not close so juveniles escape to grow and breed
    • TEDs let turtles out
    • larger mesh sizes generally

    • surplus young spill over and increase surrounding catches
    • damaged communities recover

  4. Closed seasons, ideally in the breeding season, let fish grow and breed

    • fishermen may oppose because shoaling breeders are easy to catch

    In-situ: species remain in natural conditions with the whole community and inter-species relationships

    • protected areas (SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Ramsar, MCZ), land ownership, agri-environment schemes, habitat creation and design (area, shape, corridors, diversity), management of abiotic and biotic factors, plagioclimax maintenance, population control, reintroductions and rewilding (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish beavers)
  2. •

    Limits of in-situ: external threats (pollution, climate change), enforcement, cost, conflicts with land use, small fragmented populations

  3. •

    Ex-situ: captive breeding when in-situ is impossible or insufficient

    • selection criteria
    • difficulties keeping and breeding (habitat size, food, relationships, breeding triggers, group dynamics, gene pool / inbreeding, hybridisation)
    • techniques (stud books, cryopreservation, AI, embryo transfer, micropropagation, cloning)
    • seed banks
    • successes (Hawaiian goose, Iberian lynx, red kite, large blue)
  4. •

    Limits of ex-situ: expensive, few species, loss of wild behaviour, small gene pools, release requires suitable habitat and post-release support (hard vs soft release, monitoring)

  5. •

    Judgement: ex-situ is a temporary safeguard / support that only succeeds if in-situ habitat exists to return to

    • both needed, in-situ fundamental

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

In-situ conservation keeps a species in its natural habitat with the whole community it depends on - food species, pollinators, predators and the abiotic conditions it is adapted to. It is delivered through protected areas such as SSSIs, NNRs, SACs, SPAs, Ramsar sites and Marine Conservation Zones, land ownership by conservation bodies, agri-environment schemes and habitat creation and management. Its weaknesses are that external threats such as pollution and climate change cross reserve boundaries, enforcement and cost, conflicts with other land uses, and the fact that many populations are already small and fragmented.

Ex-situ captive breeding is used when in-situ protection is impossible or insufficient. It is hard: animals need enough space, the right food, social groupings and breeding triggers, and small founder populations risk inbreeding and hybridisation, which studbooks, artificial insemination and embryo transfer help to manage. It is expensive, works for few species, and captive animals may lose wild behaviour; release succeeds only where suitable habitat exists and with soft release and post-release monitoring.

The two are not alternatives. Ex-situ methods are a temporary safeguard that can rescue a species from extinction, as with the Arabian oryx, but they only succeed if there is protected habitat to return to. In-situ conservation is therefore fundamental and ex-situ a support for it; the relative importance depends on how close to extinction the species is.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

In-situ conservation means protecting species in their natural habitat, for example in nature reserves and national parks. This is good because the species stays in the wild with the other species it needs. Ex-situ conservation means captive breeding in zoos and then releasing the animals back into the wild. This can save species that are nearly extinct but it is expensive and animals may not survive when released. Both methods are important and are often used together.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 defines both approaches correctly but Level 3 gives specific mechanisms (types of protected area, breeding difficulties, release methods) and named examples.
  • Level 3 weighs the limitations of each approach explicitly; Level 2 gives one limitation for ex-situ only.
  • 'Both are important' is not a judgement about relative importance; Level 3 explains why in-situ is fundamental and when ex-situ matters most.
