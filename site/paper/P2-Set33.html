Question 110 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 30-31 · PRI-03
[4 marks]01.1Describe four ways in which the Wildlife and Countryside Act (1981) protects wildlife in the UK.
[4 marks]01.2Explain the difference between CITES Appendix I and Appendix II, giving an example species for each.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why controlling international trade can be more effective than trying to stop collection of a species in its own country.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Designation of protected areas (SSSIs, MCZs)
  • Protection of wild birds and their nests (except some pests / game birds)
  • Protection of named mammals such as otters, dormice, red squirrels, badgers and their setts
  • Uprooting wild plants generally illegal
  • Bats must not be disturbed; roof timber treatments must be non-toxic to bats
/ 4
01.2 (4 marks)
  • Appendix I: species threatened with extinction - all international (commercial) trade banned, except movement for conservation breeding
  • Examples: great apes, big cats, rhinos, blue whale
  • Appendix II: species that may become threatened if trade is not closely controlled - trade permitted under licence from countries where the species is well protected
  • Examples: Honduras mahogany, hippopotamus, green iguana, great white / basking shark, Venus fly trap
/ 4
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Collection may be hard to police in remote areas / poor countries, but if the animals or products cannot be exported / imported there is no market
  • Removing the profit removes the incentive to collect
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 30-31; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.2.
Question 210 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-05
[8 marks]02.1Explain how each of the following reduces the environmental impact of fishing.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why catch quotas work well for pelagic species such as mackerel but poorly in mixed demersal fisheries.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (8 marks)
  • Normal diagonal mesh closes as the net fills / stretches so small fish cannot escape; square-mesh panels do not close so juveniles escape to grow and breed; TEDs let turtles out; larger mesh sizes generally
  • Minimum size lets fish reach breeding age before capture (eg UK lobster minimum carapace above breeding size, cod 35 cm); maximum size / V-notching egg-carrying females protects large, highly fecund breeding adults (Hebridean lobsters over 145 mm, Nassau grouper)
  • NTZs (Lundy, Lamlash Bay, Chagos, Great Barrier Reef) protect breeding populations and habitats; surplus young spill over and increase surrounding catches; damaged communities recover
  • Closed seasons, ideally in the breeding season, let fish grow and breed; fishermen may oppose because shoaling breeders are easy to catch
/ 8
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Pelagic species form single-species shoals so fishing stops when the quota is reached
  • In mixed fisheries many species are caught together; once one species' quota is reached it must be discarded (dead) while fishing for others continues
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 344-348; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 310 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 47-49 · CBR-08
[4 marks]03.1Describe coppicing and explain how it increases the biodiversity of a woodland.
[4 marks]03.2Explain how the reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park increased biodiversity.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why culling or removing species may be necessary in habitat management.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Trees (hazel, oak) are cut to ground level every 3-30 years and allowed to regrow from the stump
  • About 0.5 ha cut each year on a rotation (eg 8 years) so the wood is a patchwork of areas of different ages
  • Each age has its own community: newly cut areas get high light for wildflowers and butterflies; older areas give dense cover for nesting birds / dormice
  • Pollarding cuts above browsing height so deer / livestock cannot eat the regrowth
/ 4
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Wolves (top predator, exterminated by 1926) reduced deer populations and changed their behaviour
  • Fewer young trees eaten so trees and riverside vegetation recovered
  • More trees allowed beaver populations to increase; beaver dams increased wetland area
  • Wetland species (birds, fish, invertebrates) became more common; overall biodiversity rose, though wolves cause problems for livestock farmers
/ 4
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Undesirable species (introduced predators / competitors, or natural succession species such as scrub, conifers in the New Forest heathland) reduce survival of the target species
  • Removal restores suitable conditions (eg heathland for sand lizards and smooth snakes) or reduces predation / competition
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 47-49; AQA spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4.
Question 410 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 357-358 · AQUA-04
[4 marks]04.1Describe a polyculture system using several species of carp and explain how it increases total productivity.
[4 marks]04.2Explain how integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) and aquaponics reduce pollution while increasing food production.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why tuna aquaculture cannot yet replace tuna fishing.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Silver carp eat phytoplankton, bighead carp zooplankton, grass carp and tilapia vegetation, common carp bottom-feed on molluscs / insects, catfish or perch eat small fish
  • Species occupy different niches / trophic levels so they do not compete and all food sources are used
  • Bottom feeders re-suspend nutrients boosting plankton / plant growth
  • Predators control over-breeding species (tilapia) so remaining fish grow larger; total yield exceeds any single species
/ 4
04.2 (4 marks)
  • IMTA: fed species (salmon, shrimp) alongside inorganic extractive species (seaweeds absorbing ammonia and phosphate) and organic extractive species (filter-feeding mussels eating waste particles / plankton)
  • Wastes of one species become nutrients for another so less pollution and extra harvests
  • Aquaponics: aquaculture water rich in nutrients / organic matter feeds hydroponic salad crops (lettuce, herbs, watercress)
  • Plants remove the nutrients that would cause deoxygenation and cleaned water returns to the fish (tilapia, carp, catfish); rice-fish paddies similar
/ 4
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Bluefin tuna do not breed well in captivity so young fish are caught from the wild and fattened in sea cages
  • Production still depends on (and removes juveniles from) the wild population; carnivores need large fish inputs
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 357-358; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.
Question 510 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 308-310, 313 · AGR-02
[3 marks]05.1Explain why a moderate water shortage reduces crop productivity even though the plants survive.
[4 marks]05.2Explain the problems caused by waterlogged soils and describe two ways farmers reduce soil water levels.
[2 marks]05.3Explain how irrigating with groundwater can cause soil salinisation and how this harms crops.
[1 mark]05.4State how a farmer could raise the pH of an acidic soil and explain why a low pH reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Plants close their stomata to reduce transpiration losses
  • Closed stomata stop the absorption of carbon dioxide so photosynthesis and growth stop
  • Less water for nutrient absorption / transport and cell turgidity; severe shortage dehydrates cells and inhibits biochemical reactions
/ 3
05.2 (4 marks)
  • Anaerobic soil favours denitrifying bacteria and inhibits nitrifying bacteria so nitrate is lost faster than it forms; higher risk of fungal disease; root respiration inhibited
  • Drainage ditches or pipes
  • Deep ploughing; avoiding compaction by machinery / livestock; adding organic matter to encourage worms
/ 4
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Groundwater often contains dissolved salts; when the water evaporates from the soil the salts are left behind and accumulate
  • High soil salinity causes osmotic dehydration of roots / crop death; washing out with extra water uses more water and salinises rivers
/ 2
05.4 (1 marks)
  • Add crushed lime (calcium carbonate / hydroxide); low pH increases leaching, inhibits nutrient uptake and mobilises toxic aluminium and lead
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 308-310, 313; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.

[6 marks]06.1Use Table 1 to explain why the plantation has a much higher timber yield but far lower biodiversity.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why commercial plantations are felled before the trees reach full size.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why there is little demand today for coppiced or pollarded wood.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (6 marks)
  • Non-indigenous species (Sitka spruce from North America) chosen for fast growth and pest resistance; indigenous wildlife's needs for food, pollination, seed dispersal and habitat are not met
  • Single species: easy planting, thinning and felling over large areas; but each tree species supports its own community so few species overall
  • Close planting makes trees compete for light so they grow tall and straight (good timber) but shades out ground flora and the species that depend on them
  • Simple age structure: uniform size makes management easy but gives little variety of light, temperature, wind; no old trees or dead wood for invertebrates and woodpeckers
  • Mixed woodland: varied niches, stratification, dead wood, indigenous trees each with their own dependent species
/ 6
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Trees are felled when income will be greatest - approaching full size when the growth rate starts to decline
  • Continuing to grow them ties up land and capital for little extra timber; natural forests keep old and dead trees
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Industrial society replaced species-specific uses (baskets, fencing, charcoal, tool handles) with large-scale uses: chipboard, plywood, pulp
  • Wood fuel replaced by fossil fuels / electricity; so traditional management declined and woods became uniform
/ 2

Question 710 marks · Manipulation of food species & agricultural energetics · spec 3.5.1.2 · Genn pp. 326-328 · FS-04

Table 1 shows food conversion ratios (FCR) for livestock: the mass of feed needed to produce 1 kg of live mass gain.

Table 1
LivestockFCR / kg feed per kg gain
Farmed salmon1.2
Chicken1.8
Pork3.0
Beef (feedlot)7.0
Sheep8.0
[3 marks]07.1Use Table 1 to explain why intensive poultry and fish production convert feed to food more efficiently than beef.
[3 marks]07.2Explain three ways farmers reduce food chain energy losses in livestock production.
[4 marks]07.3Explain why growing crops for direct human consumption usually produces more food per hectare than rearing livestock, and describe one situation where livestock is the better option.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • Chicken and salmon need far less feed per kg gained (1.2-1.8 vs 7.0)
  • Fish are ectotherms so no energy is used for thermoregulation and water supports their mass; poultry grow fast and are kept warm indoors with movement restricted
  • Cattle are large endotherms, use energy in movement, and lose energy in methane from cellulose digestion
/ 3
07.2 (3 marks)
  • Control of movement (housing / restricted grazing) so less energy is used in muscle activity
  • Temperature control (heated / insulated buildings) so less energy is used in maintaining body temperature
  • Species selection: choose species with low FCRs (poultry, fish); high-energy easily digested diets; slaughter at the fastest growth stage
/ 3
07.3 (4 marks)
  • Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next; most is lost as heat in respiration / metabolism or in undigested material
  • Humans eating plants (primary consumers) receive more of the energy captured by photosynthesis than eating animals fed on those plants (secondary consumers)
  • Livestock better where land cannot grow crops (upland, semi-arid, steep, thin soils): grazing animals with symbiotic gut bacteria digest cellulose in grass that humans cannot
  • Omnivores (pigs) convert food wastes (dairy by-products, catering waste) into meat
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 326-328; AQA spec 3.5.1.2.

A student measured the wind speed at 10 sites inside a woodland and at 10 sites in a nearby clearing.

Table 1
LocationMean wind speed / m s-1Standard deviation / m s-1
Woodland1.20.4
Clearing3.51.1
[3 marks]08.1Name the statistical test the student should use to find out whether the difference between the means is significant. Give two reasons for your choice.
[1 mark]08.2Explain what the student should do if it is not possible to show that the data are normally distributed.
[2 marks]08.3The test gave p = 0.02. Explain what this means.
[2 marks]08.4Explain how the standard deviations in Table 1 support the conclusion that the means are different.
[2 marks]08.5Explain why a statistically significant result does not prove that trees cause the lower wind speed.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • t-test
  • The data are measured (continuous) values and two means are being compared
  • The data are (assumed to be) normally distributed
/ 3
08.2 (1 marks)
  • Use the Mann-Whitney U test instead (compares medians)
/ 1
08.3 (2 marks)
  • There is a 2% probability that the difference occurred by random chance
  • As this is less than 0.05 the difference is statistically significant / the null hypothesis is rejected
/ 2
08.4 (2 marks)
  • The ranges of values (mean plus or minus SD) do not overlap: woodland up to 1.6 and clearing down to 2.4
  • About 68% of values lie within one SD of each mean so the two data sets are clearly separated
/ 2
08.5 (2 marks)
  • Significance shows the difference is unlikely to be due to chance, not that a causal relationship exists
  • Another variable (eg topography / aspect of the sites) could cause both; a controlled experiment or further evidence is needed

Question 1010 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 56-57 · HAB-05
[9 marks]10.1Describe the conservation efforts used to protect tropical rainforests and evaluate their effectiveness.
[1 mark]10.2State one reason why rainforest conservation is important to countries far from the tropics.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Protected areas: Korup NP Cameroon (1260 km2, 600 tree species, endemic Ancistrocladus korupensis with anti-HIV / antimalarial alkaloids); Tumucumaque NP Brazil (39 000 km2, adjoining French Guiana reserve); Alto Maues; Rio Bravo Belize funded by charities, companies and ecotourism
