Question 110 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 369, 386-387 · FOR-05

The Rio Bravo Conservation and Management Area in Belize exploits mahogany under a sustainable management plan while conserving a rainforest of over 200 tree, 400 bird and 70 mammal species.

[5 marks]01.1Describe five features of the timber harvesting at Rio Bravo that make it sustainable.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why involving local communities is essential to the success of the reserve.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why selective logging is better for wildlife than clear-felling, and give one disadvantage.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (5 marks)
  • Harvest rate below the maximum sustainable yield; only economically valuable species (mahogany) selectively logged, not clear-felled
  • Trees felled only where other mahoganies stand upwind so seeds recolonise the clearing; large seed trees and small trees left to grow
  • Buffer zones with no harvesting or machinery near rivers / wetlands
  • Restricted machinery and planned track routes re-used to avoid damaging roots of retained / rare / fruit trees
  • Each felled tree tagged and tracked to the retailer for FSC accreditation; rangers control illegal logging; tree nurseries replant over-exploited and ecologically important species
/ 5
01.2 (3 marks)
  • The reserve removed traditional uses (fuelwood, timber, farming clearings, hunting, medicines) so people would otherwise resent / ignore it
  • Employment as rangers, forestry and tourism workers; ecotourists use local restaurants, produce and souvenirs; offcuts given to local carvers
  • Benefits give the community an incentive to protect the forest; carbon sequestration payments from companies fund management
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Removing individual trees leaves small clearings so remaining populations survive nearby and the forest structure / microclimate is kept; timber resource used over a longer period
  • Disadvantage: more labour-intensive and expensive; more tracks needed per tree
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 369, 386-387; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.
Question 210 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 357-358 · AQUA-04
[4 marks]02.1Describe a polyculture system using several species of carp and explain how it increases total productivity.
[4 marks]02.2Explain how integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) and aquaponics reduce pollution while increasing food production.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why tuna aquaculture cannot yet replace tuna fishing.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
  • Silver carp eat phytoplankton, bighead carp zooplankton, grass carp and tilapia vegetation, common carp bottom-feed on molluscs / insects, catfish or perch eat small fish
  • Species occupy different niches / trophic levels so they do not compete and all food sources are used
  • Bottom feeders re-suspend nutrients boosting plankton / plant growth
  • Predators control over-breeding species (tilapia) so remaining fish grow larger; total yield exceeds any single species
/ 4
02.2 (4 marks)
  • IMTA: fed species (salmon, shrimp) alongside inorganic extractive species (seaweeds absorbing ammonia and phosphate) and organic extractive species (filter-feeding mussels eating waste particles / plankton)
  • Wastes of one species become nutrients for another so less pollution and extra harvests
  • Aquaponics: aquaculture water rich in nutrients / organic matter feeds hydroponic salad crops (lettuce, herbs, watercress)
  • Plants remove the nutrients that would cause deoxygenation and cleaned water returns to the fish (tilapia, carp, catfish); rice-fish paddies similar
/ 4
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Bluefin tuna do not breed well in captivity so young fish are caught from the wild and fattened in sea cages
  • Production still depends on (and removes juveniles from) the wild population; carnivores need large fish inputs
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 357-358; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.
Question 310 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 78-81 · LP-05

Figure 1 shows the population of red deer on an island after they were introduced in year 0. No predators are present.

Figure 1
[4 marks]03.1Name the shape of the growth curve and explain the changes in growth rate shown.
[2 marks]03.2Explain the difference between density-dependent and density-independent factors, with an example of each.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why the deer population on the island may need to be culled even though it appears stable.
[2 marks]03.4Explain how predator and prey populations regulate each other.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Sigmoidal (S-shaped) growth curve
  • Slow start (few breeding individuals), then rapid / exponential growth (years 4-10) as abundant food and space mean birth rate far exceeds death rate
  • Growth slows as density-dependent factors (intra-species competition for food, disease) raise the death rate
  • Population stabilises / fluctuates around about 920-950 - the carrying capacity - where birth rate equals death rate
/ 4
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Density-dependent: effect on survival increases with population density - food shortage / intra-species competition, disease spread, predation
  • Density-independent: chance of survival unaffected by density - drought, flood, volcanic eruption, severe winter
/ 2
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Without wolves (natural predators removed) the population is regulated only by starvation / disease at a high density
  • At this density deer over-graze, preventing tree regeneration and damaging habitats / other species; culling keeps numbers below the damaging level (as in Scotland)
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Rising prey numbers give predators more food so predator numbers rise; high predation then reduces the prey population
  • Food shortage reduces predators, allowing prey to recover - cyclic fluctuations with predators lagging behind prey
/ 2

Question 410 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 52-55 · HAB-03
[4 marks]04.1Explain why tropical rainforests have such high biodiversity.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why rainforest species may be particularly vulnerable to environmental change.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why rainforest plants and animals such as frogs are a rich source of medicines.
[2 marks]04.4Explain the importance of tropical rainforest for the hydrological cycle and for soils.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Stable tropical climate over long periods gave time for species to evolve and adapt to local abiotic and biotic factors; many rare species with small ranges
  • High light, warm temperatures and high rainfall all year allow continuous photosynthesis and growth, supporting rich food webs with no seasonal food shortage (no hibernation / migration)
  • Stratification (emergent, canopy, understorey, shrub, ground layers) and epiphytes create many niches; intense competition for light drives specialisation
  • Biotic pressures (predation, inter-species relationships) drive evolution of specialised pollination / seed dispersal relationships; plants flower and fruit at different times
/ 4
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Adapted to stable conditions so they have narrow ranges of tolerance; a small change can move conditions outside the range
  • Many species are rare with small ranges / small populations
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
  • No cold winter kills herbivores so plants defend themselves with toxic chemicals (alkaloids) that are medicinally active in controlled doses (quinine, diosgenin, curare)
  • Animals without teeth / claws (frogs, reptiles) rely on chemical defences or venoms (fer-de-lance venom - captopril)
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Over half the Amazon's rainfall comes from transpiration within the forest; clearance reduces downwind rainfall
  • Rapid decay recycles nutrients so soils are shallow / poor; canopy and roots prevent erosion of the thin soil - clearance leads to rapid erosion and infertility
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 52-55; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 510 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 22-23 · HUM-02
[6 marks]05.1Explain how each of the following changes in abiotic factors caused by human activities can make a habitat unsuitable for species living there.
[2 marks]05.2Explain how increased water turbidity caused by ploughing, mining or dredging harms aquatic organisms.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why species that were already rare are most likely to be lost when abiotic conditions change.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (6 marks)
  • Drainage of wetlands / lowering water tables by groundwater abstraction dries out wetlands; HEP schemes alter river levels flooding sandbank nests of turtles / birds; species with narrow tolerance (specialists) affected first; sundews lost when drained soil becomes aerobic / nitrogen-rich and taller competitors invade
  • Hot water discharges and organic wastes (sewage) reduce dissolved oxygen, killing aerobic organisms such as fish and insect larvae
  • Acid mine drainage and acid rain gases lower pH, denaturing proteins of exposed tissues (fish eggs, gills) and dissolving calcium exoskeletons (crayfish)
/ 6
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Reduces light penetration so submerged plants cannot photosynthesise
  • Blocks the gills of filter feeders such as bivalve molluscs; sediment smothers the bed
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Rare species often have very specialised habitat requirements / narrow ranges of tolerance
  • A small change moves conditions outside their range while generalists survive; small populations cannot recover
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 22-23; AQA spec 3.1.2.2.

[3 marks]07.1Use Table 1 to explain why liquid water cannot exist on the surface of Mars.
[3 marks]07.2Explain how the Earth's mass and its distance from the Sun created suitable conditions for life.
[2 marks]07.3Explain the importance of the Earth's magnetic field and its speed of rotation for living organisms.
[2 marks]07.4Explain how the tilt of the Earth's axis affects living organisms.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • Mars has low mass so weak gravity and could not retain a dense atmosphere
  • Very low atmospheric pressure means water would boil / evaporate away even at low temperatures
  • Greater distance from the Sun gives low insolation and a mean temperature of -60 °C so water is frozen
/ 3
07.2 (3 marks)
  • Mass great enough for gravity to retain an atmosphere containing the elements needed (C, H, O, N as CO2, methane, ammonia, water vapour) and to maintain pressure so liquid water does not boil
  • Distance from the Sun (the 'Goldilocks zone') gives insolation producing temperatures at which water is liquid (0-35 °C over most of the surface) and proteins are not denatured
  • Too close (Venus) - too hot; too far (Mars) - too cold
/ 3
07.3 (2 marks)
  • The molten core produces a magnetosphere that deflects the solar wind / charged particles, preventing biologically damaging radiation reaching the surface
  • The 24-hour rotation limits heating by day and cooling by night, reducing temperature extremes (Venus' slow rotation gives extreme conditions)
/ 2
07.4 (2 marks)
  • The tilted axis causes seasonal variation in insolation as the Earth orbits the Sun
  • Producing seasons (temperature, day length) to which species' life cycles (breeding, migration, dormancy) are adapted; the Moon's gravity keeps the tilt stable and creates tides
/ 2

Question 810 marks · Scientific methodologies & sampling design · spec 3.7.1 · Genn pp. 390-393 · RM-01

A student investigated whether the number of earthworms per m2 changes with distance from a hedge across an arable field.

[2 marks]08.1Explain why systematic sampling along a transect is more appropriate than random sampling for this investigation.
[4 marks]08.2Describe how a preliminary study could be used to decide the distance between sampling sites and the number of samples at each site.
[4 marks]08.3Describe how earthworms could be extracted from a 0.5 m x 0.5 m sample area and give two limitations of the method.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • There is an environmental gradient (distance from the hedge / changing conditions) so samples must be taken at regular intervals along it to detect the change
  • Random sampling might leave gaps / miss the pattern; systematic sampling avoids bias while covering the gradient
/ 2
08.2 (4 marks)
  • Sample at close intervals (eg every 1 m) first and see at what spacing the variations are still detected without unnecessary data; wider intervals (eg 4 m) may miss peaks and troughs
  • Take increasing numbers of samples at one site and calculate the running mean; the number needed is where the mean stops fluctuating / stabilises
  • Calculate standard deviation to assess scatter: more variable data need more samples
  • Test the method (extraction technique, timing) for reliability before the main study
/ 4
08.3 (4 marks)
  • Remove surface vegetation; pour an irritant solution (dilute mustard / detergent in water) onto the area until saturated (and around it), keep adding as it infiltrates
  • Collect worms that come to the surface for a standardised time; count / identify / weigh
  • Limitations: solution may not percolate impermeable soils or reach deep worms; worms may move sideways or downwards; some may die or not respond; slow-moving worms missed
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 390-393; AQA spec 3.7.1.

[4 marks]09.4Describe how each of the following measures obtained from commercial catches can indicate overfishing.

09.1 (2 marks)
  • 600 + (90 + 150) - (220 + 80) = 540
  • Stock fell by 60 so the catch exceeds the MSY / unsustainable
