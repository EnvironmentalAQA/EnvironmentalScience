Question 15 marks · Energy & material cycles: natural vs human systems · spec 3.6.2-3.6.3 · Genn pp. 373 · SUS-15
[1 mark]01.1Which of the following is a feature of natural systems? Tick one box.
  • A  Reactions that require high temperatures
  • B  Reliance on non-renewable energy
  • C  Waste products that are the raw materials for other processes
  • D  Dispersal of elements so they cannot be re-used

Show mark scheme
01.1 (1 marks)
  • Waste products that are the raw materials for other processes
/ 1
01.2 (2 marks)

[3 marks]02.1Use Table 1 to identify which species is r-selected and which is k-selected, and explain the difference between the strategies.
[3 marks]02.2Explain why k-selected species are much more easily over-exploited than r-selected species.

02.1 (3 marks)
  • Anchovy r-selected; blue whale k-selected
  • r-selected: mature quickly, produce many young, disperse widely, short lives; adapted to respond rapidly to high death rates / unstable conditions (mice, locusts, greenfly, rabbits)
  • k-selected: mature late, few young with parental care, long-lived; populations stable near carrying capacity (whales, elephants, rhinos)
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
  • Low reproductive rate means losses from hunting / habitat change cannot be replaced quickly
  • An increase in death rate above the low birth rate causes a population crash
  • Recovery takes decades (eg blue whale still far below pre-whaling numbers after the 1986 moratorium); r-selected species rebound within a few years once exploitation stops
/ 3
02.3 (1 marks)
  • New population = starting population + births + immigrants - deaths - emigrants
/ 1
02.4 (3 marks)
  • The greatest rate of exploitation (harvest) possible without causing long-term population decline
  • Equals the surplus production: highest when the population is well below carrying capacity where growth rate is fastest (middle of the sigmoid curve)
  • At carrying capacity net growth is zero so any harvest reduces the population; harvesting above MSY causes decline
/ 3

[4 marks]03.1Use Table 1 to explain the relative merits of extensive and intensive aquaculture.
[3 marks]03.2Describe the stages in breeding and rearing salmon in an intensive farm.

03.1 (4 marks)
  • Extensive: low inputs of energy, food and chemicals so low cost and low environmental impact; sustainable; but low yield per area and dependent on natural productivity
  • Intensive: control of all limiting factors gives very high yield and profit per area; but high inputs (fishmeal from wild fish, energy), disease and lice at high density, pollution from wastes, antibiotic / pesticide use, escapes
  • Judgement depends on species, market and site
/ 4

  • Eggs raised in aerated freshwater tanks, dead / diseased eggs removed daily; fry fed fishmeal pellets and moved through tanks as they grow
  • After 12-18 months smolts transferred to seawater cages, fed pellets until harvest at 3-5 kg
/ 3
03.3 (3 marks)
  • About 10% of the world's mangroves have been cleared for coastal shrimp lagoons (up to 100 ha), removing nurseries and coastal protection
  • Lagoons need water exchange to remove wastes and aeration to prevent deoxygenation as wastes decompose
  • Eyestalk ablation stimulates breeding; high densities increase disease so antibiotics / pesticides used
/ 3

Question 410 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 18-19 · BIOV-07
[2 marks]04.1Explain why the ecosystem services provided by other species are often given a low conservation priority.
[3 marks]04.2Explain how soil organisms provide services that humans depend on.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why pollination by insects is more useful than wind pollination to plants growing in forests.
[3 marks]04.4Explain how seed dispersal by animals increases the survival of a plant's offspring.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • The processes (eg regulation of atmospheric gases by photosynthesis and respiration) act in a dynamic equilibrium and cancel each other out
  • Because there is no overall change their actions go unnoticed / their importance is not appreciated until they are lost
/ 2
04.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Invertebrates, fungi and bacteria break down / decompose dead organic matter, releasing nutrients for plant growth
  • The organic matter and humus produced hold the soil together (reducing erosion) and regulate the water cycle, giving more even river flow and less flooding
  • Organic acids from decomposition aid the weathering of rock, producing more soil and releasing more nutrients
  • Microbes carry out the processes of the carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles so wastes do not build up and nutrients are not depleted
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Trees reduce wind velocity so wind pollination would be ineffective / unreliable over long distances
  • Insects search over long distances for flowers so dispersed populations can be pollinated, and less pollen needs to be produced (energy saving); specialised flowers reduce pollen being wasted on other species
/ 2
04.4 (3 marks)
  • Dispersal is not haphazard: animals live in the same habitat as the plant so are likely to carry seeds to locations where they can survive
  • Seeds can be carried longer distances than by wind and can be larger, containing more nutrients for the seedling
  • Seeds passing through the gut are deposited in faecal material which acts as a fertiliser, and passage through the intestine may stimulate germination
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 18-19; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.3.

Question 610 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2 · Genn pp. 7-8 · LIFE-02
[4 marks]06.1Explain four properties of water that make it essential for life.
[4 marks]06.2Describe how photosynthetic organisms changed the atmosphere and explain how this allowed life to colonise the land.
[2 marks]06.3Explain how carbon sequestration by early photoautotrophs helped maintain a suitable temperature on Earth.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (4 marks)
  • General physiological solvent: most biological reactions take place between substances dissolved in water
  • Transport medium in blood / sap carrying gases, sugars, amino acids, nutrients, wastes
  • High specific heat capacity moderates temperature changes; evaporation absorbs heat (cooling)
  • Anomalous expansion: ice floats so water bodies do not freeze solid; absorbs UV protecting early marine life; provides aquatic habitats
/ 4
06.2 (4 marks)
  • From about 2.7 billion years ago photosynthetic archaea / bacteria (then algae and plants) released oxygen; at first it reacted with dissolved iron (forming iron ore deposits) then built up in the oceans and atmosphere (from about 2.45 billion years ago)
  • Oxygen in the stratosphere absorbed UV, forming ozone (O3) in a dynamic equilibrium of O, O2 and O3
  • The ozone layer reduced UV reaching the surface so organisms could survive out of the water
  • Oxygen allowed aerobic respiration releasing more energy so complex multicellular organisms evolved; many anaerobic archaea died out
/ 4
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Photosynthesis removed CO2 (a greenhouse gas) from the atmosphere, storing carbon in sediments, carbonate rocks and fossil fuels
  • This reduced infrared retention, offsetting the increasing brightness of the Sun (about 10% per billion years) so temperatures did not rise excessively
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 7-8; AQA spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2.

[25 marks]11.1Using tropical coral reefs as an example, discuss how the ecological features of a habitat determine both its importance and its vulnerability to human activities.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Ecological features: cnidarian polyps, cilia, nematocysts, zooxanthellae symbiosis, limestone skeletons building reefs, complex structure and food web; specific abiotic requirements (temperature, light, salinity, low turbidity)

  • Conservation: MPAs, No Take Zones, fishing controls, tourism management, protection of linked habitats, CITES
  • Comparison with other habitats (rainforests, mangroves, Antarctica) where relevant
  • Evaluation of local vs global threats and justified conclusion

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how feedback mechanisms, tipping points and diversity influence the stability of natural systems, and how human activities affect them.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Sustainability definitions; population and per capita impact; attitudes of rural and urban societies
  • Negative feedback: cloud cover and albedo, CO2 and photosynthesis, evaporation and precipitation, density-dependent population regulation
  • Positive feedback driven by human activities: permafrost, acidification, albedo decline, methane hydrates, fires, cirrus clouds, decomposition
  • Tipping points: rare under negative feedback; large events (Permian methane hydrate); self-sustaining change from human activities
  • Diversity and resistance: reefs and rainforests vs low-diversity agroecosystems lacking detritivores, pollinators and predators
  • Human responses that override feedback (fertilisers, irrigation, pesticides); links to climate change chapter; justified conclusion
