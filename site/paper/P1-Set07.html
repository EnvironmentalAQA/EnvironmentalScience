Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Site factors: topography - narrow exit from a large deep basin so a small dam holds a large volume; impermeable, strong bedrock without faults or seismic activity; large catchment with regular high rainfall and low evaporation; catchment land use with low pollution risk (pesticides, industry) and low soil erosion to avoid sedimentation; forest / vegetation in the flooded area decaying anaerobically releasing methane; access for workers and materials and distance to demand; value of the land lost (urban, conservation, agricultural) - loss-benefit analysis differs between countries

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

A reservoir site is judged first on topography: a narrow exit from a large, deep basin lets a small, cheap dam hold a large volume. The bedrock must be impermeable so water is not lost and strong enough, without faults or seismic activity, to carry the dam. The catchment should be large with regular, high rainfall and low evaporation, and its land uses should not pose a pollution risk from industry or pesticides or cause the soil erosion that would silt the reservoir. Flooding forest or vegetation causes anaerobic decay and methane. Access for workers and materials, distance to the area of demand, and the value of the land lost - urban areas and nature reserves protected, farmland less so - complete a loss-benefit analysis that different countries weigh differently.

Supply can also be increased without new reservoirs: rainwater collection in cities, estuary barrages, desalination where freshwater is genuinely inadequate, newly found aquifers in North Africa and Kenya, inter-basin transfers as in China and from Wales to England, artificial recharge of aquifers with wet-season surplus, river-regulation reservoirs and afforestation to even out flow. Demand can be cut by low-water appliances, xeriscaping, drip irrigation, grey-water re-use, metering and repairing the leaks that lose about a fifth of UK supply, and treatment lets contaminated sources be re-used.

Reservoirs give large, reliable supplies and flood control but flood land and habitats and release methane; conservation is cheap and quick but limited by behaviour; transfers create conflicts between regions; desalination carries a high energy and carbon cost. As demand grows, the sustainable answer is conservation first, then a combination of sources matched to each region.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (4 marks)

A reservoir needs a large catchment area with lots of rain, impermeable rock so the water does not drain away, and a narrow valley so the dam is small. It should be near the people who need the water and not flood important land. Other ways to get water include desalination, which is expensive, and collecting rainwater. Water can be conserved by fixing leaks, using water meters and having low-flush toilets. All of these help make water supplies more sustainable.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 lists correct site factors but without the reasons (why impermeable, why the valley shape matters, why land use in the catchment matters, methane from flooded vegetation).
  • Level 3 covers far more alternatives (aquifer recharge, river regulation, transfers, afforestation) and evaluates each; Level 2 evaluates only desalination.
  • Level 3 ends with a prioritised judgement; Level 2 ends with 'all of these help'.
