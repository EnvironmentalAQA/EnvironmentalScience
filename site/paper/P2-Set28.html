Question 110 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 316-320 · AGR-05
[4 marks]01.1Describe two case studies of biological control, one successful and one that caused problems.
[2 marks]01.2Explain how pheromone traps can be used in pest control.
[3 marks]01.3Explain why the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in livestock is a concern for human health.
[1 mark]01.4Give one way farmers can support crop pollinators.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Success: Cactoblastis moth introduced from South America controlled prickly pear cactus in Australia; Encarsia parasitic wasps control greenhouse whitefly
  • Problems: cane toads introduced to Australia to control sugar cane beetles did not control them but ate many other species and are toxic to predators; rosy wolfsnail introduced to Polynesia to control African land snails ate endemic Partulid snails (48 species extinct); harlequin ladybirds introduced to Europe eat native ladybirds
  • Lesson: control agents should be specialist feeders on the pest
/ 4
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Release an artificial sex scent attracting males (or females) of the pest species
  • Used to monitor when the pest arrives so pesticide is timed correctly, or (in enclosed greenhouses) to trap enough of one sex to prevent breeding
/ 2
01.3 (3 marks)
  • Regular low doses kill only the most sensitive bacteria so resistant strains survive and multiply
  • Agricultural use exceeds medical use, so resistance spreads
  • Resistant zoonotic bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter) can transfer to humans and are harder to treat; hence reduced use of antibiotics is a sustainability goal
/ 3
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Grow nectar-rich flowering plants alongside crops; restrict pesticides harmful to bees / spray when not in flower; introduce bee hives
/ 1

Question 210 marks · Manipulation of food species & agricultural energetics · spec 3.5.1.2 · Genn pp. 321-323 · FS-01
[6 marks]02.1Compare selective breeding, asexual reproduction and cross-breeding as methods of improving livestock or crops.
[4 marks]02.2Explain how artificial insemination and embryo transfer increase the rate at which desirable characteristics spread through a livestock population.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (6 marks)
  • Selective breeding: parents chosen for desirable genetic characteristics (Limousin muscle growth, Merino wool, Cheviot hardiness); combines genes but characteristics of offspring not fully predictable; many generations needed; risk of inbreeding
  • Asexual reproduction (cuttings, runners, tissue culture, cloning): offspring genetically identical to the parent so characteristics are predictable and survival high; but no variation so no improvement and vulnerability to the same disease; cloning of animals (nucleus transfer into an enucleated egg, surrogate) still developing
  • Cross-breeding: two breeds combined to give a mix of characteristics with hybrid vigour (heterosis) and lower inbreeding risk, eg Zebu (heat tolerant) x Ayrshire (high milk yield)
/ 6
02.2 (4 marks)
  • AI: semen from a male with desirable characteristics can fertilise many more females than natural mating; frozen semen stored and transported easily and used after the male dies
  • Embryo transfer: FSH stimulates a superior female to release many eggs, which are fertilised (IVF) with sperm from a superior male
  • Each embryo is implanted in a different surrogate female
  • So the donor produces many offspring per year rather than one per pregnancy
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 321-323; AQA spec 3.5.1.2.
Question 310 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 24-25 · HUM-04
[2 marks]03.1Explain why introduced species often have a greater effect on island communities than on mainland communities.
[6 marks]03.2For each of the following, describe an example of an introduced species and explain its effect on an indigenous species.
[2 marks]03.3Explain how hybridisation with an introduced species threatens the red deer and the Scottish wildcat.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Island communities evolved from few colonising species in isolation, often without mammal predators or large herbivores
  • So indigenous species lack adaptations (eg ground-nesting birds, flightlessness) to survive introduced cats, rats, pigs, goats
/ 2
03.2 (6 marks)
  • Competitor: grey squirrel (North America) out-competes red squirrel - digests acorns, larger, wins nest sites; rhododendron shades ground and releases toxins preventing native regrowth
  • Predator: American mink (escaped fur farms) reduced water vole range; cane toads in Australia; cats / rats / pigs on islands eating ground-nesting birds; Nile perch in Lake Victoria ate cichlids to extinction
  • Pathogen: squirrel pox virus carried by grey squirrels kills red squirrels; crayfish plague carried by signal crayfish kills white-clawed crayfish; Dutch elm disease, ash dieback, sudden oak death imported on plants / soil
/ 6
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Closely related introduced species (sika deer; domestic cats) cross-breed producing fertile hybrids
  • The natural gene pool is changed / diluted with genes it would not naturally contain, so the pure indigenous species may disappear
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 24-25; AQA spec 3.1.2.2.
Question 410 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 369, 386-387 · FOR-05

The Rio Bravo Conservation and Management Area in Belize exploits mahogany under a sustainable management plan while conserving a rainforest of over 200 tree, 400 bird and 70 mammal species.

[5 marks]04.1Describe five features of the timber harvesting at Rio Bravo that make it sustainable.
[3 marks]04.2Explain why involving local communities is essential to the success of the reserve.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why selective logging is better for wildlife than clear-felling, and give one disadvantage.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (5 marks)
  • Harvest rate below the maximum sustainable yield; only economically valuable species (mahogany) selectively logged, not clear-felled
  • Trees felled only where other mahoganies stand upwind so seeds recolonise the clearing; large seed trees and small trees left to grow
  • Buffer zones with no harvesting or machinery near rivers / wetlands
  • Restricted machinery and planned track routes re-used to avoid damaging roots of retained / rare / fruit trees
  • Each felled tree tagged and tracked to the retailer for FSC accreditation; rangers control illegal logging; tree nurseries replant over-exploited and ecologically important species
/ 5
04.2 (3 marks)
  • The reserve removed traditional uses (fuelwood, timber, farming clearings, hunting, medicines) so people would otherwise resent / ignore it
  • Employment as rangers, forestry and tourism workers; ecotourists use local restaurants, produce and souvenirs; offcuts given to local carvers
  • Benefits give the community an incentive to protect the forest; carbon sequestration payments from companies fund management
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Removing individual trees leaves small clearings so remaining populations survive nearby and the forest structure / microclimate is kept; timber resource used over a longer period
  • Disadvantage: more labour-intensive and expensive; more tracks needed per tree
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 369, 386-387; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.

[4 marks]05.1Use Table 1 to explain why orange roughy populations collapsed within ten years of large-scale fishing beginning in the late 1970s.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why herring populations recover from overfishing much faster than cod.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why the total mass of fish landed can stay the same while a population is being overfished.
[2 marks]05.4Describe how the Greenland shark and blue (common) skate illustrate the threat of by-catch to k-selected species.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • k-selected: very slow growth, does not breed until 30, few eggs, so the population cannot replace losses
  • Fish reach catchable size (20) before they breed (30) so fishing can remove the entire future breeding population
  • Deep-water aggregations easily located and caught by trawling
  • Recovery takes decades so fisheries have had to be closed
/ 4
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Herring breed at 2 years - the same age they become catchable - so there is always a breeding population and many young
  • Cod are catchable at 2 but do not mature until 4, so heavy fishing removes most fish before they breed
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Larger numbers of smaller / younger fish are caught, or bigger boats, nets and sonar increase catch effort
  • Masks the decline of the breeding population until a sudden collapse
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Neither is a target species but both are caught in demersal trawls; skate now extinct in the Baltic and critically endangered elsewhere
  • Very late maturity (skate 11 years, Greenland shark 100-150 years, living over 400) and few young mean even low by-catch mortality exceeds replacement
/ 2

Question 610 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2 · Genn pp. 7-8 · LIFE-02
[4 marks]06.1Explain four properties of water that make it essential for life.
[4 marks]06.2Describe how photosynthetic organisms changed the atmosphere and explain how this allowed life to colonise the land.
[2 marks]06.3Explain how carbon sequestration by early photoautotrophs helped maintain a suitable temperature on Earth.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (4 marks)
  • General physiological solvent: most biological reactions take place between substances dissolved in water
  • Transport medium in blood / sap carrying gases, sugars, amino acids, nutrients, wastes
  • High specific heat capacity moderates temperature changes; evaporation absorbs heat (cooling)
  • Anomalous expansion: ice floats so water bodies do not freeze solid; absorbs UV protecting early marine life; provides aquatic habitats
/ 4
06.2 (4 marks)
  • From about 2.7 billion years ago photosynthetic archaea / bacteria (then algae and plants) released oxygen; at first it reacted with dissolved iron (forming iron ore deposits) then built up in the oceans and atmosphere (from about 2.45 billion years ago)
  • Oxygen in the stratosphere absorbed UV, forming ozone (O3) in a dynamic equilibrium of O, O2 and O3
  • The ozone layer reduced UV reaching the surface so organisms could survive out of the water
  • Oxygen allowed aerobic respiration releasing more energy so complex multicellular organisms evolved; many anaerobic archaea died out
/ 4
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Photosynthesis removed CO2 (a greenhouse gas) from the atmosphere, storing carbon in sediments, carbonate rocks and fossil fuels
  • This reduced infrared retention, offsetting the increasing brightness of the Sun (about 10% per billion years) so temperatures did not rise excessively
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 7-8; AQA spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2.
Question 710 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 67-71 · HAB-11

Figure 1 shows a simplified Antarctic food web.

Figure 1

Primary consumersZooplankton, krill
Secondary consumersSmall fish, squid, baleen whales, crabeater seals, penguins
Higher consumersToothfish, albatross, leopard seal, killer whale
[3 marks]07.1Explain why the sea around Antarctica is so productive despite the cold.
[3 marks]07.2Use Figure 1 to explain why fishing for krill and for Patagonian toothfish threatens other species.
[3 marks]07.3Explain the importance of Antarctica in controlling global natural cycles.
[1 mark]07.4Explain why the area of winter sea ice around Antarctica has increased in recent years even though air and sea are warming.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • Upwelling currents bring nutrients from the seabed to surface waters
  • 24-hour daylight in summer allows a huge phytoplankton bloom
  • Algae also grow under sea ice where krill shelter and feed; krill support most of the food web
/ 3
07.2 (3 marks)
  • Krill is the main food of baleen whales, penguins, seals and fish so over-harvesting removes food from most of the web
  • Toothfish overfishing removes the food of sperm whales and elephant seals
  • Long-lining for toothfish catches and drowns albatrosses on the hooks (by-catch)
/ 3
07.3 (3 marks)
  • Ice on land stores 70% of the Earth's fresh water, keeping sea levels low
  • High albedo of ice reflects sunlight, limiting warming (reduction in ice area causes further warming)
  • Carbon sequestration: carbon in algae eaten by krill sinks to the seabed in krill faeces
/ 3
07.4 (1 marks)
  • Stronger winds spreading ice; more snowfall from warmer, moister air freezing onto slush; fresh meltwater from land forming a surface layer that freezes more easily (long-term trend still expected to be a decline)
/ 1

Question 810 marks · Impacts of agriculture, social/political factors & sustainable agriculture · spec 3.5.1.3-3.5.1.4 · Genn pp. 329-333 · AGI-02
[3 marks]08.1Describe how agriculture alters the hydrological cycle.
[4 marks]08.2Explain how each of the following consumer choices can reduce the environmental impact of food production.
[3 marks]08.3Explain why post-war European governments introduced grants and guaranteed prices, and describe one environmental consequence.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Irrigation depletes rivers and aquifers (eg Aral Sea, Ogallala); increases evaporation
  • Soil erosion and compaction reduce water retention and infiltration so runoff and river flow fluctuations increase
  • Evapotranspiration changes: increased in irrigated arid areas, decreased where forests were cleared
/ 3
08.2 (4 marks)
  • Seasonal / local: avoids energy for heating, lighting greenhouses out of season and long-distance transport (food miles) and the pollution this causes
  • Organic: uses natural processes (rotation, legumes, manure, biological control, predator habitats) instead of artificial fertilisers and pesticides, so less pollution, energy use and harm to non-target species; free-range and Fairtrade also accepted for welfare / social reasons
/ 4
08.3 (3 marks)
  • Food shortages after 1945 (dependence on US food aid); farmers could not afford loans to invest so grants funded machinery, drainage, hedgerow removal, liming, improved livestock
  • Guaranteed market / price support: government bought surpluses to hold prices up (and sold stores in poor years) giving farmers security to increase output
  • Consequences: loss of hedgerows and wetlands, intensification, pesticide and fertiliser pollution; later food surpluses (butter mountains) that could not be sold without harming LEDC producers
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 329-333; AQA spec 3.5.1.3-3.5.1.4.

A student sampled the ground-flora of two woodlands. Table 1 shows the results for woodland A.

Table 1
SpeciesNumber of individuals (n)
Bluebell40
Wood anemone30
Dog's mercury20
Bramble10
Total (N)100
[3 marks]09.1Use the formula D = N(N - 1) / Σn(n - 1) to calculate Simpson's index of diversity for woodland A.
[2 marks]09.2Woodland B had an index of 1.6. State what this shows and give one limitation of using the index alone to compare the woodlands.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
  • Σn(n - 1) = (40 x 39) + (30 x 29) + (20 x 19) + (10 x 9) = 1560 + 870 + 380 + 90 = 2900
  • N(N - 1) = 100 x 99 = 9900
  • D = 9900 / 2900 = 3.41 (accept 3.4)
/ 3

  • Woodland A has a higher biodiversity (greater variety and more even abundance) than woodland B
  • The index does not identify which species are present / a rare or important species may be missed; depends on identification skill and sampling method

Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
