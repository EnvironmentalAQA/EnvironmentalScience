Question 110 marks · Specialist techniques & statistical analysis · spec 3.7.2.3 · Genn pp. 420-423 · RM-19

A student measured the wind speed at 10 sites inside a woodland and at 10 sites in a nearby clearing.

Table 1
LocationMean wind speed / m s-1Standard deviation / m s-1
Woodland1.20.4
Clearing3.51.1
[3 marks]01.1Name the statistical test the student should use to find out whether the difference between the means is significant. Give two reasons for your choice.
[1 mark]01.2Explain what the student should do if it is not possible to show that the data are normally distributed.
[2 marks]01.3The test gave p = 0.02. Explain what this means.
[2 marks]01.4Explain how the standard deviations in Table 1 support the conclusion that the means are different.
[2 marks]01.5Explain why a statistically significant result does not prove that trees cause the lower wind speed.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • t-test
  • The data are measured (continuous) values and two means are being compared
  • The data are (assumed to be) normally distributed
/ 3
01.2 (1 marks)
  • Use the Mann-Whitney U test instead (compares medians)
/ 1
01.3 (2 marks)
  • There is a 2% probability that the difference occurred by random chance
  • As this is less than 0.05 the difference is statistically significant / the null hypothesis is rejected
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • The ranges of values (mean plus or minus SD) do not overlap: woodland up to 1.6 and clearing down to 2.4
  • About 68% of values lie within one SD of each mean so the two data sets are clearly separated
/ 2
01.5 (2 marks)
  • Significance shows the difference is unlikely to be due to chance, not that a causal relationship exists
  • Another variable (eg topography / aspect of the sites) could cause both; a controlled experiment or further evidence is needed
/ 2

Question 210 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.3 · Genn pp. 233-235 · ECON-07

[2 marks]02.1Calculate the overall efficiency of each station.

  • Conventional: 40 / 100 = 40%
  • CHP: (35 + 45) / 100 = 80%

  • A sphere has the smallest surface area for any volume; less surface means less heat loss (with insulation of low thermal conductivity)

Question 310 marks · Importance & features of energy resources · spec 3.3.2 · Genn pp. 175-177 · ENF-03
[3 marks]03.1Distinguish between renewable and depletable energy resources, giving an example of a resource that is both renewable and depletable.
[3 marks]03.2Explain why the abundance of an energy resource is not the same as the amount available for use. Give two examples.
[4 marks]03.3Describe the locational constraints of two named renewable energy resources.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • Renewable: re-forms naturally relatively quickly so use does not necessarily reduce future availability (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biofuel)
  • Depletable: use can reduce future availability - all non-renewables (fossil fuels, uranium) plus renewables exploited unsustainably
  • Wood / biofuels are renewable but depletable if forests are felled faster than they regrow
/ 3
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Other factors (technology, location, cost) restrict how much can be harnessed
  • Examples: coal too deep to mine; winds high above the ground / far offshore where turbines cannot be placed; waves in deep water where equipment cannot be anchored; low-intensity sunlight that cannot produce high temperatures
  • New technologies or higher prices may make these usable in future
/ 3
03.3 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Wind: strong reliable winds - shallow seas, open plains, uplands; low land-use conflict (not near urban areas, ecologically sensitive or scenic areas); near the grid
  • HEP: high reliable rainfall, large catchment, narrow valley for a small dam with a large basin, impermeable stable bedrock
  • Solar: high light intensity, low cloud cover
  • Wave: coasts with strong reliable prevailing winds and a long fetch
  • Tidal: large tidal range, coastal features (estuaries, channels) that focus the flow
  • Geothermal: hot rocks near the surface / recent volcanic activity
  • Biofuels: nearby farmland / forest / urban waste to avoid transporting bulky low-density fuel
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 175-177; AQA spec 3.3.2.

Question 510 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 210-211, 214-217 · REN-05
[2 marks]05.1Explain the locational requirements for harnessing wave power.
[2 marks]05.2Describe how one named wave power device generates electricity.
[2 marks]05.3Give two design problems that have delayed the development of wave power.
[4 marks]05.4Compare the environmental impacts of tidal barrages, tidal lagoons and in-stream tidal turbines.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • High mean wind velocities that are consistent in strength and direction
  • A long fetch (distance of open water) over which waves build up, eg Atlantic coasts of the UK
/ 2
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Oscillating water column (eg Islay Limpet): waves force water up and down in a submerged chamber; the air forced in and out drives a turbine
  • Or point absorber (floating buoy moving relative to a fixed base turns a generator); surface attenuator (Pelamis - hinged sections push pistons pumping fluid over a turbine); overtopping device (Wave Dragon - waves fill a reservoir above sea level which drains through turbines); oscillating wave surge converter (Oyster)
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Equipment must withstand storms and corrosion
  • Difficult to anchor off stormy coasts / in deep water
  • Expensive to transmit electricity from isolated coasts to consumers
  • Competition with established technologies
/ 2
05.4 (4 marks)
  • Barrage: huge quantities of construction materials; reduces tidal range so intertidal mudflats used by wading birds are lost; changes sedimentation (erosion near turbines, deposition elsewhere), lower turbidity; pollutants concentrate in the lagoon; blocks migratory fish and shipping
  • Lagoon: similar but smaller scale; only part of the estuary enclosed so the most sensitive areas and main channel can be avoided
  • In-stream turbines: do not block the tidal flow so little impact on range or sediment; but noise affects cetaceans and many dispersed turbines could have a larger total impact; harness less energy
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 210-211, 214-217; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 610 marks · The atmosphere: energy processes (UV, IR, visible light) · spec 3.2.1.1 · Genn pp. 87-89 · ATM-02

Figure 1 shows a simplified energy budget for the Earth and its atmosphere. Values are percentages of incoming solar radiation.

Figure 1
Energy flowPercentage of incoming solar radiation / %
Reflected by clouds, dust and atmosphere25
Reflected by the Earth's surface5
Absorbed by the atmosphere20
Absorbed by the Earth's surface50
Infrared emitted by the surface and absorbed by greenhouse gases95
Infrared re-emitted by the atmosphere back to the surface85
Infrared emitted to space70
[1 mark]06.1Use Figure 1 to calculate the planetary albedo of the Earth.
[6 marks]06.2Describe how each of the following wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation behaves in the atmosphere.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why the amount of infrared re-emitted to the surface (85%) is larger than the amount emitted to space (70%).
[1 mark]06.4Explain the term thermal stratification of the atmosphere.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (1 marks)
  • 25 + 5 = 30%
/ 1
06.2 (6 marks)
  • UV: short wavelength; mostly absorbed in the stratosphere by monatomic, diatomic and triatomic oxygen (ozone layer) which are formed and destroyed in a dynamic equilibrium; converted to chemical energy in these reactions; little reaches the surface
  • Visible light: transmitted through the atmosphere easily (some reflected by clouds / dust); absorbed by the Earth's surface and converted to heat; some absorbed by chlorophyll and converted to chemical energy in photosynthesis
  • IR: emitted by the warm surface (long-wave); absorbed by greenhouse gases (CO2, water vapour, methane) and converted to heat; the warm atmosphere re-emits IR to the surface and reduces conduction losses; some escapes to space
/ 6
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Greenhouse gases absorb most of the IR emitted by the surface and re-radiate it in all directions, including back down
  • The surface is repeatedly warmed and re-emits, so the total IR exchanged between surface and atmosphere exceeds the incoming solar energy; only the net loss (70%) balances the absorbed solar energy at equilibrium
/ 2
06.4 (1 marks)
  • The atmosphere forms layers (troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere) with different temperature gradients because energy is absorbed at different altitudes (eg UV heating the stratosphere, surface heating the troposphere)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 87-89; AQA spec 3.2.1.1.
Question 710 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2, 3.2.2.3 · Genn pp. 96-99 · CC-03

Figure 1 shows the sea surface temperature anomaly in the eastern Pacific off Peru and the annual Peruvian anchovy catch for a series of years including a strong El Niño event in 1997-98.

Figure 1
[4 marks]07.1Describe the normal (non-El Niño) pattern of winds and ocean currents in the tropical Pacific and explain why the Peruvian coast supports a rich fishery.
[3 marks]07.2Explain how an El Niño event caused the collapse of the anchovy catch in 1998 shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]07.3Describe two other effects of El Niño events.
[1 mark]07.4Suggest why some scientists link global climate change to changes in the frequency of El Niño events, and why this link is uncertain.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • Trade winds blow westwards across the Pacific near the equator, driving a surface current in the same direction
  • Surface water moving away from South America is replaced by deep, cold water drawn upwards (upwelling) near the coast
  • The upwelling water is rich in nutrients (nitrates, phosphates) so large algal / phytoplankton blooms occur
  • These support a rich food web including the anchovy fishery; the water warms as it travels west
/ 4
07.2 (3 marks)
  • The trade winds weaken or reverse so the surface current slows / reverses and warm water spreads east
  • Upwelling of nutrient-rich cold water off South America stops
  • Phytoplankton productivity collapses so the food web / anchovy population collapses; catch fell from about 7.7 to 1.7 million tonnes
/ 3
07.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Heavy rain and floods in the normally dry coastal deserts of Peru / South America
  • Drought and reduced rainfall in eastern Australia / Indonesia
  • Droughts in NE Africa, southern Africa, China
  • Fewer hurricanes in the North Atlantic / fewer cyclones in Japan
/ 2
07.4 (1 marks)
  • Events appear to be occurring more frequently and are driven by wind patterns that warming may alter, but the causes of ENSO are not fully understood and natural fluctuations make trends hard to separate

Question 1010 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.5 · Genn pp. 245-254 · AIR-07
[9 marks]10.1Explain how the properties of smoke and of the gases involved in acid rain lead to their environmental effects, and evaluate the effectiveness of the methods used to control them.
[1 mark]10.2State one effect of tropospheric ozone on plants.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Smoke: solid particulates (PM10, PM5, PM1) from incomplete combustion; small particles stay suspended, penetrate lungs; toxic chemicals (acids, phenols, metals) carried on particles; high albedo cools climate; blocks light reducing photosynthesis; damages buildings
  • Smoke smogs form under temperature inversions in valleys (London 1952, 12 000 deaths); synergism with SO2
  • Controls: Clean Air Act 1956 (smokeless zones), smokeless fuels / gas / electricity, coal treatment, electrostatic precipitators, cyclones, bag filters, scrubbers, DPFs, better combustion; smoke smogs have largely been eliminated in UK cities
  • Acid rain gases: SO2 (coal combustion, smelting sulfide ores) oxidised by ozone to SO3 - sulfuric acid; NOx (high temperature combustion) - nitric acid; HCl from PVC incineration; reactive gases travel long distances (transboundary pollution)
  • Effects: pH denatures proteins / enzymes in exposed cells (gills, eggs, root hairs, stomata); dissolves exoskeletons; leaches Ca and Mg then mobilises toxic Al and Pb; corrodes metals, dissolves limestone; severity depends on bedrock (limestone neutralises)
  • Controls: fuel desulfurisation (coal washing, hydrodesulfurisation, low-sulfur ship fuels), dry and wet FGD, fluidised bed combustion, catalytic converters, urea sprays; switching from coal to gas / renewables
  • Evaluation: SO2 emissions in Europe have fallen dramatically and lake pH is recovering; NOx harder to control because of growing vehicle numbers; catalytic converters need lead-free fuel and warm engines; FGD is costly and produces wastes / gypsum; controlling primary pollutants also prevents secondary pollutants (ozone, PANs)
  • Judgement on relative success of controls for particulates vs SO2 vs NOx
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
Any 1 from:
  • Reduces growth rates of trees / crops
  • Damages leaf cuticles (allowing SO2 to cause more damage)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 245-254; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.5.
