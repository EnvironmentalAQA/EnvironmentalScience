Question 110 marks · Thermal & oil pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.7 · Genn pp. 261-262 · WPOL-04

In 2020 the tanker MV Wakashio ran aground on a coral reef off Mauritius and leaked about 1000 tonnes of fuel oil into a shallow lagoon near mangroves and a marine park.

[3 marks]01.1Explain why inflatable booms were deployed immediately, and give one limitation of booms.
[3 marks]01.2Describe how skimmers and absorbent materials remove oil, and state how the collected oil is disposed of.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why dispersants were not used in the lagoon.
[2 marks]01.4Explain how bioremediation could help remove the oil that remained on the mangrove sediments, and suggest how it could be accelerated.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • Booms are floating tubes with a skirt that restrict the movement of floating oil, keeping it near the source / away from sensitive sites (mangroves, reef, marine park)
  • They work best in sheltered / calm water such as the lagoon
  • Limitation: they do not work in open water where currents or waves are strong; oil can flow under the skirt in a strong current
/ 3
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Skimmers: rotating (metal) discs / belts pick up floating oil which is scraped off and stored
  • Absorbent materials (high surface area, eg textile mops, straw, human hair) soak up oil and are then removed
  • Collected oil / absorbents are incinerated or sent to landfill (or oil recycled)
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Dispersants break the oil into an emulsion of droplets, making it more mobile so it spreads through the water column (to the reef / seabed organisms) rather than staying on the surface
  • They do not reduce the amount of oil; the dispersant / oil mixture may be toxic to corals and plankton in shallow, enclosed water
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Bacteria that break down hydrocarbons digest the residual oil (most rapid where temperature, oxygen and nutrients are high)
  • Accelerated by adding nutrients (nitrates / phosphates) and aerating (ploughing) / irrigating the sediment
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 261-262; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.7.

Question 310 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 192-194 · FN-05
[3 marks]03.1Describe three factors that have restricted the growth of nuclear power.
[3 marks]03.2Explain how a plutonium 'breeder' reactor can produce more fissile fuel than it uses.
[4 marks]03.3Give two advantages and two disadvantages of thorium reactors compared with uranium reactors.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Public opposition following accidents (Three Mile Island 1979, Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011)
  • Very complex, expensive technology needing an advanced industrial infrastructure
  • Concerns about links between civil nuclear materials and weapons / terrorism
  • Uncertainty over permanent disposal of radioactive waste
  • Uncertain decommissioning costs
/ 3
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Only 0.7% of natural uranium is fissile U-235; the remaining 99.3% is non-fissile U-238 (a 'fertile' fuel)
  • Neutron bombardment in the reactor converts U-238 to fissile plutonium-239 (via beta decays)
  • So much more energy is harnessed from the mined uranium; fast reactors need no moderator but are complex and costly
/ 3
03.3 (4 marks)
  • Advantages: thorium is three times more abundant; much less radioactive waste with shorter half-lives; harder to make weapons material; no fuel enrichment needed
  • Disadvantages: thorium-232 is not fissile so must be bred into uranium-233 (slow breeding rate); U-233 is an alpha emitter so very hazardous to handle; less developed technology with high development costs
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 192-194; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 410 marks · Energy conservation: transport, buildings, industry · spec 3.3.4.2.1 · Genn pp. 227-228 · ECON-03
[3 marks]04.1Explain what is meant by the embodied energy of a vehicle and how it can be reduced.
[4 marks]04.2Describe four principles of designing vehicles for end of life.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why there is an optimum speed for fuel efficiency in a car.
[1 mark]04.4Give one way driver behaviour or vehicle systems can reduce fuel use in congested traffic.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • The energy used to extract raw materials, manufacture the components and assemble the vehicle (for a typical car about 1.5 years of its fuel use)
  • Reduced by using recycled materials (eg recycled aluminium needs 1/20 of the energy of new)
  • Lighter designs using less material; longer vehicle life spreading the embodied energy
/ 3
04.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Use of recyclable materials wherever possible
  • Easy identification of components and their composition (code stamps)
  • Easy dismantling and separation of components / materials
  • Reusable components for new vehicles
  • Compostable materials for parts that cannot be recycled
/ 4
04.3 (2 marks)
  • At low speed a large proportion of the energy just runs the engine rather than moving the car
  • At high speed energy is wasted overcoming air resistance (which rises with the square of speed); optimum for many cars about 56 mph
/ 2
04.4 (1 marks)
  • Automatic stop / start systems cutting the engine when stationary; smooth driving in higher gears avoiding sudden braking / acceleration
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 227-228; AQA spec 3.3.4.2.1.
Question 510 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 150-151 · BGC-02
[6 marks]05.1Explain how each of the following human activities changes the amount of carbon in the atmosphere.
[3 marks]05.2Give three anaerobic environments created by human activities that release methane.
[1 mark]05.3Explain how rising atmospheric CO2 causes ocean acidification.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (6 marks)
  • Ploughing: increases the oxygen supply to soil decomposers so aerobic decomposition of dead organic matter is faster; carbon in the soil DOM store falls and atmospheric CO2 rises
  • Draining: waterlogged anaerobic conditions preserved dead organic matter (peat) and produced methane slowly; drainage makes the soil aerobic so stored carbon is rapidly decomposed to CO2 (though methane release stops)
  • Marine pollution: kills phytoplankton so less dissolved CO2 is absorbed by photosynthesis and less carbon sinks to sediments in dead plankton; less CO2 then dissolves from the atmosphere to replace it, so atmospheric CO2 rises
/ 6
05.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Rice paddy fields
  • Landfill sites
  • Anaerobic sediments in reservoirs
  • Livestock intestines (cattle)
  • Manure / slurry stores
/ 3
05.3 (1 marks)
  • More CO2 dissolves in the sea, forming carbonic acid which dissociates into hydrogen carbonate and hydrogen ions; more H+ lowers the pH
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 150-151; AQA spec 3.2.4.2.

Question 710 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.2 · Genn pp. 162-164 · SOIL-04
[4 marks]07.1Explain how each of the following human activities increases soil erosion.
[2 marks]07.2Explain why landslides become more likely on steep slopes some years after deforestation.
[4 marks]07.3Describe four environmental impacts of accelerated soil erosion away from the field where it occurs.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • Overgrazing: vegetation is eaten faster than it grows so soil is exposed; hooves disturb the soil, damage roots and compact it, reducing infiltration and increasing runoff
  • Reduced biota (ploughing, agrochemicals, less organic matter): less humus so less adhesion between particles; fewer worms so less aeration and lower infiltration capacity; fewer nutrients released so less vegetation cover
/ 4
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Tree roots that held the soil together decompose so the soil is bound less strongly
  • Heavy rain adds mass and lubricates particles; without interception more water enters the soil, making slumping more likely
/ 2
07.3 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Sedimentation in rivers reduces flow capacity so flooding downstream is more likely
  • Sedimentation in reservoirs reduces their storage volume
  • Coastal sedimentation covering reefs / seagrass; turbidity reducing light for aquatic plants
  • Increased atmospheric particulates from wind erosion worsening asthma; dust covering crops / urban areas
  • Desertification as vegetation cannot re-establish; reduced rainfall
  • Nutrients (phosphates) carried on soil particles causing eutrophication
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 162-164; AQA spec 3.2.5.2.

Question 910 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 269-272 · NUT-03

Figure 1 is a flow diagram of the main stages in a sewage treatment works using activated sludge treatment.

Figure 1
StageProcess
Pre-treatmentScreens and grit traps
Primary treatmentPrimary sedimentation
Secondary treatmentAeration tank then secondary sedimentation
Tertiary treatmentPhosphate stripping / micro-strainers / UV
Sludge treatmentAnaerobic digestion
[2 marks]09.1Explain the purpose of screens and grit traps.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why primary sedimentation is described as the most important process.
[3 marks]09.3Describe what happens in the aeration tank and explain why the sludge from the secondary sedimentation tank is returned to it.
[3 marks]09.4Explain why tertiary treatment may be needed before effluent is discharged, and describe one tertiary process.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • Screens (metal grills) trap floating / suspended non-biodegradable items (paper, plastic) which are landfilled or incinerated
  • Grit traps: the channel widens so flow slows and stones / road grit sink out because there is not enough kinetic energy to carry them
/ 2
09.2 (2 marks)
  • The effluent stands in large tanks so the faecal / organic solids sink and are removed as sludge
  • This removes over 95% of the organic matter from the fluid so the risk of deoxygenation of the river is greatly reduced
/ 2
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Aerobic bacteria digest / break down the remaining dissolved and suspended organic matter
  • Air (or pure oxygen) is mixed in by paddle wheels or air stones so oxygen shortage does not limit the aerobic process
  • The suspended bacteria collected in the secondary sedimentation tank are the 'activated sludge'; returning them maintains a large bacterial population so digestion is rapid
/ 3
09.4 (3 marks)
  • Effluent from secondary treatment may still contain dissolved inorganic nutrients (phosphates, nitrates) that cause eutrophication, and some bacteria
  • Needed where the discharge site is ecologically sensitive or used by many people (bathing water, drinking water abstraction)
  • Phosphate stripping with iron(III) sulfate; micro-strainers (very fine sieves) then UV light or chlorine to kill remaining bacteria
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 269-272; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.
Question 1010 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 89-107 · CC-10
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the success of the methods and strategies used to reduce global climate change.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by a carbon footprint.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Control of greenhouse gases: energy conservation (transport, buildings, industry), renewable and nuclear energy replacing fossil fuels, CCS, reduced deforestation and afforestation (carbon sequestration), methane capture from landfill / digesters, HFC controls, changes in agriculture
  • Geoengineering: white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - largely untried, possible unpredictable consequences
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement - targets, but non-binding / uneven participation; carbon trading and taxes; comparison of per capita carbon footprints between countries
  • Evidence of limited success: global CO2 concentrations still rising (about 280 ppm pre-industrial to over 410 ppm); temperatures up about 1 °C; emissions in some developed countries falling but rising in developing economies
  • Reasons for difficulty: fossil fuels underpin economies, need for major lifestyle change, cost, time delays and natural fluctuations masking effects, political disagreement, developing countries' right to develop
  • Adaptation strategies (flood defences, drainage, managed retreat, infrastructure changes) reduce impacts but do not address the cause
  • Comparison with the Montreal Protocol's success for ozone
  • Judgement: partial success - technology exists and some regional reductions achieved, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise; tipping points make urgency greater
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The total greenhouse gas / CO2 emissions caused by an individual, organisation, product or country (often expressed per capita per year)
/ 1

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss how knowledge of the properties of pollutants can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: state of matter, density, persistence, toxicity, specificity, reactivity, adsorption, solubility, bioaccumulation / biomagnification, synergism, mutagenic / carcinogenic / teratogenic action, mobility
  • How properties predict behaviour: dispersal (gases vs solids, density - Bhopal), how far and how long (persistence, mobility), where they concentrate (adsorption on sediments, food chains)
  • Environmental features interacting with properties: temperature inversions, currents, pH, oxygen, light, adsorbent materials
  • Control based on properties: persistent / liposoluble pesticides banned (organochlorines) and replaced by non-persistent / specific ones; systemic pesticides and seed coatings
  • Heavy metals: lead uses discontinued, storage at high pH to reduce solubility; mercury chemical form; radioactive waste categorised by half-life / activity and contained (encapsulation, sealed storage)
  • Reactive primary pollutants controlled to prevent secondary pollutants (catalytic converters, FGD, urea sprays); oil properties (floats, insoluble) exploited by booms and skimmers; density in cyclone separators
  • Principles: CPA and CGM based on properties; emission location / timing; ALARA and BATNEEC; polluter pays; precautionary principle
  • New technologies: satellite oil-spill monitoring, GPS, polymer adsorption, phytoremediation, bioremediation
  • Evaluation: prevention better than cure; limits of remediation; cost-effectiveness; contrast success of ozone (Montreal) with climate change; judgement
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
