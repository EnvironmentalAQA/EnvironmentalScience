Question 110 marks · Importance & features of energy resources · spec 3.3.2 · Genn pp. 175-177 · ENF-03
[3 marks]01.1Distinguish between renewable and depletable energy resources, giving an example of a resource that is both renewable and depletable.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why the abundance of an energy resource is not the same as the amount available for use. Give two examples.
[4 marks]01.3Describe the locational constraints of two named renewable energy resources.
Show mark scheme
01.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Renewable: re-forms naturally relatively quickly so use does not necessarily reduce future availability (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biofuel)
  • 1Depletable: use can reduce future availability - all non-renewables (fossil fuels, uranium) plus renewables exploited unsustainably
  • 1Wood / biofuels are renewable but depletable if forests are felled faster than they regrow
/ 3
01.2 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Other factors (technology, location, cost) restrict how much can be harnessed
  • 1Examples: coal too deep to mine; winds high above the ground / far offshore where turbines cannot be placed; waves in deep water where equipment cannot be anchored; low-intensity sunlight that cannot produce high temperatures
  • 1New technologies or higher prices may make these usable in future
/ 3
01.3 4 marks
Any 4 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 4.
  • 1Wind: strong reliable winds - shallow seas, open plains, uplands; low land-use conflict (not near urban areas, ecologically sensitive or scenic areas); near the grid
  • 1HEP: high reliable rainfall, large catchment, narrow valley for a small dam with a large basin, impermeable stable bedrock
  • 1Solar: high light intensity, low cloud cover
  • 1Wave: coasts with strong reliable prevailing winds and a long fetch
  • 1Tidal: large tidal range, coastal features (estuaries, channels) that focus the flow
  • 1Geothermal: hot rocks near the surface / recent volcanic activity
  • 1Biofuels: nearby farmland / forest / urban waste to avoid transporting bulky low-density fuel
/ 4

Question 310 marks · Noise pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.13 · Genn pp. 293-295, 418 · NOISE-05

A motor-racing circuit has residents living 500 m away. The circuit operator wants to compare two designs of acoustic barrier: an earth baffle mound and a timber acoustic fence.

[5 marks]03.1Describe how you would carry out an investigation to compare the effectiveness of the two barriers.
[1 mark]03.2Explain why readings should be taken with the same wind direction.
[2 marks]03.3Explain the difference between the LA10 and LA90 measures of traffic noise.
[2 marks]03.4Outline two ways in which the residents' houses could be designed to reduce the noise reaching occupants.
Show mark scheme
03.1 5 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Use a calibrated sound meter (dB(A)) at fixed distances (eg 10, 50, 100, 250, 500 m) from the track behind each barrier and at a control site with no barrier
  • 1Standardise the noise source: same cars / same race or a standard test noise; same time of day, wind speed / direction, weather; same height of meter above ground
  • 1Take repeated readings at each distance and calculate means (and standard deviation) to assess variability
  • 1Use a measure such as LA10 or Leq over a set period rather than a single peak reading
  • 1Compare reduction in dB relative to the control at each distance; plot noise level against distance for each barrier; statistical test (t-test) to assess significance
/ 5
03.2 1 mark
1 mark.
  • 1Wind carries sound / affects how far it travels so different wind directions would change readings independently of the barrier
/ 1
03.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1LA10 is the noise level exceeded for 10% of the period - a measure of the noisiest periods
  • 1LA90 is the level exceeded for 90% of the period - a measure of the quietest / background level
/ 2
03.4 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Double / triple glazing
  • 1Acoustic insulation in walls / roof
  • 1Bedrooms on the side facing away from the track
  • 1Dense hedges / trees and earth mounds in gardens
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 293-295, 418; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.13.
Question 410 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 395-396, 415-416 · RM-06

A student compared the invertebrates in a stream above and below a sewage outfall using kick sampling.

[3 marks]04.1Describe how kick sampling is carried out so that the samples from the two sites are comparable.
[3 marks]04.2Give three limitations of kick sampling.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how a Surber sampler improves on kick sampling.
[2 marks]04.4Explain why the invertebrates found give a better indication of long-term sewage pollution than a single measurement of dissolved oxygen.
Show mark scheme
04.1 3 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Net held with its straight edge on the river bed facing the current (open to the flow)
  • 1The bed in front is disturbed by kicking / scraping a boot for a standard number of kicks and time (eg 30 s)
  • 1Same substrate type, water depth and flow rate at both sites; same net mesh; repeat samples; invertebrates identified with a key and counted
/ 3
04.2 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Difficult to standardise the vigour / area kicked
  • 1Buried organisms or those fixed to stones are not dislodged
  • 1Some organisms swim and avoid the net
  • 1Flow rate varies and affects capture
  • 1Fragile organisms destroyed
/ 3
04.3 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1A fixed area of bed is enclosed by a frame so the sampled area is standardised; side nets reduce escapes
  • 1But only works in fast enough flow and is hard to set on rough substrates / deep water
/ 2
04.4 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Pollutant / oxygen levels fluctuate (eg with flow, time of day) so one reading may miss a pollution event
  • 1Sensitive taxa (stonefly, mayfly larvae) disappear and tolerant ones (midge larvae, hoglouse) remain, integrating conditions over weeks / months (biotic index)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 395-396, 415-416; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.

[4 marks]06.1Describe and explain the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why the atmosphere is described as being in a state of dynamic equilibrium.
[2 marks]06.3Give two ways in which the atmosphere supports life other than by providing gases for biological molecules.
[2 marks]06.4Name the two layers of the atmosphere most affected by human activities.
Show mark scheme
06.1 4 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1CO2 rises from about 412 ppm in January to a peak of 416 ppm in May, falls to a minimum of 407 ppm in September, then rises again
  • 1During spring / summer, photosynthesis by plants (growing season) removes CO2 faster than respiration releases it, so the concentration falls
  • 1In autumn / winter photosynthesis is reduced (leaf fall) but respiration and decomposition continue, so CO2 rises
  • 1The atmosphere is in dynamic equilibrium: the concentration fluctuates around a mean; more land / vegetation in the northern hemisphere makes the cycle large
/ 4
06.2 2 marks
1 mark for each point.
  • 1Natural processes that add gases (respiration, combustion, volcanoes, denitrification) are balanced by processes that remove them (photosynthesis, dissolving, nitrogen fixation)
  • 1So the average composition stays the same over long timescales although the rates vary and concentrations fluctuate around a mean
/ 2
06.3 2 marks
Any 2 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 2.
  • 1Absorbs harmful UV / solar wind radiation in the stratosphere (ozone layer)
  • 1Greenhouse gases delay the escape of infrared, keeping the surface warm (33 °C warmer than without)
  • 1Atmospheric pressure allows liquid water to exist
  • 1Distributes heat from the tropics to higher latitudes by winds
  • 1Transports water vapour to areas that would otherwise be dry

[3 marks]09.1Describe three factors that have restricted the growth of nuclear power.
[3 marks]09.2Explain how a plutonium 'breeder' reactor can produce more fissile fuel than it uses.

09.1 3 marks
Any 3 from the points below - 1 mark each, maximum 3.
  • 1Public opposition following accidents (Three Mile Island 1979, Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011)
  • 1Very complex, expensive technology needing an advanced industrial infrastructure
  • 1Concerns about links between civil nuclear materials and weapons / terrorism

  • 1Neutron bombardment in the reactor converts U-238 to fissile plutonium-239 (via beta decays)

  • 2Disadvantages: thorium-232 is not fissile so must be bred into uranium-233 (slow breeding rate); U-233 is an alpha emitter so very hazardous to handle; less developed technology with high development costs

Indicative content - credit any of the following (and other relevant, accurate points):
  • Control of greenhouse gases: energy conservation (transport, buildings, industry), renewable and nuclear energy replacing fossil fuels, CCS, reduced deforestation and afforestation (carbon sequestration), methane capture from landfill / digesters, HFC controls, changes in agriculture
  • Geoengineering: white roofs, ocean fertilisation, solar shades - largely untried, possible unpredictable consequences
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement - targets, but non-binding / uneven participation; carbon trading and taxes; comparison of per capita carbon footprints between countries
  • Evidence of limited success: global CO2 concentrations still rising (about 280 ppm pre-industrial to over 410 ppm); temperatures up about 1 °C; emissions in some developed countries falling but rising in developing economies
  • Reasons for difficulty: fossil fuels underpin economies, need for major lifestyle change, cost, time delays and natural fluctuations masking effects, political disagreement, developing countries' right to develop
  • Adaptation strategies (flood defences, drainage, managed retreat, infrastructure changes) reduce impacts but do not address the cause
  • Comparison with the Montreal Protocol's success for ozone
  • Judgement: partial success - technology exists and some regional reductions achieved, but global strategies have not yet stopped the rise; tipping points make urgency greater
