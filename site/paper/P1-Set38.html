Question 110 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 99-100, 105-108 · CC-09

A low-lying coastal town in eastern England is at risk of flooding from rising sea level and more intense rainfall.

[3 marks]01.1Describe three ways the town could adapt to the increased flood risk.
[2 marks]01.2Explain the circumstances in which managed retreat may be chosen instead of coastal defences.
[2 marks]01.3Describe two impacts of higher temperatures on transport infrastructure.
[3 marks]01.4Explain how carbon capture and storage (CCS) could reduce CO2 emissions from a fossil fuel power station, and give one limitation.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Raise river banks / build sea walls, flood barriers or wave screens
  • Permeable urban surfaces (gravel, soil) instead of concrete / tarmac to slow runoff
  • River flow management: upstream dams / low soil dams / afforestation / maintained flood plains to delay runoff
  • Raised buildings on stilts or floating houses
  • Pumping rainwater from land below river level
  • Managed retreat - abandoning land where protection costs exceed value (eg Happisburgh)
/ 3
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Where the cost of flood prevention is greater than the value of what is protected, or defences would be ineffective
  • Land is abandoned as water levels rise / coast erodes; defences used only where land is very important
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Road surfaces deform as tar melts - need tarmac with a higher melting point
  • Rail track expands and buckles above the temperature it was pre-stretched for (27 °C in UK) - relay with higher pre-stress
  • Bridge damage from high river flows / debris; landslides on deforested slopes
/ 2
01.4 (3 marks)
  • CO2 is captured from the flue gases (or carbon removed from the fuel before combustion)
  • Transported by pipeline / ship / tanker and stored in depleted oil or gas fields, aquifers, or used for secondary oil recovery
  • Limitation: developmental / expensive, uses energy (reducing efficiency), risk of leakage, limited storage sites
/ 3

02.1 (1 marks)
  • The length of time a pollutant remains in the environment before it is broken down / degraded (chemically or biologically)

Question 310 marks · Noise pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.13 · Genn pp. 287 · NOISE-02
[3 marks]03.1Describe three effects of noise on human health other than hearing damage.

03.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:

  • Communication problems / educational underperformance (eg schools under flight paths)
  • Sleep disturbance
/ 3

Question 410 marks · The atmosphere: energy processes (UV, IR, visible light) · spec 3.2.1.1 · Genn pp. 86-89 · ATM-01

Table 1 shows the composition of the dry atmosphere. Figure 1 shows the changes in carbon dioxide concentration measured at a site in the northern hemisphere over one year.

Table 1
GasPercentage by volume / %
Nitrogen78
Oxygen21
Argon and other rare gases0.9
Carbon dioxide0.04

Figure 1
[4 marks]04.1Describe and explain the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]04.2Explain why the atmosphere is described as being in a state of dynamic equilibrium.
[2 marks]04.3Give two ways in which the atmosphere supports life other than by providing gases for biological molecules.
[2 marks]04.4Name the two layers of the atmosphere most affected by human activities.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • CO2 rises from about 412 ppm in January to a peak of 416 ppm in May, falls to a minimum of 407 ppm in September, then rises again
  • During spring / summer, photosynthesis by plants (growing season) removes CO2 faster than respiration releases it, so the concentration falls
  • In autumn / winter photosynthesis is reduced (leaf fall) but respiration and decomposition continue, so CO2 rises
  • The atmosphere is in dynamic equilibrium: the concentration fluctuates around a mean; more land / vegetation in the northern hemisphere makes the cycle large
/ 4
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Natural processes that add gases (respiration, combustion, volcanoes, denitrification) are balanced by processes that remove them (photosynthesis, dissolving, nitrogen fixation)
  • So the average composition stays the same over long timescales although the rates vary and concentrations fluctuate around a mean
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Absorbs harmful UV / solar wind radiation in the stratosphere (ozone layer)
  • Greenhouse gases delay the escape of infrared, keeping the surface warm (33 °C warmer than without)
  • Atmospheric pressure allows liquid water to exist
  • Distributes heat from the tropics to higher latitudes by winds
  • Transports water vapour to areas that would otherwise be dry
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Troposphere
  • Stratosphere
/ 2

Question 610 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1-3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 410-413, 417 · RM-10
[3 marks]06.1Describe how soil texture is determined using a stack of sieves.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why a soil auger gives a more representative sample than a trowel.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why the organic matter content will be overestimated if a soil sample is heated above 550 °C.
[3 marks]06.4Describe how a turbidimeter measures water turbidity and explain how quantitative results are obtained.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Sieves assembled with the coarsest mesh on top; a dried, crushed soil sample is placed in the top sieve
  • Stack shaken (about 2 minutes) until no more particles pass; each fraction weighed
  • Percentages of sand (0.02-2 mm), silt (0.002-0.02 mm) and clay (base pan, below 0.002 mm) calculated; material over 2 mm (stones, twigs) is not soil; plotted on a soil triangle
/ 3
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Auger collects a core of known cross-sectional area and depth
  • So samples from different sites are directly comparable (also needed for bulk density = dry mass / volume)
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Minerals such as calcium carbonate break down releasing carbon dioxide
  • So mass is lost that is not organic matter
/ 2
06.4 (3 marks)
  • A beam of light passes through the sample in a dark chamber; a light sensor at 90° measures light scattered by suspended particles - more turbid, more scatter
  • Standardise bulb brightness / colour, distance and container
  • A calibration curve is produced using samples with known amounts of suspended matter so readings convert to concentrations; Secchi discs / turbidity bottles give only semi-quantitative results
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 410-413, 417; AQA spec 3.7.2.1-3.7.2.2.

Question 810 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 239-240 · PPRO-02
[3 marks]08.1Explain the difference between a mutagen, a carcinogen and a teratogen.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why a birth abnormality caused by a teratogen cannot be inherited by future generations, whereas one caused by a mutagen may be.
[2 marks]08.3Distinguish between somatic and gonadic effects of a mutagen.
[3 marks]08.4Explain what is meant by synergism and give an example.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Mutagen: an agent that changes the chemical structure / base sequence of DNA (causes mutations), eg ionising radiation, UV, PCBs, cadmium, asbestos
  • Carcinogen: a mutagen that causes cancer / uncontrolled cell division producing a tumour
  • Teratogen: an agent that causes birth abnormalities by preventing normal gene expression (inhibiting proteins / enzymes) without changing DNA structure, eg mercury, 2,4,5-T
/ 3
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Teratogens do not change the DNA structure, so the abnormality is not in the genes and cannot be passed on
  • Mutagens change DNA; if the mutation is in a gamete / egg / sperm (gonadic effect) it can be inherited
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Somatic: mutation in a general body cell, affecting the individual only (eg cancer / tumour)
  • Gonadic: mutation in cells of the ovaries or testes / gametes / embryo, which may cause abnormalities in offspring
/ 2
08.4 (3 marks)
  • Two or more pollutants whose effects interact to produce a different (usually more serious) effect
  • The pollutants themselves do not react to form a new pollutant (that would be a secondary pollutant)
  • Example: ozone damages leaf cuticles so sulfur dioxide can cause more damage to the exposed cells; smoke and sulfur dioxide in smogs; neonicotinoids with fungicides
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 239-240; AQA spec 3.4.1.
Question 910 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 135-136 · MIN-02

Figure 1 shows the mass of copper contained in the Earth's crust at different ore purities, illustrating Lasky's principle.

Figure 1
[1 mark]09.1State Lasky's principle.
[1 mark]09.2Use Figure 1 to calculate how many times more copper is contained in deposits of 0.25-0.5% purity than in deposits above 2.0%.
[3 marks]09.3Explain the difference between the reserves and the resource of a mineral.
[2 marks]09.4Explain, using Lasky's principle, why the ability to exploit slightly lower grade ores greatly increases reserves.
[3 marks]09.5Explain why the main problem for future mineral supplies is not the quantity that exists in the crust.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (1 marks)
  • As the (linear) purity / grade of a mineral deposit decreases, the amount of the mineral present increases exponentially / logarithmically
/ 1
09.2 (1 marks)
  • 2400 / 40 = 60 times
/ 1
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Reserves: the amount that can be exploited now, economically, with existing technology (deposits above the cut-off ore grade)
  • Resource: all the material that could be exploited technically and economically now or in the future (with realistic price rises or new technology), including deep, low-grade or protected deposits
  • The resource is finite but the reserves change: they rise if prices rise or technology improves, and fall if prices fall
/ 3
09.4 (2 marks)
  • Lowering the cut-off ore grade includes deposits of lower purity in the reserves
  • Because the amount of material rises exponentially as purity falls, a small drop in COOG adds a very large mass (eg 0.5-1.0% has 4x the copper of 1.0-2.0%)
/ 2
09.5 (3 marks)
  • The total quantity (stock / resource base) is far greater than could ever be used
  • But most is at purities too low or in locations too inaccessible for current economic exploitation
  • The challenge is developing technologies (low-energy extraction such as bioleaching, phytomining; better exploration) that make low-grade deposits exploitable; energy needs rise rapidly as ore grade falls
/ 3

Question 1010 marks · Fluctuations in supply/demand & energy storage · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 217-224 · EST-06
[9 marks]10.1Explain why the development of energy storage technologies is essential if renewable energy resources are to provide most of a country's electricity.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by peak shaving.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Renewables are intermittent (solar at night, calm days, slack tides) and some unpredictable; demand fluctuates daily, weekly, seasonally and short-term; supply and demand rarely match
  • Without storage, surplus is wasted and shortages must be met by fossil-fuel peaking plants; large-scale electricity storage not yet possible directly so energy must be converted
  • Pumped-storage HEP: proven, rapid response, peak shaving, but limited sites
  • Batteries: lithium-ion for grid and vehicles; factors affecting viability; V2G
  • Hydrogen: electrolysis, storage methods, fuel cells, hydrogen economy, P2G into the gas grid; high energy density enables vehicle use
  • Compressed air with heat recovery; thermal storage (molten salt for CSP, high-volume stores, thermal mass); flywheels; supercapacitors
  • Grid management / ICT and diversity of resources (wind + solar + tidal + biofuel) reduce but do not remove the need for storage; energy conservation reduces peaks
  • Evaluation of efficiency losses, costs, resource limits (lithium), and which technologies suit short-term vs inter-seasonal storage; judgement
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Storing surplus energy at times of low demand / high supply to meet later peaks in demand, so peak generating capacity can be reduced
/ 1
