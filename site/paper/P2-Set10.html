Question 110 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 78-81 · LP-05

Figure 1 shows the population of red deer on an island after they were introduced in year 0. No predators are present.

Figure 1
[4 marks]01.1Name the shape of the growth curve and explain the changes in growth rate shown.
[2 marks]01.2Explain the difference between density-dependent and density-independent factors, with an example of each.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why the deer population on the island may need to be culled even though it appears stable.
[2 marks]01.4Explain how predator and prey populations regulate each other.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Sigmoidal (S-shaped) growth curve
  • Slow start (few breeding individuals), then rapid / exponential growth (years 4-10) as abundant food and space mean birth rate far exceeds death rate
  • Growth slows as density-dependent factors (intra-species competition for food, disease) raise the death rate
  • Population stabilises / fluctuates around about 920-950 - the carrying capacity - where birth rate equals death rate
/ 4
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Density-dependent: effect on survival increases with population density - food shortage / intra-species competition, disease spread, predation
  • Density-independent: chance of survival unaffected by density - drought, flood, volcanic eruption, severe winter
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Without wolves (natural predators removed) the population is regulated only by starvation / disease at a high density
  • At this density deer over-graze, preventing tree regeneration and damaging habitats / other species; culling keeps numbers below the damaging level (as in Scotland)
/ 2
01.4 (2 marks)
  • Rising prey numbers give predators more food so predator numbers rise; high predation then reduces the prey population
  • Food shortage reduces predators, allowing prey to recover - cyclic fluctuations with predators lagging behind prey
/ 2

Question 210 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 66-67 · HAB-10
[3 marks]02.1Describe the ecological features of mangroves and the adaptations of mangrove trees.
[4 marks]02.2Explain four reasons why mangroves are important.
[3 marks]02.3Describe the main threats to mangroves and the conservation efforts used.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Tropical intertidal coastal forests of halophytic (salt-tolerant) trees in saline, oxygen-deficient (anaerobic) mud
  • Prop / aerial roots for support in soft sediment and for gas exchange (pneumatophores); salt excretion
  • Seeds germinate on the parent tree and float, rooting where they touch sand - rapid natural regeneration
/ 3
02.2 (4 marks)
  • Coastal erosion / storm protection: roots and vegetation absorb wave energy (least tsunami damage in 2004 where mangroves were intact)
  • Fisheries: nursery grounds where dense roots protect young fish that later live on reefs / open sea
  • Protection of coral reefs: slow river water so suspended solids settle and nutrients are absorbed before reaching reefs
  • Resources: timber, fuel; medicines (antimicrobials against MRSA, cyanobacterium for lung cancer); high biodiversity (crabs, shrimps, sponges, birds)
/ 4
02.3 (3 marks)
  • Clearance for aquaculture (shrimp ponds), urban development, ports, tourism; pollution; sea level rise / climate change; destruction of coral reefs that shelter the coast
  • Legal protection / designated areas (eg Sri Lanka protected all remaining mangroves in 2015)
  • Replanting and natural regeneration; control of damaging activities; Ramsar sites
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 66-67; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 310 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 357-358 · AQUA-04
[4 marks]03.1Describe a polyculture system using several species of carp and explain how it increases total productivity.
[4 marks]03.2Explain how integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) and aquaponics reduce pollution while increasing food production.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why tuna aquaculture cannot yet replace tuna fishing.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Silver carp eat phytoplankton, bighead carp zooplankton, grass carp and tilapia vegetation, common carp bottom-feed on molluscs / insects, catfish or perch eat small fish
  • Species occupy different niches / trophic levels so they do not compete and all food sources are used
  • Bottom feeders re-suspend nutrients boosting plankton / plant growth
  • Predators control over-breeding species (tilapia) so remaining fish grow larger; total yield exceeds any single species
/ 4
03.2 (4 marks)
  • IMTA: fed species (salmon, shrimp) alongside inorganic extractive species (seaweeds absorbing ammonia and phosphate) and organic extractive species (filter-feeding mussels eating waste particles / plankton)
  • Wastes of one species become nutrients for another so less pollution and extra harvests
  • Aquaponics: aquaculture water rich in nutrients / organic matter feeds hydroponic salad crops (lettuce, herbs, watercress)
  • Plants remove the nutrients that would cause deoxygenation and cleaned water returns to the fish (tilapia, carp, catfish); rice-fish paddies similar
/ 4
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Bluefin tuna do not breed well in captivity so young fish are caught from the wild and fattened in sea cages
  • Production still depends on (and removes juveniles from) the wild population; carnivores need large fish inputs
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 357-358; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.

[2 marks]04.1Calculate the energy ratio (output / input) for each system.
[3 marks]04.2Use your answers to explain the difference between productivity and efficiency.
[3 marks]04.3Explain what is meant by an energy subsidy and give three examples.
[2 marks]04.4Explain why spreading the same total inputs over a larger area would increase total food production.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (2 marks)
  • Extensive cereal 5.0; intensive cereal 3.0
  • Intensive beef 0.1
/ 2
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Intensive cereal is most productive per hectare (60 GJ) but less efficient (3.0) than extensive (5.0)
  • Each extra unit of input gives a smaller extra yield (law of diminishing returns)
  • Beef has very low productivity and efficiency because energy is lost at an extra trophic level
/ 3
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Any input that increases productivity but requires the use of energy (usually fossil fuel)
  • Manufacture of nitrate fertiliser (Haber process) and pesticides; fuel for machinery; pumping irrigation water; grain drying; food processing and transport; embodied energy of machinery
/ 3
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Because of diminishing returns, the first units of input on unfertilised land give the largest yield increases
  • Moving inputs from intensively farmed land (where extra input adds little) to extensively farmed land gives a greater total yield - though land, cost and distribution limit this
/ 2

Question 510 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 18-19 · BIOV-03
[6 marks]05.1Explain how living organisms provide each of the following ecosystem services.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why Darwin's orchid of Madagascar depends on a single moth species, and why such specialisation is both an advantage and a risk.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why elephants are particularly important seed dispersers.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (6 marks)
  • Photosynthesis and aerobic respiration balance O2 and CO2 in a dynamic equilibrium; evapotranspiration adds water vapour forming clouds and rain; microbes drive the nitrogen and other biogeochemical cycles so wastes do not accumulate and nutrients are recycled
  • Detritivores, fungi and bacteria decompose dead organic matter releasing nutrients and forming humus that binds soil; organic acids weather rock forming new soil; roots bind soil, reducing erosion and regulating water flow / flooding
  • Species provide food, pollination (bees, moths, birds, bats - more reliable than wind, especially in forests), seed dispersal (animals carry larger seeds to suitable sites, faeces as fertiliser, gut passage stimulates germination) and habitat provision (trees as nest sites and canopy microclimate; mollusc shells for hermit crabs)
/ 6
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Its nectar is at the end of a 30 cm tube reached only by the long tongue of the sphinx moth, which carries pollen only between orchids of the same species
  • Advantage: no pollen wasted on other species and the moth has an exclusive food source; risk: if either species declines the other cannot survive
/ 2
05.3 (2 marks)
  • They are not ruminants so seeds are not destroyed by repeated chewing / pass through intact; they eat the fruit of most tree species
  • Not territorial so they carry seeds over large distances / areas
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 18-19; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.3.

Question 810 marks · Dynamic equilibria, feedback & tipping points · spec 3.6.1 · Genn pp. 371-372 · SUS-02
[3 marks]08.1Explain why natural systems regulated by negative feedback rarely reach tipping points, and give an example of a natural tipping point in the geological past.
[4 marks]08.2Explain why diverse systems are more resistant to change than simple systems, using a natural and a human example.
[3 marks]08.3Give three positive feedback mechanisms by which human-induced warming may become self-sustaining.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Negative feedback responds to any change by re-establishing the previous equilibrium
  • Only large unusual events overwhelm the feedback so a new equilibrium forms
  • Example: methane hydrate release at the end of the Permian causing climate change and mass extinction
/ 3
08.2 (4 marks)
  • In a diverse system each component is relatively less important so the loss / change of one has a small effect; alternative food sources and relationships buffer the change
  • Natural: coral reefs and tropical rainforests with complex food webs are stable; simple polar / desert systems fluctuate
  • Human: agroecosystems focus on a few crops and remove detritivores, pollinators and predators so are vulnerable to pests, disease, weather
  • Diverse technical systems: a society using a range of renewable energy resources is more secure than one relying on a single source
/ 4
08.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Melting permafrost releasing methane
  • Reduced albedo as ice / snow melt
  • Methane hydrate release
  • Forest and peat fires
  • Faster soil decomposition
  • Ocean acidification reducing sequestration
  • Cirrus cloud formation
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 371-372; AQA spec 3.6.1.

09.1 (6 marks)
  • Demersal trawl: seabed species - cod, haddock, plaice, shrimps, scampi; chains / weights damage the seabed (mix aerobic and anaerobic layers, destroy sea fans, corals, seagrass); high by-catch in mixed fisheries; high fuel use

  • Drift nets are non-selective curtains near the surface catching whales, dolphins, turtles, sharks
/ 2
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Lost or discarded nets and traps continue to catch and kill organisms; dead animals act as bait attracting more
  • Biodegradable ropes / panels so lost traps fall apart; radio transmitters on traps so they can be recovered; net recovery schemes

Question 1010 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.1 · Genn pp. 375-379 · SUS-06
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the extent to which the principles of the circular economy can be applied to three of the following: water supplies, mineral supplies, waste management, pollution control, energy supplies, food production.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by industrial symbiosis.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Water: catchment management, conservation, metering, greywater, re-use of treated effluent, aquifer recharge, sustainable urban drainage - largely cyclical already; limits in arid regions
  • Minerals: exploiting low-grade ores with low-temperature processes (bioleaching), extending product lifespans, repair / re-use / repurpose / recycle, design for disassembly, reduced packaging; limits: dispersal, alloys, energy of collection
  • Waste management: repair, re-use, refurbish, recycle, composting and anaerobic digestion returning nutrients, minimal landfill, energy from residual waste; EU targets on packaging, batteries, WEEE
  • Pollution control: shift from post-production treatment to non-release - fuel desulfurisation not FGD, fuel cells / electric vehicles not combustion, industrial symbiosis (Kalundborg: waste heat, gypsum, fly ash, yeast used by neighbours); limits for legacy pollution
  • Energy: renewables, storage, energy density technologies, low-temperature manufacturing, CHP; limits of intermittency, transport fuels
  • Food: natural processes for nutrients (legumes, organic matter), pest control (IPM), soil biota, agri-environment schemes, IMTA aquaculture; tension with maximum yield for a growing population
  • Land uses supporting ecosystems: urban green infrastructure, gardens, permeable surfaces, habitat in redeveloped docks / lagoons
  • Judgement: high applicability in some sectors (water, waste), partial in others (minerals, energy for transport); needs policy support
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Locating industries so that the wastes of one (heat, gases, by-products) become the raw materials of another, mimicking natural cycles (eg Kalundborg eco-industrial park)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 375-379; AQA spec 3.6.4.1.
