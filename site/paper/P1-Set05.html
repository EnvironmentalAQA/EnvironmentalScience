Question 410 marks · Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus · spec 3.2.4.2 · Genn pp. 150-151 · BGC-02
[6 marks]04.1Explain how each of the following human activities changes the amount of carbon in the atmosphere.
[3 marks]04.2Give three anaerobic environments created by human activities that release methane.
[1 mark]04.3Explain how rising atmospheric CO2 causes ocean acidification.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (6 marks)
  • Ploughing: increases the oxygen supply to soil decomposers so aerobic decomposition of dead organic matter is faster; carbon in the soil DOM store falls and atmospheric CO2 rises
  • Draining: waterlogged anaerobic conditions preserved dead organic matter (peat) and produced methane slowly; drainage makes the soil aerobic so stored carbon is rapidly decomposed to CO2 (though methane release stops)
  • Marine pollution: kills phytoplankton so less dissolved CO2 is absorbed by photosynthesis and less carbon sinks to sediments in dead plankton; less CO2 then dissolves from the atmosphere to replace it, so atmospheric CO2 rises
/ 6

Any 3 from:
  • Rice paddy fields
  • Landfill sites
  • Anaerobic sediments in reservoirs
  • Livestock intestines (cattle)
  • Manure / slurry stores
/ 3
04.3 (1 marks)
  • More CO2 dissolves in the sea, forming carbonic acid which dissociates into hydrogen carbonate and hydrogen ions; more H+ lowers the pH

Question 510 marks · Heavy metals: lead, mercury · spec 3.4.3.2.11 · Genn pp. 276-277 · HM-02

In the 1950s a chemical factory at Minamata, Japan, discharged inorganic mercury compounds into a shallow bay. Thousands of local people who ate fish and shellfish from the bay suffered severe neurological damage and many babies were born with birth abnormalities.

[4 marks]05.1Explain how the low-toxicity inorganic mercury discharged into the bay came to cause such severe poisoning.
[3 marks]05.2Explain why methyl mercury caused both neurological damage in adults and birth abnormalities.
[3 marks]05.3Compare the toxicity of the three chemical forms of mercury.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (4 marks)
  • Inorganic mercury settled into anaerobic sediments where anaerobic microbes converted it to organic (methyl) mercury
  • Methyl mercury is liposoluble so it is absorbed easily and stored in fat / passes through cell membranes
  • It bioaccumulated in fish and shellfish and biomagnified along the food chain to reach much higher concentrations than in the water
  • Local people ate large amounts of fish (critical group) so received toxic doses
/ 4
05.2 (3 marks)
  • Liposoluble so it crosses the blood-brain barrier into the brain, inhibiting enzymes / nerve function (neurotoxin)
  • It crosses the placenta and harms the developing unborn baby
  • Mercury is a teratogen, preventing normal gene expression / development
/ 3
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Elemental (liquid) mercury: not easily absorbed through skin or gut, though vapour is absorbed in the lungs
  • Inorganic compounds (eg mercury oxide): moderately well absorbed in the gut
  • Organic compounds (eg methyl mercury): easily absorbed through skin, gut and lungs; liposoluble so most toxic and bioaccumulates
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 276-277; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.11.

Question 715 marks · Ozone depletion · spec 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 108-109 · OZ-01
[3 marks]07.1Describe how ozone is formed and destroyed naturally in the stratosphere.
[6 marks]07.2Explain the properties of CFCs that made them useful, and the properties that cause ozone depletion (the Rowland-Molina hypothesis).
[3 marks]07.3Explain why increased UVB reaching the Earth's surface is harmful to living organisms. Give three effects.
[1 mark]07.4State what is meant by an ozone hole.
[1 mark]07.5Give one reason why concentration in ppm is not used to measure ozone depletion.
[1 mark]07.6Give one reason why the first evidence for ozone depletion came from ground-based measurements.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • UV light splits diatomic oxygen (O2) into two monatomic oxygen atoms (O)
  • Monatomic oxygen reacts with diatomic oxygen to form ozone (O3)
  • UV light also splits ozone into O2 and O; the reactions form a dynamic equilibrium which absorbs UV (converting it to chemical energy / heat)
/ 3
07.2 (6 marks)
  • Useful: boiling points near ambient temperature so easily liquefied under low pressure (aerosol propellants, fridge / air-conditioning coolants); dissolve grease but do not damage electrical components (solvents); non-flammable; non-toxic
  • Persistence: chemically stable / unreactive in the troposphere so they remain long enough to be carried up to the stratosphere
  • Dissociation by UV: in the stratosphere higher UV levels break the carbon-chlorine bonds releasing chlorine free radicals
  • Chlorine reacts with monatomic oxygen (and ozone), preventing ozone formation; chlorine is regenerated so one atom destroys many ozone molecules; bromine / iodine act similarly
/ 6
07.3 (3 marks)
  • UVB energy is absorbed by cells and breaks up biological molecules / DNA causing mutations, skin cancer, cataracts, sunburn (human health)
  • Damage to leaf tissue and reduced photosynthesis / crop yields (plants)
  • Damage to marine algae, phytoplankton, corals and planktonic larvae (marine organisms), reducing productivity and food webs
/ 3
07.4 (1 marks)
  • An area where the total ozone column falls below 220 Dobson Units (normal is about 300 DU) - a level never recorded before CFC pollution
/ 1
07.5 (1 marks)
  • Concentration varies with altitude and the stratosphere is not normally sampled; Dobson Units measure the total column thickness instead
/ 1
07.6 (1 marks)
  • The British Antarctic Survey at Halley detected higher UV at ground level, showing ozone above had decreased (satellites above the stratosphere cannot measure downward UV directly)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-109; AQA spec 3.2.1.3.

Question 910 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 100-102 · CC-05
[3 marks]09.1Explain why it is difficult to be certain that a particular flood or heatwave is caused by anthropogenic climate change.
[2 marks]09.2Explain why historic temperature records collected in towns may overestimate global warming.
[5 marks]09.3Describe how each of the following sources of proxy data is used to estimate past climates and give one limitation.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
  • The climate naturally fluctuates (solar output, Earth's orbit) so extreme events can occur without human influence
  • An individual event, or even several, does not show a trend; the change may be in the frequency of events which can only be assessed years later
  • Changes occur at different time and spatial scales (local storm vs global trend); interconnected systems make attribution complex
/ 3
09.2 (2 marks)
  • Towns have a heat-island effect (heat emissions, dark surfaces absorbing light)
  • As the town grows around the weather station the warming recorded is partly local, not global; older equipment / sparse global coverage also reduce reliability
/ 2
09.3 (5 marks)
  • Tree rings: width of each annual ring shows the growth rate, indicating temperature / rainfall in that year; rings easily dated by counting; limitation: growth also affected by other factors (water, pests, shading), only covers the life of the tree / limited regions
  • Ice cores: annual snow layers compact into ice; air bubbles trapped in the ice give the CO2 (and methane) concentration when trapped; oxygen isotope ratios indicate temperature; up to 800 000 years (Dome C); limitation: deep layers compressed / less distinct, dating needs radio-isotope analysis and volcanic ash layers; only polar regions
  • Others accepted: coral growth rings; pollen in lake sediments dated by radiocarbon
/ 5
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 100-102; AQA spec 3.2.1.2.
Question 1015 marks · The atmosphere: energy processes (UV, IR, visible light) · spec 3.2.1.1, 3.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 86-89, 108-113 · ATM-06

Table 1 shows the three forms of oxygen found in the stratosphere.

Table 1
Form of oxygenNumber of atoms per molecule
Monatomic oxygen (O)1
Diatomic oxygen (O2)2
Triatomic oxygen / ozone (O3)3
[3 marks]10.1Explain how the three forms of oxygen protect life at the surface from ultraviolet radiation.
[2 marks]10.2Give two other ways in which the atmosphere supports life.
[1 mark]10.3Explain why the atmosphere is thermally stratified into layers.
[9 marks]10.4The troposphere and stratosphere are the layers most affected by human activities. Discuss how human activities alter the energy processes in these two layers and evaluate the difficulties of predicting the consequences.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (3 marks)
  • Together they form a dispersed layer in the stratosphere (the ozone layer / ozonosphere)
  • They absorb ultraviolet light, which drives a dynamic equilibrium of reactions that form and destroy ozone
  • The UV energy is converted to chemical / heat energy so most UV that passed the upper atmosphere never reaches the surface
/ 3
10.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Provides carbon, oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen (as N2, O2, CO2, H2O) for biological molecules
  • Delays the escape of infrared energy, keeping the surface warm
  • Distributes heat and water vapour by winds / ocean currents
  • Provides atmospheric pressure so liquid water can exist
  • Deflects damaging radiation of the solar wind in the upper atmosphere
/ 2
10.3 (1 marks)
  • Energy is absorbed at different altitudes (eg UV absorbed in the stratosphere, surface heating warms the troposphere) so temperature gradients change with altitude, producing layers
/ 1
10.4 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Troposphere: enhanced greenhouse effect - anthropogenic CO2 (fossil fuels, deforestation), methane (rice, livestock, landfill), N2O, CFCs, tropospheric ozone; more infrared absorbed and re-emitted; aerosols and smoke increase albedo / scatter light; land-use change alters surface albedo and evapotranspiration
  • Stratosphere: CFCs / halons / HCFCs release chlorine and bromine which catalyse ozone destruction, shifting the O / O2 / O3 equilibrium; less UV absorbed in the stratosphere, more reaches the surface; stratospheric cooling
  • Consequences: changes to climate, ocean currents (thermohaline), hydrological cycle, sea level, species distributions; increased UV damage to organisms
  • Difficulties of prediction: linked processes where one change alters others; feedback (positive and negative) and tipping points; time lags (ocean heat capacity, long residence times of CFCs and CO2); natural variability (solar output, volcanoes, Milankovitch cycles); incomplete understanding of atmospheric processes; limited historical data so proxies needed; models with different assumptions
  • Evaluation: some effects well understood (greenhouse physics, ozone chemistry proven, Montreal Protocol success) but scale and timing of regional impacts uncertain; precautionary principle
  • Justified conclusion
/ 9

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss how knowledge of the properties of energy resources can be used to reduce the severity of their impact on the environment.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Properties: energy density, renewability, abundance, location, intermittency / predictability, storage, transport, conversions, technology level, environmental impacts
  • Fossil fuels: choosing lower-impact fuels (gas over coal), desulfurisation, CCS, efficient combustion, siting power stations near cooling water and coal ports; new extraction technologies (directional drilling, secondary / tertiary recovery) and their impacts (fracking, tar sands)

  • Energy conservation reduces demand: transport, buildings, industry (CHP, heat exchangers)
  • Impacts before and during use; life-cycle / embodied energy
  • Evaluation: no resource is impact-free; knowledge allows the best mix and mitigation; judgement
