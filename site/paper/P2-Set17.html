Question 110 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-60 · HAB-06
[4 marks]01.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows reefs to support a high biomass in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[3 marks]01.2Describe the abiotic conditions needed by reef-building corals and explain why these make reefs vulnerable.
[3 marks]01.3Explain three ways in which coral reefs are important to humans.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside polyp cells (up to 30 000 per mm2)
  • They use the polyp's CO2 and excretory wastes (nitrogen, phosphorus) for photosynthesis and growth, so nutrients are recycled efficiently
  • They supply the polyp with glucose and oxygen - over 95% of its food energy; the polyp provides a protected habitat and nutrients
  • Polyps also trap plankton with cilia and stinging nematocysts; surplus CO2 is used to build the calcium carbonate skeleton
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • High light (shallow, clear water) for the algae; warm stable temperatures (about 18-30 °C); low turbidity; constant salinity; well-oxygenated water
  • Found only where all these coincide (about 1% of ocean area)
  • Small changes (warming, sediment, freshwater runoff) move conditions outside the narrow range of tolerance
/ 3
01.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Fisheries: support over a quarter of marine species including fish, lobsters, conch
  • Erosion protection: complex structure dissipates wave energy protecting low coral islands; coral sand builds shores
  • Medicinal discoveries: AZT and Ara-C from sponges, dolastatin from sea hares, bryostatin from Bugula, trabectedin from sea squirts, secosteroids, coral for bone grafts
  • Climate control: carbonate skeletons sequester CO2
  • Tourism (diving, snorkelling)
/ 3

Question 210 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.1 · Genn pp. 28-29 · PRI-02
[8 marks]02.1Explain the meaning of each of the following terms and explain why such species are given conservation priority.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why the degree of population dispersal is considered when setting priorities.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (8 marks)
  • EDGE: Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered - few close relatives, often the last species of their genus / family (Bactrian camel, pygmy hippo, northern bald ibis); loss would remove a unique branch of evolution / genes
  • Endemic: found in only one area (often islands - Galapagos, Madagascar, Seychelles: Aldabra giant tortoise, red ruffed lemur); if lost locally there is no population elsewhere to recolonise
  • Keystone: disproportionately important for the structure of the community relative to its abundance - predation of dominant species, seed dispersal, habitat creation (jaguar, forest elephants keeping paths and water holes open, wolves, beavers); losing them causes loss of many other species
  • Flagship: high public profile (tiger, panda, elephant, orang-utan) raising funds and support that also protects the whole habitat and less charismatic species (fungi, bats, moths)
/ 8
02.2 (2 marks)
  • A fragmented population may consist of isolated groups each too small to be viable (resources, inbreeding) even if total numbers are large
  • A species confined to a single area (eg bonobos in one country) is vulnerable to any local change / conflict, unlike chimpanzees spread across many countries
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 28-29; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.1.
Question 310 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 308-310, 313 · AGR-02
[3 marks]03.1Explain why a moderate water shortage reduces crop productivity even though the plants survive.
[4 marks]03.2Explain the problems caused by waterlogged soils and describe two ways farmers reduce soil water levels.
[2 marks]03.3Explain how irrigating with groundwater can cause soil salinisation and how this harms crops.
[1 mark]03.4State how a farmer could raise the pH of an acidic soil and explain why a low pH reduces fertility.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • Plants close their stomata to reduce transpiration losses
  • Closed stomata stop the absorption of carbon dioxide so photosynthesis and growth stop
  • Less water for nutrient absorption / transport and cell turgidity; severe shortage dehydrates cells and inhibits biochemical reactions
/ 3
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Anaerobic soil favours denitrifying bacteria and inhibits nitrifying bacteria so nitrate is lost faster than it forms; higher risk of fungal disease; root respiration inhibited
  • Drainage ditches or pipes
  • Deep ploughing; avoiding compaction by machinery / livestock; adding organic matter to encourage worms
/ 4
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Groundwater often contains dissolved salts; when the water evaporates from the soil the salts are left behind and accumulate
  • High soil salinity causes osmotic dehydration of roots / crop death; washing out with extra water uses more water and salinises rivers
/ 2
03.4 (1 marks)
  • Add crushed lime (calcium carbonate / hydroxide); low pH increases leaching, inhibits nutrient uptake and mobilises toxic aluminium and lead
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 308-310, 313; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.

04.1 (4 marks)
  • k-selected: very slow growth, does not breed until 30, few eggs, so the population cannot replace losses
  • Fish reach catchable size (20) before they breed (30) so fishing can remove the entire future breeding population
  • Deep-water aggregations easily located and caught by trawling

/ 4
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Herring breed at 2 years - the same age they become catchable - so there is always a breeding population and many young
  • Cod are catchable at 2 but do not mature until 4, so heavy fishing removes most fish before they breed
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Larger numbers of smaller / younger fish are caught, or bigger boats, nets and sonar increase catch effort
  • Masks the decline of the breeding population until a sudden collapse
/ 2
04.4 (2 marks)
  • Neither is a target species but both are caught in demersal trawls; skate now extinct in the Baltic and critically endangered elsewhere
  • Very late maturity (skate 11 years, Greenland shark 100-150 years, living over 400) and few young mean even low by-catch mortality exceeds replacement

Question 610 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3 · Genn pp. 36-37 · CBR-03
[3 marks]06.1Explain the difference between hard and soft release and state the types of species for which each is suitable.
[5 marks]06.2Describe five features of a suitable release site.
[2 marks]06.3Explain the importance of post-release monitoring.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (3 marks)
  • Hard release: individuals released with no post-release support - suitable for species whose behaviour is instinctive and need no learned skills (insects, fish, amphibians, reptiles)
  • Soft release: gradual release into larger areas with food provided while they learn to find food, avoid predators - needed for mammals and birds
  • Problems faced: finding / recognising food and poisonous foods, hunting skills (captive-born predators never learned), avoiding predators, acceptance into wild social groups
/ 3
06.2 (5 marks)
Any 5 from:
  • Large enough suitable habitat for a viable population
  • Reliable food supply
  • Low predation risk / original threat removed
  • Suitable breeding sites
  • Water
  • Support of the local human population
  • Official / legal protection of habitat and species
/ 5
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Tracking (radio / GPS tags, rings) shows survival, dispersal and breeding of released animals so the success of the project can be assessed
  • Identifies causes of failure so future releases can be improved / extra support given

Question 710 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.1 · Genn pp. 374-375 · SUS-05
[6 marks]07.1Describe the main principles of the circular economy.
[2 marks]07.2Explain what is meant by 'optimum production rather than maximum production', using a comparison between a natural ecosystem and an agroecosystem.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why international cooperation (eg through the EU or UN) is needed to implement circular economy initiatives.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Cycling of materials (like biogeochemical cycles) - recycling, re-use
  • Energy from renewable sources
  • Human activities should support ecosystems / conserve biodiversity
  • Separation of technical and biological materials; connected systems where the waste of one process feeds another (industrial symbiosis)
  • Diverse systems are more resistant to change
  • Design of products for extended use and end-of-life re-use; optimum rather than maximum production; technologies that improve efficiency and enable dismantling

  • Natural systems over-produce so the surplus supports the processes the system relies on: plant products feed pollinators, seed dispersers, decomposers, mycorrhizae
  • Agroecosystems aim for maximum harvested yield, removing the surplus that would maintain soil biota, pollinators and pest predators, so they need artificial inputs
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)

  • A common approach gives a level playing field, coordinated monitoring and support for less successful countries

Table 1

[3 marks]09.1Explain why commercial crop varieties often lack the characteristics shown in Table 1.
[3 marks]09.2Explain what is meant by a Vavilov centre (centre of diversity) and why these areas are conservation priorities.
[3 marks]09.3Explain why a large population does not necessarily have a large gene pool, and why a small gene pool is a problem.
[1 mark]09.4Give one wild plant species with potential as a new commercial food crop and the characteristic that makes it valuable.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
  • Domesticated crops were bred from a small number of original plants so they have a small gene pool / low genetic diversity
  • Selective breeding for yield, appearance or shelf life may have lost other useful genes (eg taste, disease resistance)
  • Genetically similar plants share the same susceptibilities so a new disease or environmental change can affect the whole crop
/ 3
09.2 (3 marks)
  • A region with a high concentration of the wild relatives of important crops - usually where the crop was first domesticated (eg wheat / barley in the Middle East; potato, tomato in South America; rice in India; maize in Central America)
  • They hold the largest gene pools / most genetic variety for future crop breeding (disease resistance, drought and salt tolerance, yield)
  • Many are in regions where environmental degradation / habitat loss threatens the wild plants
/ 3
09.3 (3 marks)
  • A large population may all descend from a few closely related individuals so they are genetically similar
  • Similar adaptations mean all are susceptible to the same change (disease, climate)
  • Greater risk of inbreeding so disadvantageous recessive genes are expressed (eg amelanism, poor muscle development in lion cubs); so species should be protected across their whole range to retain regional genes
/ 3
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Morama bean (drought resistant, soya-like protein); yeheb tree (drought resistant nuts, poor soils); potato bean (high protein); perennial maize / Kernza (no annual replanting, less ploughing / erosion); salt-tolerant spinach relatives
/ 1
