Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Fishing's impacts go beyond depleting target species: k-selected species that breed late and produce few young collapse and recover slowly; by-catch of immature target fish, over-quota fish and non-commercial species is thrown back dead, with drift nets killing whales, dolphins and turtles, longlines drowning albatrosses and pair trawls porpoises; lost gear keeps ghost fishing; demersal trawls destroy sea fans, corals and seagrass; and removing any species alters its competitors, prey and predators.

Catch quotas limit the weight landed and work well for single-species pelagic shoals where fishing stops when the quota is reached, but in mixed demersal fisheries over-quota fish are discarded dead, so the quota does not protect them. Gear design tackles by-catch directly: mesh sizes and square-mesh escape panels let small fish out because, unlike diagonal mesh, they do not close under drag; turtle exclusion devices, acoustic pingers for dolphins, curved hooks, decoys, sinkers and night setting protect turtles, cetaceans and albatrosses; biodegradable and radio-tracked traps limit ghost fishing. Restricting effort - boat size, engine power, days at sea, decommissioning - reduces total pressure, and banning drift nets or demersal trawling on sensitive seabeds such as Rockall Bank protects habitats.

Effectiveness depends on enforcement at sea, which is difficult, on reliable stock data for quotas, on the species and fishery type, and on international cooperation because fish migrate; some measures cut catches or raise costs, and as local stocks fall fleets simply move further away. Gear modifications are the most effective per unit cost because they reduce damage without reducing legal catch; quotas and effort limits only work where they are enforced.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Fishing damages the environment by overfishing, catching by-catch such as dolphins and damaging the seabed with trawling. Quotas limit how many fish can be caught so stocks can recover. Bigger mesh sizes let small fish escape so they can grow and breed. Turtle exclusion devices let turtles escape from nets. Some fishing methods can be banned in sensitive areas. No-take zones let fish breed. These methods help but they are hard to enforce at sea and some fishermen ignore them.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 covers impacts and methods soundly but without the mechanisms (why square mesh works, why quotas fail in mixed fisheries).
  • Level 3 evaluates each method on its own terms and then compares them; Level 2's evaluation is a single general sentence.
  • Named examples (Rockall Bank, albatross measures, ghost-fishing controls) give Level 3 the range the top band expects.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Protected areas are the core of rainforest conservation. Korup National Park in Cameroon protects 1260 km2 with about 600 tree species, including the endemic Ancistrocladus korupensis whose alkaloids have anti-HIV and antimalarial activity; Brazil's Tumucumaque National Park covers 39 000 km2 and adjoins a reserve in French Guiana; the Rio Bravo area in Belize is funded by charities, companies, ecotourism and selective logging of high-value mahogany that does not damage the forest long term. Debt-for-nature swaps have cancelled Guatemalan and Brazilian debt in return for protection, CITES listing restricts trade in Honduras mahogany, the ITTO sets sustainable timber aims, ecotourism and community employment give local people an income from standing forest, and carbon sequestration payments are emerging.

Where they are enforced, protected areas work, but they face illegal logging, mining, poaching, road building and conflict, and ecotourism itself has damaged sites in Costa Rica and Malaysia. The deeper problem is that the drivers of deforestation - population growth and international demand for beef, soya, palm oil, timber and minerals - originate outside the forest, and climate change threatens even well-protected areas; the ITTO's aims are largely unmet and overall loss continues.

The most effective schemes are those that combine large protected areas with economic alternatives for local people, such as Rio Bravo, but they can only slow the loss unless demand in importing countries also falls.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (4 marks)

Rainforests are protected by national parks where logging is banned, for example in Brazil. Debt-for-nature swaps mean a country's debt is cancelled if it protects its forest. Ecotourism brings money from tourists so the forest is worth more standing than cut down. Sustainable logging only takes some trees. These efforts have saved some areas but deforestation is still happening because of illegal logging and farming, so they have only been partly effective.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 covers the main types of effort and a fair conclusion, but the question says 'describe' - Level 3 gives named examples with figures (Korup, Tumucumaque, Rio Bravo) as the mark scheme expects.
  • Level 3 explains why effectiveness is limited (drivers originate outside the forest, ITTO aims unmet, climate change) rather than just noting continued loss.
  • The judgement in Level 3 identifies what works best and what would be needed; Level 2 stops at 'partly effective'.

[25 marks]11.2Explain how the physical features of the Earth and the activities of living organisms have combined to create and maintain conditions suitable for life.
Indicative content

Indicative content - students are not expected to cover all of these:
  • Physical features: mass / gravity retaining an atmosphere and giving pressure for liquid water; distance from the Sun and the Goldilocks zone; speed of rotation reducing temperature extremes; tilt producing seasons; magnetic field deflecting the solar wind; the Moon stabilising the orbit and producing tides
  • Importance of liquid water: solvent, transport, temperature control, anomalous expansion, high specific heat capacity, habitats, absorption of UV before the ozone layer
  • Development of life: Archaea at vents; photosynthesis; oxygen reacting with iron then accumulating; ozone layer allowing colonisation of land; aerobic organisms
  • Life changing conditions: carbon sequestration offsetting solar brightening, biogeochemical cycles, transpiration and rainfall, regulation of atmospheric gases
  • Methods of studying past conditions: proxy data, isotope ratios, ice cores, Mg:Ca ratios; understanding of continental drift and ocean currents
  • Links to current threats (climate change, ozone depletion) and a justified conclusion about the interdependence of physical and biological factors
