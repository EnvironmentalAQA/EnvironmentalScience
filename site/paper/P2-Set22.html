[4 marks]03.1Use Table 1 to explain how natural processes differ from human industrial processes in their use of energy.
[3 marks]03.2Give three other natural processes driven by solar energy that human society depends on.
[3 marks]03.3Explain how the principle of low-temperature natural processes is being applied in human systems.

03.1 (4 marks)
  • Natural processes are driven by renewable, low energy-density solar energy captured by photosynthesis; human processes usually use high energy-density non-renewable fossil fuels
  • Natural processes occur at low temperatures because enzymes lower the activation energy of reactions; human processes need high temperatures and pressures
  • Natural processes produce no toxic wastes / their products are raw materials for other processes; industrial processes emit CO2 and NOx
  • So natural fixation is sustainable indefinitely with a low carbon footprint

  • Carbon cycle / photosynthesis
  • Atmospheric circulation / winds
  • Thermohaline circulation (density differences from heating / evaporation)
  • Decomposition
/ 3
03.3 (3 marks)
  • Bioleaching and phytomining extract metals at low temperature instead of high-temperature smelting
  • Anaerobic digestion and composting treat wastes; enzymes in industry (biological washing powders, biofuels from algae / microorganisms)
  • Heat pumps and solar thermal capture low-density heat; low-temperature manufacturing using biological polymers; fuel cells instead of combustion

Question 410 marks · Impacts of agriculture, social/political factors & sustainable agriculture · spec 3.5.1.3-3.5.1.4 · Genn pp. 329-333 · AGI-02
[3 marks]04.1Describe how agriculture alters the hydrological cycle.
[4 marks]04.2Explain how each of the following consumer choices can reduce the environmental impact of food production.
[3 marks]04.3Explain why post-war European governments introduced grants and guaranteed prices, and describe one environmental consequence.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (3 marks)
  • Irrigation depletes rivers and aquifers (eg Aral Sea, Ogallala); increases evaporation
  • Soil erosion and compaction reduce water retention and infiltration so runoff and river flow fluctuations increase
  • Evapotranspiration changes: increased in irrigated arid areas, decreased where forests were cleared
/ 3
04.2 (4 marks)
  • Seasonal / local: avoids energy for heating, lighting greenhouses out of season and long-distance transport (food miles) and the pollution this causes
  • Organic: uses natural processes (rotation, legumes, manure, biological control, predator habitats) instead of artificial fertilisers and pesticides, so less pollution, energy use and harm to non-target species; free-range and Fairtrade also accepted for welfare / social reasons
/ 4
04.3 (3 marks)
  • Food shortages after 1945 (dependence on US food aid); farmers could not afford loans to invest so grants funded machinery, drainage, hedgerow removal, liming, improved livestock
  • Guaranteed market / price support: government bought surpluses to hold prices up (and sold stores in poor years) giving farmers security to increase output
  • Consequences: loss of hedgerows and wetlands, intensification, pesticide and fertiliser pollution; later food surpluses (butter mountains) that could not be sold without harming LEDC producers
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 329-333; AQA spec 3.5.1.3-3.5.1.4.

  • Site selection (aspect, avoiding frost pockets); heated buildings and shelter for livestock
/ 3

  • Reducing heat loss (shelter, heated buildings) means less food energy is used for heat and more is available for growth

  • Long days increase milk production and egg laying; poultry grow best with short days; sheep mate as days shorten in autumn
  • Artificial lighting extends the growing season for greenhouse crops or simulates autumn in spring to give sheep a second mating / lambing season

Question 710 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2 · Genn pp. 7-8 · LIFE-02
[4 marks]07.1Explain four properties of water that make it essential for life.
[4 marks]07.2Describe how photosynthetic organisms changed the atmosphere and explain how this allowed life to colonise the land.
[2 marks]07.3Explain how carbon sequestration by early photoautotrophs helped maintain a suitable temperature on Earth.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • General physiological solvent: most biological reactions take place between substances dissolved in water
  • Transport medium in blood / sap carrying gases, sugars, amino acids, nutrients, wastes
  • High specific heat capacity moderates temperature changes; evaporation absorbs heat (cooling)
  • Anomalous expansion: ice floats so water bodies do not freeze solid; absorbs UV protecting early marine life; provides aquatic habitats
/ 4
07.2 (4 marks)
  • From about 2.7 billion years ago photosynthetic archaea / bacteria (then algae and plants) released oxygen; at first it reacted with dissolved iron (forming iron ore deposits) then built up in the oceans and atmosphere (from about 2.45 billion years ago)
  • Oxygen in the stratosphere absorbed UV, forming ozone (O3) in a dynamic equilibrium of O, O2 and O3
  • The ozone layer reduced UV reaching the surface so organisms could survive out of the water
  • Oxygen allowed aerobic respiration releasing more energy so complex multicellular organisms evolved; many anaerobic archaea died out
/ 4
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Photosynthesis removed CO2 (a greenhouse gas) from the atmosphere, storing carbon in sediments, carbonate rocks and fossil fuels
  • This reduced infrared retention, offsetting the increasing brightness of the Sun (about 10% per billion years) so temperatures did not rise excessively
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 7-8; AQA spec 3.1.1.1-3.1.1.2.
Question 810 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 22-23 · HUM-02
[6 marks]08.1Explain how each of the following changes in abiotic factors caused by human activities can make a habitat unsuitable for species living there.
[2 marks]08.2Explain how increased water turbidity caused by ploughing, mining or dredging harms aquatic organisms.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why species that were already rare are most likely to be lost when abiotic conditions change.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (6 marks)
  • Drainage of wetlands / lowering water tables by groundwater abstraction dries out wetlands; HEP schemes alter river levels flooding sandbank nests of turtles / birds; species with narrow tolerance (specialists) affected first; sundews lost when drained soil becomes aerobic / nitrogen-rich and taller competitors invade
  • Hot water discharges and organic wastes (sewage) reduce dissolved oxygen, killing aerobic organisms such as fish and insect larvae
  • Acid mine drainage and acid rain gases lower pH, denaturing proteins of exposed tissues (fish eggs, gills) and dissolving calcium exoskeletons (crayfish)
/ 6
08.2 (2 marks)
  • Reduces light penetration so submerged plants cannot photosynthesise
  • Blocks the gills of filter feeders such as bivalve molluscs; sediment smothers the bed
/ 2
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Rare species often have very specialised habitat requirements / narrow ranges of tolerance
  • A small change moves conditions outside their range while generalists survive; small populations cannot recover
/ 2

Question 910 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 66-67 · HAB-10
[3 marks]09.1Describe the ecological features of mangroves and the adaptations of mangrove trees.
[4 marks]09.2Explain four reasons why mangroves are important.
[3 marks]09.3Describe the main threats to mangroves and the conservation efforts used.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (3 marks)
  • Tropical intertidal coastal forests of halophytic (salt-tolerant) trees in saline, oxygen-deficient (anaerobic) mud
  • Prop / aerial roots for support in soft sediment and for gas exchange (pneumatophores); salt excretion
  • Seeds germinate on the parent tree and float, rooting where they touch sand - rapid natural regeneration
/ 3
09.2 (4 marks)
  • Coastal erosion / storm protection: roots and vegetation absorb wave energy (least tsunami damage in 2004 where mangroves were intact)
  • Fisheries: nursery grounds where dense roots protect young fish that later live on reefs / open sea
  • Protection of coral reefs: slow river water so suspended solids settle and nutrients are absorbed before reaching reefs
  • Resources: timber, fuel; medicines (antimicrobials against MRSA, cyanobacterium for lung cancer); high biodiversity (crabs, shrimps, sponges, birds)
/ 4
09.3 (3 marks)
  • Clearance for aquaculture (shrimp ponds), urban development, ports, tourism; pollution; sea level rise / climate change; destruction of coral reefs that shelter the coast
  • Legal protection / designated areas (eg Sri Lanka protected all remaining mangroves in 2015)
  • Replanting and natural regeneration; control of damaging activities; Ramsar sites
/ 3

Question 1010 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3-3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 32-49 · CBR-09
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the relative importance of ex-situ and in-situ methods in the conservation of endangered species.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by rewilding.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • In-situ: species remain in natural conditions with the whole community and inter-species relationships; protected areas (SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Ramsar, MCZ), land ownership, agri-environment schemes, habitat creation and design (area, shape, corridors, diversity), management of abiotic and biotic factors, plagioclimax maintenance, population control, reintroductions and rewilding (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish beavers)
  • Limits of in-situ: external threats (pollution, climate change), enforcement, cost, conflicts with land use, small fragmented populations
  • Ex-situ: captive breeding when in-situ is impossible or insufficient; selection criteria; difficulties keeping and breeding (habitat size, food, relationships, breeding triggers, group dynamics, gene pool / inbreeding, hybridisation); techniques (stud books, cryopreservation, AI, embryo transfer, micropropagation, cloning); seed banks; successes (Hawaiian goose, Iberian lynx, red kite, large blue)
  • Limits of ex-situ: expensive, few species, loss of wild behaviour, small gene pools, release requires suitable habitat and post-release support (hard vs soft release, monitoring)
  • Judgement: ex-situ is a temporary safeguard / support that only succeeds if in-situ habitat exists to return to; both needed, in-situ fundamental
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Restoring natural processes / communities to a habitat changed by humans, often by reintroducing missing (keystone) species and removing human management such as grazing
/ 1

Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Ecological features: cnidarian polyps, cilia, nematocysts, zooxanthellae symbiosis, limestone skeletons building reefs, complex structure and food web; specific abiotic requirements (temperature, light, salinity, low turbidity)

  • Conservation: MPAs, No Take Zones, fishing controls, tourism management, protection of linked habitats, CITES
  • Comparison with other habitats (rainforests, mangroves, Antarctica) where relevant
  • Evaluation of local vs global threats and justified conclusion
