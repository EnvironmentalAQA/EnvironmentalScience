Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Succession is the sequence by which pioneers colonise bare ground, modify the abiotic conditions and are replaced by later seres until a climax community controlled by climate is reached; where grazing, mowing, burning or coppicing halts it, a plagioclimax forms. This tells a conservation manager whether to intervene: chalk grassland, heathland and coppiced woodland are plagioclimaxes that disappear without continued management, whereas a climax habitat is best left alone, and habitat creation must plan the stages, control scrub and manage secondary succession.

Population dynamics - birth and death rates, density-dependent and independent factors, carrying capacity, sigmoid growth, predator-prey cycles and r- and k-selection - inform decisions of a different kind. Maximum sustainable yield sets harvest levels in fisheries, whaling and forestry; k-selected species that breed late and slowly need strict protection and recover slowly; culling may be justified where predators are absent, as with Scottish red deer without wolves, or where introduced species threaten natives, while the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone shows how restoring a predator changes vegetation through the food web. Monitoring population size, distribution, survival and age structure measures success, and knowledge of adaptation and ranges of tolerance guides how abiotic conditions are managed.

The limits are uncertainty: natural fluctuations, unknown carrying capacities and the difficulty of predicting how communities respond. Ecological understanding cannot remove that uncertainty, but without it conservation decisions - to manage or not, to cull or protect, how much to harvest - are guesses.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Succession is when a habitat changes over time from pioneer species to a climax community. Conservationists need to understand this because some habitats like heathland will turn into woodland if they are not managed by grazing or burning. Population dynamics is about how populations grow and are limited by factors like food and predators. This helps conservation because you can work out how many animals can be taken without the population declining, and when a population needs protecting. Monitoring populations shows if conservation is working.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 explains both concepts correctly and links them to decisions, but generally; Level 3 uses the technical vocabulary (seres, plagioclimax, k-selection, MSY) with named cases (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish deer, chalk grassland).
  • Level 3 covers more applications (habitat creation, culling introduced species, adaptation and tolerance) and evaluates the uncertainties.
  • Structure: Level 3 gives each concept its own paragraph with the decisions it informs, then a conclusion.
