Question 110 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 352-355 · AQUA-01
[3 marks]01.1Explain why carnivorous species such as salmon dominate aquaculture in MEDCs while herbivores such as carp and tilapia dominate in LEDCs.
[3 marks]01.2Describe how all-female rainbow trout are produced and explain why this is done.
[4 marks]01.3Explain how each of the following abiotic factors is controlled in an intensive salmon farm.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (3 marks)
  • MEDCs: market demand and higher prices for the flavour of carnivorous fish (salmon, trout) justify high inputs (fishmeal, cooler climates suit them)
  • LEDCs: herbivores / omnivores feed on natural vegetation, phytoplankton and wastes so need low inputs; warm climates suit tilapia
  • Herbivores are more energy-efficient (lower trophic level) so more food per unit area
/ 3
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Some genetically female (XX) fish are treated with testosterone so they become functional males producing only X-carrying sperm
  • Crossed with normal females (XX) all offspring are XX females
  • Female trout meat has a better flavour; in tilapia the reverse (all males) because males grow larger / more efficiently
/ 3
01.3 (4 marks)
  • Salmon need high oxygen: water sprays / weirs aerate; wastes removed to prevent deoxygenation
  • Constant circular flow keeps fish swimming the same way - fewer collisions / injuries, more muscular fish, higher stocking density
  • Artificial lighting delays maturation (salmon stop growing at maturity) so larger fish; controls smoltification
  • Cool sites (Scotland, Norway) because warm water holds less oxygen although warmth increases growth
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 352-355; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.
Question 210 marks · Adaptation, terminology, succession & population control · spec 3.1.3.2 · Genn pp. 82-85 · LP-02
[8 marks]02.1Define each of the following ecological terms and give an example.
[2 marks]02.2Explain the difference between an ecosystem and a biome.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (8 marks)
  • Species: a group of closely related organisms that naturally interbreed to produce fertile offspring and are reproductively isolated from other groups (Passer domesticus, house sparrow); the only 'real' taxon
  • Niche: the role a species plays in its habitat - how it uses resources and relates to other species (tawny owl: nocturnal carnivore nesting in tree holes eating small mammals); two species cannot share exactly the same niche in one habitat
  • Community: all the populations of all species (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, protists, archaea) living in an area
  • Biome: a large geographical region with a specific climate and characteristic community, including all areas where it occurs (all tropical rainforests, tundra, savannah)
/ 8
02.2 (2 marks)
  • An ecosystem is the biotic community plus abiotic factors of one area, relatively self-contained (a particular coral reef)
  • A biome includes all separate ecosystems with the same climate and community type (all coral reefs); each reef is a different ecosystem within the same biome
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 82-85; AQA spec 3.1.3.2.

  • So a change in the population of one species has a relatively small impact on the whole community

Question 510 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.6 · Genn pp. 402-406 · MON-02
[8 marks]05.1Explain how each of the following new technologies improves the validity of ecological research.
[2 marks]05.2Explain why new technologies allow the collection of 'more representative' data.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (8 marks)
  • Tracking: continuous data on movements, territory size, migration routes and habitat use without disturbance, over large areas and long periods; acoustic tags in seawater; geolocators for small birds; more representative than occasional sightings
  • DNA: identifies individuals, gene pool size / inbreeding and regional populations (tracing ivory, timber, fish); eDNA detects rare or hidden aquatic species (great crested newt, invasive carp) from shed cells without capture
  • Image recognition: software identifies individuals from unique markings (tiger stripes, whale shark spots, fin damage) in camera-trap / drone / satellite images, giving population size, lifespan, social structure from large image sets quickly and objectively
  • Acoustic: bat detectors, hydrophones and sonograms record species that are hard to see (bats, cetaceans, insects), giving presence, abundance and activity continuously, day and night
/ 8
05.2 (2 marks)
  • Data collected continuously / over large areas / at night rather than at a few times and places a researcher can visit
  • Less disturbance so animals behave naturally; larger sample sizes; objective rather than subjective identification
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 402-406; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.6.

[2 marks]06.1Explain what is meant by a plagioclimax community and why chalk grassland is an example.
[4 marks]06.2Use Table 1 to explain why grazing produced the highest plant diversity.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why different grazing animals are chosen for different plagioclimax habitats.
[2 marks]06.4Explain how burning maintains heathland.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (2 marks)
  • A community maintained at a stage before the climax by regular human activity (grazing, mowing, burning, coppicing) that deflects succession
  • Chalk grassland was produced by centuries of sheep grazing; without it succession to scrub and woodland resumes
/ 2
06.2 (4 marks)
  • Grazing removes taller / dominant grasses and prevents scrub establishing (5% cover) so small plants are not shaded out
  • Trampling creates bare ground for wildflower seed germination and warm patches for invertebrates / reptiles
  • Dung creates small nutrient-rich patches supporting different plants and invertebrates; sheep eat short grasses and some bushes
  • Mowing removes everything at once including plants grazers avoid, and removes flowers / seeds before setting; burning removes vegetation but also kills invertebrates and allows scrub regrowth; abandoned plot dominated by scrub (55%) so few species
/ 4
06.3 (2 marks)
  • Sheep eat short grass (some breeds eat scrub); ponies avoid flowers but eat bracken; cattle remove long rough grass
  • Choice depends on the vegetation to be controlled and the plants to be protected
/ 2
06.4 (2 marks)
  • Removes mature heather bushes and young trees that grazers will not eat, creating open unshaded ground
  • Heat stimulates dormant heather seeds to germinate so young heather re-establishes
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 47-49; AQA spec 3.1.3.3.

Question 810 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.1 · Genn pp. 360-364 · FOR-01
[5 marks]08.1Describe the resources obtained from forests. Give one example for each category.
[3 marks]08.2Explain why forests are an important carbon reservoir and how they can be used to counteract climate change.
[2 marks]08.3Explain how forests regulate the hydrological cycle and local climate.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (5 marks)
  • Timber: building construction, furniture, tools, telegraph poles, concrete shuttering (oak, beech, mahogany, teak, conifer softwood)
  • Fuel: fuelwood and charcoal - still the main energy source for most people in LEDCs
  • Food: fruit, nuts, bushmeat, livestock fodder; crops of forest origin (coffee, cacao, bananas, Brazil nuts); pigs and chickens were forest animals
  • Fibres: paper (wood pulp cellulose), cotton, viscose / rayon
  • Medicines: quinine (cinchona), taxol (yew), aspirin (willow); most species not yet researched
/ 5
08.2 (3 marks)
  • Wood is mainly cellulose which is hard to digest, so carbon stored in wood has a long residence time; total forest carbon (vegetation plus soil) is about double that in the atmosphere
  • Growing trees sequester CO2 by photosynthesis and release oxygen; forest soils hold more carbon than the trees (especially boreal)
  • Planting / protecting forests removes CO2 and reduces the enhanced greenhouse effect
/ 3
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Highest transpiration of any ecosystem increases downwind rainfall; interception increases evaporation; deep organic soils retain water and delay runoff so river flow is more even and flooding reduced
  • Low albedo absorbs sunlight; heat stored and released at night reduces day-night temperature extremes; canopy microclimate: lower light, wind, higher humidity
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 360-364; AQA spec 3.5.3.1.
