Question 110 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 90-92 · CC-08
[6 marks]01.1Explain how climate change may affect the survival of species. Use named examples.
[2 marks]01.2Explain why a species living in an interdependent community may be unable to move to a new area even if suitable conditions exist there.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why polar regions warming faster than the equator affects the weather in the UK.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (6 marks)
  • Direct effect of abiotic change: species near the edge of their range of tolerance (temperature, water) may die out locally, eg sundews if marshes dry, frogs if climate drier; beech (shallow roots) suffers in drought more than oak
  • Changes in timing: earlier flowering / emergence may separate flowers from pollinators; plant toxins building up earlier may kill caterpillars; warmer winters disturb dormouse hibernation so they use fat reserves and starve
  • Biotic changes: food supply, predators, disease vectors (mosquitoes / malaria spreading), pollinators and seed dispersers may change
  • Distribution changes: species may colonise newly suitable areas (triton molluscs around the UK) but slow-growing species (trees) may disappear from old ranges faster than they colonise; suitable areas or biological corridors may not exist; human land use blocks movement
  • Population fragmentation: rising seas or upward retreat to mountains isolate populations; divided gene pools increase inbreeding; small populations may not be viable; no repopulation from elsewhere
  • Some species benefit (bats: warmer winters, more insects) but wetter stormier weather reduces feeding time
/ 6
01.2 (2 marks)
  • All the species it depends on (food plants, pollinators, prey, mycorrhizae) will not move at the same speed
  • Less mobile species (plants, non-flying animals) cannot cross unsuitable areas without a biological corridor
/ 2
01.3 (2 marks)
  • The temperature difference between mid-latitude and polar air drives the jet streams; a smaller difference makes the jet stream slower and more meandering (Rossby waves)
  • Weather systems stall over an area, giving prolonged drought or rainfall / floods; unusually cold or warm air carried north or south
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 90-92; AQA spec 3.2.1.2.
Question 210 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.2 · Genn pp. 162-164 · SOIL-04
[4 marks]02.1Explain how each of the following human activities increases soil erosion.
[2 marks]02.2Explain why landslides become more likely on steep slopes some years after deforestation.
[4 marks]02.3Describe four environmental impacts of accelerated soil erosion away from the field where it occurs.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
  • Overgrazing: vegetation is eaten faster than it grows so soil is exposed; hooves disturb the soil, damage roots and compact it, reducing infiltration and increasing runoff
  • Reduced biota (ploughing, agrochemicals, less organic matter): less humus so less adhesion between particles; fewer worms so less aeration and lower infiltration capacity; fewer nutrients released so less vegetation cover
/ 4
02.2 (2 marks)
  • Tree roots that held the soil together decompose so the soil is bound less strongly
  • Heavy rain adds mass and lubricates particles; without interception more water enters the soil, making slumping more likely
/ 2
02.3 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Sedimentation in rivers reduces flow capacity so flooding downstream is more likely
  • Sedimentation in reservoirs reduces their storage volume
  • Coastal sedimentation covering reefs / seagrass; turbidity reducing light for aquatic plants
  • Increased atmospheric particulates from wind erosion worsening asthma; dust covering crops / urban areas
  • Desertification as vegetation cannot re-establish; reduced rainfall
  • Nutrients (phosphates) carried on soil particles causing eutrophication
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 162-164; AQA spec 3.2.5.2.
Question 35 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.1 · Genn pp. 237-238 · PPRO-06
[1 mark]03.1Which of the following is a secondary pollutant? Tick one box.
  • A  Sulfur dioxide from a coal-fired power station
  • B  Tropospheric ozone
  • C  Carbon monoxide from a car exhaust
  • D  Nitrogen monoxide from a diesel engine
[4 marks]03.2Explain the difference between a primary pollutant and a secondary pollutant, using acid rain as an example.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (1 marks)
  • B - Tropospheric ozone (formed by reactions of NOx in sunlight)
/ 1
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Primary pollutant: released directly by human activities, eg sulfur dioxide from burning coal / smelting sulfide ores; NOx from high-temperature combustion
  • Secondary pollutant: produced by chemical reactions between primary pollutants, often with non-pollutants (water, oxygen, sunlight)
  • Sulfur dioxide is oxidised by ozone to sulfur trioxide which dissolves to form sulfuric acid; NOx dissolve to form nitric acid
  • The acids (sulfurous, sulfuric, nitric) are secondary pollutants; ozone itself is a secondary pollutant of NOx photochemistry
/ 4

Question 410 marks · Fluctuations in supply/demand & energy storage · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 219-221 · EST-03
[4 marks]04.1Explain how hydrogen can be used to store energy from intermittent renewable resources, and describe two ways the stored energy can be released.
[3 marks]04.2Compare three methods of storing hydrogen, giving a disadvantage of each.
[2 marks]04.3Explain how a fuel cell differs from a rechargeable battery.
[1 mark]04.4Explain what is meant by the 'hydrogen economy'.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Surplus electricity (eg windy nights) electrolyses water to produce hydrogen which is stored
  • Hydrogen has a high energy density so it can be transported / stored for long periods
  • Released by combustion for heat, industrial processes or steam turbines; or by fuel cells combining hydrogen and oxygen electrochemically to produce electricity and water
  • Can also be used as a vehicle fuel or converted to methane / ammonia
/ 4
04.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Compressed gas (up to 700 times normal volume) - energy to run the compressor, strong tanks
  • Liquefied at very low temperature - energy for refrigeration and compression
  • Adsorbed on a metal matrix (metal hydride) at low pressure - tanks larger and heavier than a petrol tank storing the same energy
  • Conversion to ammonia - some energy needed but less than the alternatives
/ 3
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Both convert chemical energy to electricity electrochemically, but a battery stores a fixed amount of chemicals which are reformed by recharging
  • A fuel cell keeps producing electricity as long as fresh fuel (hydrogen / alcohol) and oxidant are supplied
/ 2
04.4 (1 marks)
  • Using hydrogen made from surplus renewable electricity as the main store and carrier of energy, released when demand exceeds supply or intermittent sources fail, for heating, vehicles and electricity
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 219-221; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 510 marks · Solid wastes · spec 3.4.3.2.12 · Genn pp. 279-282 · SW-05

  • Disposable products (razors, lighters, pens) that must be replaced

Any 3 from:

  • Population density (collection costs)

  • Public willingness to recycle / environmental awareness
  • Processing costs / household income

  • Disadvantage: needs separation / collection of clean organic waste; odour / pests / pathogens if poorly managed; low-value product; not suitable for mixed waste
/ 2
05.4 (1 marks)

[3 marks]07.1Explain why clay particles do not settle out in the sedimentation stage, and describe how they are removed in stage 4.
[2 marks]07.2Explain the purpose of aeration (stage 3).
[2 marks]07.3Explain why ozone or UV light may be used for sterilisation instead of chlorine when water comes from peaty upland sources.
[3 marks]07.4Describe how each of the following contaminants is removed from water.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (3 marks)
  • Clay particles are very small and carry electrostatic charges on their surfaces so they repel each other and stay suspended
  • Flocculants (aluminium sulfate / alum or polyelectrolytes) are added and mixed to neutralise the charges
  • The particles clump together (flocs) and settle in the clarifier tank
/ 3
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Increases dissolved oxygen and removes hydrogen sulfide (bad-egg smell) from anaerobic sources
  • Oxidises some dissolved metals (eg iron) so they become insoluble and can be removed; improves taste
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Peaty water contains organic matter which reacts with chlorine to produce toxic substances (eg trihalomethanes)
  • Ozone / UV kill pathogens without forming these by-products (though chlorine / chloramine keeps water sterile during distribution)
/ 2
07.4 (3 marks)
  • Ion exchange: toxic ions adsorb onto polymer resin beads or zeolite (also used to soften water by removing calcium / magnesium)
  • Reverse osmosis / distillation (desalination) - expensive, energy-intensive
  • Activated carbon: organic molecules causing odour / taste and pesticides adsorb onto the high-surface-area carbon particles
/ 3

Question 810 marks · Nutrient pollution, sewage treatment & acid mine drainage · spec 3.4.3.2.9 · Genn pp. 273-275 · NUT-06
[3 marks]08.1Explain why organic pollution of rivers is usually monitored by measuring Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) rather than the concentrations of the pollutants themselves.
[2 marks]08.2Explain why a coliform count is used to assess the health risk from sewage contamination rather than testing for pathogens such as typhoid.
[3 marks]08.3Give three characteristics of species that make them useful in a biotic index.
[2 marks]08.4Give one advantage and one disadvantage of using a biotic index rather than chemical monitoring.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Many different organic substances may be present, each with a different potential to cause deoxygenation, so it is difficult to measure or forecast them individually
  • BOD measures the effect (amount of oxygen used by microbes decomposing the organic matter) under standardised conditions (eg 5 days, 20 °C, dark)
  • It gives a single measure directly related to the risk of deoxygenation
/ 3
08.2 (2 marks)
  • E. coli is a common gut bacterium so its presence confirms faecal contamination and its number indicates how serious the contamination is
  • Pathogens are not always present so their absence would not prove the water is uncontaminated
/ 2
08.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Different species have different sensitivities to pollution
  • Easy to identify
  • Easy to find / normally present / usually common
  • Widely distributed
/ 3
08.4 (2 marks)
  • Advantage: assesses the effect of pollution over a period of time (recent pollution events are detected even if the pollutant has gone); no expensive equipment needed
  • Disadvantage: identification needs skill; does not identify which pollutant is present so further chemical tests are required; species may be absent for other reasons
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 273-275; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.9.

Question 1010 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.5 · Genn pp. 245-254 · AIR-07
[9 marks]10.1Explain how the properties of smoke and of the gases involved in acid rain lead to their environmental effects, and evaluate the effectiveness of the methods used to control them.
[1 mark]10.2State one effect of tropospheric ozone on plants.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Smoke: solid particulates (PM10, PM5, PM1) from incomplete combustion; small particles stay suspended, penetrate lungs; toxic chemicals (acids, phenols, metals) carried on particles; high albedo cools climate; blocks light reducing photosynthesis; damages buildings
  • Smoke smogs form under temperature inversions in valleys (London 1952, 12 000 deaths); synergism with SO2
  • Controls: Clean Air Act 1956 (smokeless zones), smokeless fuels / gas / electricity, coal treatment, electrostatic precipitators, cyclones, bag filters, scrubbers, DPFs, better combustion; smoke smogs have largely been eliminated in UK cities
  • Acid rain gases: SO2 (coal combustion, smelting sulfide ores) oxidised by ozone to SO3 - sulfuric acid; NOx (high temperature combustion) - nitric acid; HCl from PVC incineration; reactive gases travel long distances (transboundary pollution)
  • Effects: pH denatures proteins / enzymes in exposed cells (gills, eggs, root hairs, stomata); dissolves exoskeletons; leaches Ca and Mg then mobilises toxic Al and Pb; corrodes metals, dissolves limestone; severity depends on bedrock (limestone neutralises)
  • Controls: fuel desulfurisation (coal washing, hydrodesulfurisation, low-sulfur ship fuels), dry and wet FGD, fluidised bed combustion, catalytic converters, urea sprays; switching from coal to gas / renewables
  • Evaluation: SO2 emissions in Europe have fallen dramatically and lake pH is recovering; NOx harder to control because of growing vehicle numbers; catalytic converters need lead-free fuel and warm engines; FGD is costly and produces wastes / gypsum; controlling primary pollutants also prevents secondary pollutants (ozone, PANs)
  • Judgement on relative success of controls for particulates vs SO2 vs NOx
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
Any 1 from:
  • Reduces growth rates of trees / crops
  • Damages leaf cuticles (allowing SO2 to cause more damage)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 245-254; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.1-3.4.3.2.5.

Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.

[25 marks]11.2Mineral resources are non-renewable. Discuss how an understanding of geological processes and the development of new technologies can extend the period for which society can rely on mineral resources.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Why non-renewable: formed too slowly; geological concentration processes - hydrothermal, metamorphic, Proterozoic sediments, alluvial, evaporites, secondary enrichment, biological sediments
