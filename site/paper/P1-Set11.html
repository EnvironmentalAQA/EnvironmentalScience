Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Neonicotinoids are neurotoxins related to nicotine that block the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. They are highly toxic to insects but of low toxicity to vertebrates because they cannot cross the vertebrate blood-brain barrier - the specificity that made them popular. Bees, however, are insects: even sub-lethal doses impair navigation, foraging and immunity, and this has been linked to colony decline. Because the compounds are water-soluble and moderately persistent they remain in the soil, are absorbed by roots and translocated through the plant (systemic action), so they appear in pollen and nectar that bees collect; persistence may also allow build-up in soils and aquifers, although sunlight and soil microbes break them down. Some fungicides act synergistically with them, increasing toxicity to bees.

Exposure is greatest when crops are sprayed or when dust from coated seeds is blown by wind, so impacts are reduced by using seed coatings instead of sprays, spraying only when crops are not in flower or at night when bees are not foraging, and applying on still days to limit drift. The EU has banned outdoor use on flowering crops, and integrated control with cultural and biological methods reduces the need for insecticides altogether.

These measures reduce bee exposure, but the alternatives farmers turn to, such as pyrethroids, also harm pollinators, and restrictions can reduce yields, so the trade-off between crop protection and pollination services remains.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (4 marks)

Neonicotinoids are insecticides that kill insects by affecting their nervous system. They are systemic so they get into the whole plant including the pollen, and bees pick them up when they collect pollen and nectar. This harms bees and can cause colonies to die. To reduce the impact, farmers can spray when crops are not flowering, and some neonicotinoids have been banned in the EU. Other methods of pest control could be used instead.

What makes the difference
  • Level 2 links the systemic property to bee exposure correctly but omits the other properties (solubility, persistence, specificity via the blood-brain barrier, synergism) that the question's 'properties' demands.
  • Level 3 explains the sub-lethal effects (navigation, foraging, immunity) rather than 'harms bees'.
  • Level 3 includes the trade-off evaluation (alternatives also harm pollinators, yield costs) that the mark scheme rewards.
