Table 1 shows the ecological footprint and biocapacity per person for four countries and the world in 2016.

[2 marks]01.1Define ecological footprint and biocapacity.
[2 marks]01.2Explain what is meant by a global hectare and why it is used.
[1 mark]01.3Use Table 1 to calculate how many Earths would be needed if everyone lived like the average US citizen.
[3 marks]01.4Use Table 1 to describe the relationship between development and ecological footprint, and explain the concept of Earth Overshoot Day.
[2 marks]01.5Suggest two ways a country such as the UK could reduce its footprint without reducing its HDI.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Ecological footprint: the area of the Earth's surface (global hectares) needed to provide the resources used by a person / population / activity and to absorb the wastes produced
  • Biocapacity: the biological productivity of an area (forests, cropland, grazing, fisheries, built land) - its ability to supply resources and absorb wastes, expressed in global hectares
/ 2
01.2 (2 marks)
  • A hectare of land / sea with the world-average biological productivity
  • Biocapacities of real areas vary widely so a standard unit allows footprints and biocapacities of different places to be compared
/ 2
01.3 (1 marks)
  • 8.1 / 1.7 = 4.8 (accept 4.7-4.8)
/ 1
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Higher HDI is associated with a larger footprint (USA 8.1 vs Bangladesh 0.8), though the UK achieves the same HDI as the USA with about half the footprint
  • Global footprint (2.8) exceeds global biocapacity (1.7) so humanity uses resources faster than they regenerate and CO2 accumulates (overshoot)
  • Earth Overshoot Day is the date each year when a year's worth of resources / waste absorption has been used up; it falls earlier each year (8 August in 2016)
/ 3
01.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Renewable energy and energy conservation (carbon footprint is the largest component)
  • Reduced meat consumption / food waste; local seasonal food
  • Circular economy: repair, re-use, recycling; longer product lives
  • Public transport, efficient buildings

Question 310 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 30, 38-39 · PRI-04

Table 1 shows some designated protected areas in the UK.

[4 marks]03.1Complete Table 1 to show the legislation under which each area is designated and its key feature.
Table 1
DesignationDesignated underKey feature
Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI)
Special Protection Area (SPA)
Ramsar site
Marine Conservation Zone (MCZ)
[3 marks]03.2Explain how designating a protected area protects habitats and species.
[3 marks]03.3Explain why voluntary agri-environmental schemes are needed in addition to legislation.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • SSSI: UK legislation (Wildlife and Countryside Act); best examples of UK flora, fauna, geological features; management plan agreed with owner who must notify Operations Likely to Damage (ploughing, drainage, pesticides, tree planting / removal)
  • SPA: EU Birds Directive; places of international importance for birds (with SACs under the Habitats Directive form the Natura 2000 network; may restrict activities in surrounding areas)
  • Ramsar: Ramsar Convention (intergovernmental agreement); internationally important wetlands, originally for waterfowl, now wetland conservation and wise use generally
  • MCZ: UK marine legislation (Marine and Coastal Access Act) under the W&CA framework; protects marine habitats / species by restricting activities such as fishing and dredging; NNR: best SSSIs with complete communities
/ 4
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Legal status: restrictions on damaging activities within the area (and sometimes in surrounding areas, eg drainage)
  • Management agreements between owners and statutory bodies to maintain suitable conditions
  • But it does not protect against external influences such as air pollution or climate change; very few sites are state-owned so protection depends on owner cooperation
/ 3
03.3 (3 marks)
  • Much of the UK's wildlife lives in habitats created by farming (hay meadows, hedgerows) which only survive if the farming continues
  • Managing land for wildlife costs money / reduces income so farmers need payments (points for beetle banks, hedgerows, buffer strips, wild bird seed, low-input grassland)
  • Benefits everyone so society pays through central funding; legislation cannot compel positive management
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 30, 38-39; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.2.

Table 1 shows the energy inputs and food energy outputs of three farming systems.

Table 1
SystemEnergy input / GJ ha-1 yr-1Food energy output / GJ ha-1 yr-1
Extensive cereal farming (low inputs)420
Intensive cereal farming (fertiliser, pesticide, machinery)2060

[2 marks]05.1Calculate the energy ratio (output / input) for each system.
[3 marks]05.2Use your answers to explain the difference between productivity and efficiency.
[3 marks]05.3Explain what is meant by an energy subsidy and give three examples.
[2 marks]05.4Explain why spreading the same total inputs over a larger area would increase total food production.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (2 marks)
  • Extensive cereal 5.0; intensive cereal 3.0
  • Intensive beef 0.1
/ 2
05.2 (3 marks)
  • Intensive cereal is most productive per hectare (60 GJ) but less efficient (3.0) than extensive (5.0)
  • Each extra unit of input gives a smaller extra yield (law of diminishing returns)
  • Beef has very low productivity and efficiency because energy is lost at an extra trophic level
/ 3
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Any input that increases productivity but requires the use of energy (usually fossil fuel)
  • Manufacture of nitrate fertiliser (Haber process) and pesticides; fuel for machinery; pumping irrigation water; grain drying; food processing and transport; embodied energy of machinery
/ 3
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Because of diminishing returns, the first units of input on unfertilised land give the largest yield increases
  • Moving inputs from intensively farmed land (where extra input adds little) to extensively farmed land gives a greater total yield - though land, cost and distribution limit this
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 324-327; AQA spec 3.5.1.2.

Table 1 compares extensive oyster farming with intensive salmon farming.

[4 marks]06.1Use Table 1 to explain the relative merits of extensive and intensive aquaculture.
[3 marks]06.2Describe the stages in breeding and rearing salmon in an intensive farm.
[3 marks]06.3Explain how shrimp farming has affected mangroves and how disease is controlled in shrimp lagoons.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (4 marks)
  • Extensive: low inputs of energy, food and chemicals so low cost and low environmental impact; sustainable; but low yield per area and dependent on natural productivity
  • Intensive: control of all limiting factors gives very high yield and profit per area; but high inputs (fishmeal from wild fish, energy), disease and lice at high density, pollution from wastes, antibiotic / pesticide use, escapes
  • Judgement depends on species, market and site
/ 4
06.2 (3 marks)
  • Adults with desirable characteristics (fast growth, shape, colour) stripped of eggs and milt which are mixed for fertilisation
  • Eggs raised in aerated freshwater tanks, dead / diseased eggs removed daily; fry fed fishmeal pellets and moved through tanks as they grow
  • After 12-18 months smolts transferred to seawater cages, fed pellets until harvest at 3-5 kg
/ 3
06.3 (3 marks)
  • About 10% of the world's mangroves have been cleared for coastal shrimp lagoons (up to 100 ha), removing nurseries and coastal protection
  • Lagoons need water exchange to remove wastes and aeration to prevent deoxygenation as wastes decompose
  • Eyestalk ablation stimulates breeding; high densities increase disease so antibiotics / pesticides used
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 352-356; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.

Question 810 marks · Scientific methodologies & sampling design · spec 3.7.1, 3.7.2.2 · Genn pp. 390-393, 419-420 · RM-03

A group of students wants to investigate the effect of vegetation cover on rain-splash soil erosion.

[5 marks]08.1Describe how they could carry out the investigation in the laboratory.
[3 marks]08.2Give three variables, other than those you have standardised, that affect rain-splash erosion in the field.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why it is easier to obtain valid results in the laboratory than in the field, and give one disadvantage of laboratory studies.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (5 marks)
  • Trays of the same soil (texture, compaction, organic matter, depth) with different percentage vegetation cover (eg 0, 25, 50, 75, 100% grass), replicated
  • Same slope / gradient; simulated rain from a standard height at a standard rate and drop size (eg watering can / sprinkler) for a standard time
  • Collect the soil splashed / washed off (eg onto a sheet or into a trough at the base), dry and weigh it
  • Repeat each treatment several times and calculate means (and standard deviation)
  • Plot soil loss against cover; statistical test (Spearman's rank / t-test) for significance
/ 5
08.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Rainfall intensity / raindrop size / height
  • Soil texture, compaction, organic matter, depth
  • Permeability of the material beneath
  • Gradient
  • Root binding
/ 3
08.3 (2 marks)
  • All variables except the independent variable can be standardised in the laboratory; in the field uncontrollable variables (weather) must be measured instead
  • Laboratory conditions may not represent natural conditions so results may not apply directly to real ecosystems
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 390-393, 419-420; AQA spec 3.7.1, 3.7.2.2.
Question 910 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 64-65 · HAB-09

Table 1 shows the numbers of breeding pairs of two seabird species on an island before and after a rat eradication programme.

Table 1
SpeciesBreeding pairs before eradicationBreeding pairs 8 years after eradication
Manx shearwater45310
Storm petrel12140
[4 marks]09.1Explain why oceanic islands have many endemic species but low overall biodiversity.
[3 marks]09.2Use Table 1 to explain why introduced rats had such a severe effect on these birds.
[2 marks]09.3Explain how avian malaria threatens Hawaiian birds and how climate change worsens the threat.
[1 mark]09.4Give one other conservation measure for oceanic islands besides eradicating introduced species.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (4 marks)
  • Isolation makes colonisation difficult: only marine species, seabirds, plants with floating seeds or chance arrivals (tortoises on logs, wind-blown birds) reach them
  • Whole taxa (terrestrial mammals, amphibians) may be absent so few original colonists
  • Colonists evolve in isolation under different selection pressures to fill the empty niches, producing unique species (Galapagos, Madagascar lemurs, Hawaii)
  • Endemics have small ranges and populations so are vulnerable to extinction
/ 4
09.2 (3 marks)
  • The birds evolved without mammal predators so nest on the ground / in burrows with no defence
  • Rats eat eggs and chicks; numbers fell to very low levels (45 and 12 pairs)
  • After eradication breeding success rose about 7-12 fold, confirming predation was the main limiting factor
/ 3
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Introduced mosquitoes carry avian malaria to which endemic birds have no resistance; birds survive only above about 1500 m where it is too cold for malaria
  • Warming allows mosquitoes / malaria to spread to higher altitudes, shrinking the safe range
/ 2
09.4 (1 marks)
  • Control of developments and visitor numbers (biosecurity, quarantine of imports); protected areas; captive breeding of endemics
/ 1
