Question 110 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 187-188 · FN-01

Figure 1 shows the percentage of the oil in a reservoir that can be recovered using different recovery techniques.

Figure 1
[2 marks]01.1Describe how primary oil recovery works and explain why it recovers only about 20% of the oil.
[2 marks]01.2Describe secondary recovery and explain how it can be linked to carbon capture and storage.
[3 marks]01.3Describe three tertiary (enhanced) recovery techniques.
[3 marks]01.4Explain three advantages of directional drilling.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Natural pressure of gas above the oil (or dissolved in it) or water beneath forces oil up the production well; pump-jacks ('nodding donkeys') increase flow
  • Pressure falls as oil is removed so the remaining oil no longer flows to the well
/ 2
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Water or natural gas is pumped down an injection well to maintain the pressure and flow of oil (recovery to about 40%)
  • Captured CO2 can be pumped down instead, increasing oil recovery while storing the CO2 underground
/ 2
01.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Steam injection (from combustion or solar parabolic concentrators) to heat the oil and reduce its viscosity
  • Controlled underground combustion to heat the oil
  • Detergents / solvents to reduce surface tension so oil flows more easily
  • Bacteria that partially digest heavy oil into lighter oils and produce CO2 to maintain pressure
/ 3
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Many wells can be drilled from a single platform, reducing land take / number of rigs
  • Deposits under locations where rigs cannot be placed (urban areas, sensitive habitats) can be reached
  • Drilling can follow softer strata (quicker) and target multiple small reservoirs up to 10 km away, increasing total recovery
/ 3

[3 marks]02.1Explain the natural greenhouse effect.
[3 marks]02.2Use Table 1 to explain why carbon dioxide is considered the most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas even though methane and CFCs absorb far more infrared per molecule.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why reducing methane emissions would produce a faster reduction in warming than reducing CO2 emissions by the same mass.
[2 marks]02.4Give two methods of reducing methane emissions from agriculture or waste.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Visible light passes through the atmosphere easily and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, which warms and emits infrared
  • Greenhouse gases (naturally water vapour and CO2) absorb infrared better than the average for the atmosphere, converting it to heat and re-emitting some back to the surface
  • This keeps the surface about 33 °C warmer than it would otherwise be
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
  • CO2 is emitted in far larger quantities (every combustion process, deforestation) so its total effect is greatest
  • CO2 has a long residence time so it accumulates; methane has a short residence time (12 years) so it is removed faster
  • CFC emissions are now very small following the Montreal Protocol
/ 3
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Methane has a much higher global warming potential (28x) so each tonne removed has a bigger effect
  • Its short residence time (about 12 years) means atmospheric concentrations fall quickly once emissions are cut
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Collect landfill / anaerobic digester gas and use as fuel
  • Changes in cattle diet / fewer cattle / less meat consumption
  • Draining rice paddies periodically
  • Anaerobic digestion of manure with gas capture
/ 2

Question 310 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.7 · Genn pp. 145-146 · MIN-08
[3 marks]03.1Describe polymetallic (manganese) nodules and explain why they are not yet exploited on a large scale.
[3 marks]03.2Explain the environmental impacts of mining polymetallic nodules.
[4 marks]03.3Explain why rare earth metals are a supply concern and describe one new extraction technique.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • Metal-rich lumps 5-10 cm across on the deep seabed (4000-5000 m), containing about 30% manganese plus nickel, copper, cobalt, iron, titanium
  • Exploitation would be very expensive (remotely operated collectors, pumping to the surface) / no viable technology yet
  • International agreement on ownership of the seabed and its resources is needed
/ 3
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Collectors disturb the seabed and kill benthic organisms living on it
  • Separating nodules from sediment creates plumes that increase turbidity with unknown consequences
  • Re-deposition of sediment covers and kills filter feeders and other benthic organisms; slow recovery in the deep sea
/ 3
03.3 (4 marks)
  • Used in small amounts in many essential technologies (smartphones, hybrid vehicles, wind turbines, fibre optics, LEDs)
  • Exploitable land deposits are limited and mainly in China; chemical separation is complex and produces large amounts of toxic waste
  • Bacterial adsorption: dissolved rare earth ions adsorb onto the cell surfaces of bacteria (Bacillus subtilis) and are washed off at specific pHs to separate them
  • Deep sea sediments contain relatively high concentrations but no viable extraction method yet
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 145-146; AQA spec 3.2.3.7.

[2 marks]04.3Describe the process of encapsulation used for intermediate-level radioactive waste and wastes containing heavy metals.
[1 mark]04.4Explain why vitrification is preferred to encapsulation in cement for high-level waste.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (6 marks)
  • Asbestos: fibres become airborne when it disintegrates and are inhaled, causing asbestosis (scar tissue thickens alveoli) and mesothelioma; disposal: double-wrapped in heavy-duty polythene in a specialised, recorded landfill (secure, permanent, sealed storage)
  • Cyanide: very toxic enzyme inhibitor (aerobic respiration) if inhaled / ingested; disposal: incineration so carbon and nitrogen are separated and oxidised to CO2 and NOx (much smaller problem)
  • High-level radioactive waste: intense ionising radiation, heat generation, long half-lives; disposal: vitrification - mixed with molten glass in sealed stainless steel containers in ventilated concrete stores
/ 6

  • The fibres are bound / cannot become airborne so cannot be inhaled
/ 1
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Waste is mixed with a cement slurry and poured into containers of impermeable, unreactive material
  • The solid cement immobilises the waste so it cannot leak / leach out
/ 2
04.4 (1 marks)
  • Even if the glass shatters the waste stays encapsulated in the fragments; glass is more stable / resistant to heat and leaching over very long timescales
/ 1

Question 510 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 395-396, 415-416 · RM-06

A student compared the invertebrates in a stream above and below a sewage outfall using kick sampling.

[3 marks]05.1Describe how kick sampling is carried out so that the samples from the two sites are comparable.
[3 marks]05.2Give three limitations of kick sampling.
[2 marks]05.3Explain how a Surber sampler improves on kick sampling.
[2 marks]05.4Explain why the invertebrates found give a better indication of long-term sewage pollution than a single measurement of dissolved oxygen.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Net held with its straight edge on the river bed facing the current (open to the flow)
  • The bed in front is disturbed by kicking / scraping a boot for a standard number of kicks and time (eg 30 s)
  • Same substrate type, water depth and flow rate at both sites; same net mesh; repeat samples; invertebrates identified with a key and counted
/ 3
05.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Difficult to standardise the vigour / area kicked
  • Buried organisms or those fixed to stones are not dislodged
  • Some organisms swim and avoid the net
  • Flow rate varies and affects capture
  • Fragile organisms destroyed
/ 3
05.3 (2 marks)
  • A fixed area of bed is enclosed by a frame so the sampled area is standardised; side nets reduce escapes
  • But only works in fast enough flow and is hard to set on rough substrates / deep water
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Pollutant / oxygen levels fluctuate (eg with flow, time of day) so one reading may miss a pollution event
  • Sensitive taxa (stonefly, mayfly larvae) disappear and tolerant ones (midge larvae, hoglouse) remain, integrating conditions over weeks / months (biotic index)
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 395-396, 415-416; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.
Question 610 marks · Thermal & oil pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.7 · Genn pp. 259-260 · WPOL-03

Table 1 shows features of oil tanker design and operation that reduce the risk of oil pollution.

[5 marks]06.1Complete Table 1 by explaining how each feature reduces oil pollution.
Table 1
FeatureHow it reduces oil pollution
Double hull
Inert gas system
Separate oil and ballast tanks
Twin engines / rudders
Recirculation of oil during unloading (crude oil washing)
[2 marks]06.2Explain how each of the following reduces the risk of tanker accidents.
[2 marks]06.3Describe how a bund wall prevents oil pollution from a storage tank.
[1 mark]06.4Explain the purpose of an oil interceptor on the drainage system of a car park.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (5 marks)
  • Double hull: a gap of up to 2 m between the hulls so damage to the outer hull in a collision / grounding does not release oil
  • Inert gas system: cooled engine exhaust gas (no oxygen) fills the space above the oil so an explosive oil vapour / air mixture cannot form
  • Separate ballast tanks: ballast water is not carried in oil tanks so no residual oil is discharged when ballast is pumped out
  • Twin engines / rudders: if one fails the ship can still be steered / propelled and is not carried onto rocks by currents or wind
  • Crude oil washing: sludges are mixed and removed with the cargo so tanks do not need washing with seawater that was then discharged; oily water is unloaded at the terminal
/ 5
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Offshore routes keep tankers further from the coast so if there is engine failure there is more time to deal with it before the ship is carried ashore
  • GPS gives accurate navigation; AIS lets ship positions be monitored and coordinated to avoid collisions and groundings
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • The tank stands on an impermeable base surrounded by a wall
  • The volume enclosed by the wall is greater than the tank volume so all the oil is contained if the tank splits / cannot overflow
/ 2
06.4 (1 marks)
  • Separates floating oil from drainage water so the oil is retained (for later treatment) and only water flows to the drains / river
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 259-260; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.7.

  • Cadmium and zinc occur together in the same ores so mine drainage / smelting releases both

Question 910 marks · Soils: fertility, erosion & management · spec 3.2.5.2 · Genn pp. 162-164 · SOIL-03

Figure 1 shows the annual soil loss measured on experimental plots with different vegetation cover on a 10% slope in a region with heavy seasonal rain.

Figure 1
[2 marks]09.1Describe the pattern shown in Figure 1.
[4 marks]09.2Explain four ways in which vegetation reduces the rate of soil erosion.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why ploughing up and down a slope causes more erosion than contour ploughing.
[2 marks]09.4Explain why the region's climate makes the soil vulnerable to both wind and water erosion.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (2 marks)
  • Soil loss increases as vegetation cover decreases: forest lowest (0.5), bare soil highest (60 t ha-1 yr-1) - over 100 times more
  • Ploughing downslope loses nearly three times as much as contour ploughing for the same crop
/ 2
09.2 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Leaves / leaf litter intercept raindrops so soil particles are not dislodged by rain splash
  • Roots bind soil particles together
  • Vegetation acts as a windbreak reducing wind velocity and the kinetic energy available to carry particles
  • Plants increase infiltration (root channels, organic matter) so less surface runoff
  • Organic matter / humus from vegetation binds particles into stable peds
/ 4
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Furrows running downslope act as channels / gulleys that increase the velocity (kinetic energy) of runoff
  • Furrows along the contour trap the water so it loses energy, deposits particles and infiltrates
/ 2
09.4 (2 marks)
  • In the dry season the soil (especially low-clay soils) is loose and dry with little cohesion so wind blows it away
  • In the wet season heavy rain causes rain splash and surface runoff when infiltration capacity is exceeded
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 162-164; AQA spec 3.2.5.2.
Question 1010 marks · Noise pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.13 · Genn pp. 286-295 · NOISE-06
[9 marks]10.1Using named examples, explain how the sources of noise pollution can be controlled by changes to design, operation and location.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by the Leq 57 dB contour used around UK airports.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Principles: dB is logarithmic (10 dB = 10x; 3 dB = 2x) so small reductions in dB are large reductions in intensity; effects on humans (hearing, stress, behaviour) and wildlife (breeding birds, bats, cetaceans)
  • Aircraft design: high bypass ratio engines, chevron nozzles, hush kits, acoustic liners, fairings, composites, blended wing
  • Aircraft operation: take-off angle, flight paths avoiding urban areas, constant descent, reduced reverse thrust, night restrictions, Chapter noise limits, fines and noise charges, quota count
  • Airport design / location: away from population centres, taxi and engine-test areas away from housing, acoustic walls, baffle mounds, land-use restrictions, free acoustic insulation
  • Railway: track polishing, sound-absorbing ballast and suspension, pantograph fairings, wheel lubrication, composite brakes
  • Road: porous / rubber asphalt, acoustic fences, embankments, tree planting, aerodynamics, engine insulation, traffic management and routing, double glazing
  • Industrial: silencers on compressors, pressing instead of stamping, nylon rollers, drilling instead of pile-driving, bubble curtains, timing of blasting, acoustic mats / curtains, remote operation, ear protection
  • Domestic: acoustic absorbers in appliances, volume limiters, selection of quieter appliances, ear defenders
  • Evaluation: which approaches are most effective / cost-effective; conflicts (airports near cities for convenience); legislation and monitoring (Leq 57 dB contours) needed to enforce
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • The area within which the average (equivalent continuous) sound level between 07:00 and 23:00 in summer exceeds 57 dB - taken as the onset of significant community annoyance
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 286-295; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.13.
Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss the extent to which the study of ozone depletion illustrates how scientific investigation can lead to the successful solution of an environmental problem.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Stages of investigation: identifying the issue (Halley Station UV data, Dobson unit measurements), hypothesis formation (Rowland-Molina 1974: persistence, UV dissociation, chlorine reactions), data collection (ground, satellite, balloon and aircraft air sampling detecting ClO), analysis of spatial, temporal and altitude variability, proposal of solutions, enactment
  • Why Antarctica: low stratospheric temperatures, ice crystals, stratospheric clouds, polar vortex, spring sunlight
  • Effects of UVB: skin cancer, cataracts, DNA damage, plant and marine damage - motivating action
  • Solutions: Vienna Convention, Montreal Protocol - phase-out and bans, alternative processes (pump sprays), alternative materials (HCFCs, HFCs, hydrocarbons, HFAs), collection and incineration of CFCs, fund for developing countries
  • Evaluation of success: ozone hole area / depth stabilising, CFC levels falling; limitations - persistence, remaining ODSs, HFCs as greenhouse gases, decades to full recovery
  • Comparison with other atmospheric problems (climate change, acid rain) - why ozone was easier; role of peer review, international cooperation, availability of substitutes
  • Conclusion on the extent to which it is a model for other issues
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 108-113; spec 3.2.1.3.
[25 marks]11.2Discuss the extent to which energy conservation technologies could reduce the need for new energy supplies in a developed country.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Scale of the problem: how energy is used (transport, buildings, industry, water); links between affluence and per capita use

  • Limits: rebound effect, cost and payback, existing building stock, embodied energy of replacements, behaviour
  • Evaluation with quantitative examples; comparison with the cost of new supply; conclusion that conservation is the cheapest first step but cannot alone meet demand growth
