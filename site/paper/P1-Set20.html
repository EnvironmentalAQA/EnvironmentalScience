Question 110 marks · Atmospheric pollution: smoke, acid rain, NOx, hydrocarbons, CO, smogs · spec 3.4.3.2.2-3.4.3.2.3 · Genn pp. 252-253 · AIR-04
[2 marks]01.1Describe how sulfur can be removed from coal before combustion.
[3 marks]01.2Describe the process of dry flue gas desulfurisation and explain how the product can be used.
[2 marks]01.3In wet flue gas desulfurisation the flue gases are bubbled through sodium sulfite solution. Explain one advantage of this method over dry FGD.
[3 marks]01.4Explain how each of the following reduces NOx emissions.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (2 marks)
  • Most sulfur in coal is present as (solid) iron pyrites (FeS2)
  • Coal is crushed and put in a stream of flowing water: the coal is carried away while the denser pyrites are left behind (washing / streaming)
/ 2
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Flue gases are passed through a bed of crushed calcium carbonate (limestone)
  • Sulfur dioxide reacts (with oxygen and calcium carbonate) to form solid calcium sulfate (and carbon dioxide)
  • If the gases have already been cleaned of smoke, the calcium sulfate is pure enough to be sold as gypsum for building plaster
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • The sodium hydrogen sulfite formed can be heated to regenerate sodium sulfite (re-used) and release concentrated sulfur dioxide
  • The pure sulfur dioxide is a valuable raw material for making sulfur / sulfuric acid, so a waste becomes a resource
/ 2
01.4 (3 marks)
  • Fluidised bed: increased surface area allows rapid combustion at lower temperatures so less nitrogen and oxygen react to form NOx (production prevented)
  • Catalytic converter: platinum / palladium catalyst chemically reduces NOx back to nitrogen and oxygen gases in the exhaust
  • Urea sprayed into the hot flue / exhaust gases reacts with NOx, converting it to nitrogen, carbon dioxide and water
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 252-253; AQA spec 3.4.3.2.2-3.4.3.2.3.
Question 25 marks · Thermal & oil pollution · spec 3.4.3.2.6-3.4.3.2.7 · Genn pp. 254-262 · WPOL-06

02.1 (1 marks)
  • B - Steam washing (hot water / steam jets kill organisms in the sediment)

  • Chemicals cause the oil molecules to join together / solidify
  • Producing more solid material that can be collected / lifted more easily than liquid oil
/ 2

Question 310 marks · Global climate change · spec 3.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 94-96 · CC-11

Figure 1 shows the monthly flow of a river in South America that is fed partly by glacier meltwater, in 1990 and 2020. The glacier feeding the river shrank considerably between the two dates.

Figure 1
[3 marks]03.1Compare the river flow in 1990 and 2020.
[3 marks]03.2Explain how the shrinking of the glacier explains the differences.
[2 marks]03.3Suggest two problems the change in flow causes for people living downstream.
[2 marks]03.4Explain why a glacier can be extending down its valley yet still be losing ice volume.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (3 marks)
  • 2020 has higher peak flow in the wet season (32 vs 22 m3 s-1 in March)
  • 2020 has much lower dry-season flow (2 vs 9 m3 s-1 in July / August)
  • Flow is much more variable / seasonal in 2020; the river almost dries up
/ 3
03.2 (3 marks)
  • In 1990 snow / ice stored wet-season precipitation and released it gradually as meltwater during the dry season, evening out the flow
  • With less ice, precipitation now falls as rain / melts immediately and runs off quickly, increasing wet-season flow and flood risk
  • Little stored ice remains to melt in the dry season so flow falls very low
/ 3
03.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Water shortages for irrigation / drinking / HEP in the dry season
  • Flooding in the wet season
  • Sudden floods from ice-lake outbursts (glacial lake outburst floods)
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Surface meltwater flows down cracks to the base, lubricating the ice so it slides faster; if this exceeds the faster melting at the front, the front advances
  • But if snowfall at the source has not increased, total volume falls; both retreating and extending glaciers can indicate warming
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 94-96; AQA spec 3.2.1.2.
Question 410 marks · Fossil fuels & nuclear power: sustainability and new technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 187, 190 · FN-03
[4 marks]04.1Describe coal gasification and coal liquefaction and explain how each increases the usefulness of coal.
[2 marks]04.2Describe what methane hydrate is and where it is found.
[4 marks]04.3Describe two proposed methods of extracting methane from hydrate deposits and explain two environmental risks.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (4 marks)
  • Gasification: coal (including deposits too deep to mine) is burnt underground under controlled conditions to produce fuel gases (hydrogen, carbon monoxide, methane)
  • Allows deep coal to be exploited and gas can be piped / used in gas turbines or for hydrogen with CCS
  • Liquefaction: coal is converted to liquid hydrocarbons directly with solvents or via gasification and chemical conversion
  • Provides liquid fuels for vehicles / aircraft that solid coal cannot supply
/ 4
04.2 (2 marks)
  • A solid, ice-like crystalline structure in which methane is trapped within water ice
  • Found at low temperatures (polar regions / permafrost) or high pressure in ocean sediments around continental margins
/ 2
04.3 (4 marks)
  • Water heating: hot water pumped into sediments melts the hydrate releasing methane
  • Depressurisation: drilling reduces pressure so methane dissociates from the crystals
  • CO2 injection: CO2 bonds more strongly to the ice and displaces methane (could double as CCS)
  • Risks: methane not collected escapes to the atmosphere - powerful greenhouse gas / positive feedback; seabed ecology poorly understood; sediment instability / landslides
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 187, 190; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 510 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 203-205 · REN-03

A rural community in Pakistan is considering a low-head helical (Archimedes screw) turbine on a river rather than joining a high-head dam scheme.

[5 marks]05.1Explain why low-head turbines are more suitable than high-head HEP schemes for many rural areas.
[1 mark]05.2Suggest one reason why such schemes may not be installed even at suitable sites.
[2 marks]05.3The maximum flow rate at the site is 6.5 m3 s-1 giving an output of 100 kW at 90% turbine efficiency. At 10% of maximum flow the efficiency falls to 40%. Calculate the output at 10% of maximum flow.
[2 marks]05.4Outline two ways a high-head reservoir can affect the river downstream.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (5 marks)
  • No large dam / reservoir needed so no flooding of farmland, homes or habitats and no barrier across the whole river (run-of-river / micro-hydro diverts part of the flow)
  • Suitable for rivers with a small drop in height and lower flow rates that are common in lowland rural areas
  • Small scale matches local demand; electricity used locally without long transmission lines / grid connection
  • Low construction cost and simple technology maintained by the community
  • Helical turbines tolerate high turbidity / debris without damage and fish pass through unharmed; Kaplan turbines with adjustable blades cope with variable flow
/ 5
05.2 (1 marks)
  • Lack of capital / finance, technical knowledge or grid access; seasonal drying of rivers; conflicts with irrigation water; planning / land ownership
/ 1
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Output is proportional to flow x efficiency: 100 x 0.10 x (40 / 90)
  • = 4.4 kW (accept 4.4-4.5)
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Constant regulated flow removes seasonal extremes: dry-season sandbanks used by nesting birds / turtles lost; high flows that clear gravel spawning beds lost; floodplain no longer flooded
  • Sudden flow increases for peak generation raise turbidity, wash away species, reduce light for photosynthesis
  • Sediment trapped so less nutrient replenishment downstream; barrier to migrating fish (salmon, sturgeon) and isolation of populations
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 203-205; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 610 marks · Scientific methodologies & sampling design · spec 3.7.1 · Genn pp. 390-393 · RM-01

A student investigated whether the number of earthworms per m2 changes with distance from a hedge across an arable field.

[2 marks]06.1Explain why systematic sampling along a transect is more appropriate than random sampling for this investigation.
[4 marks]06.2Describe how a preliminary study could be used to decide the distance between sampling sites and the number of samples at each site.
[4 marks]06.3Describe how earthworms could be extracted from a 0.5 m x 0.5 m sample area and give two limitations of the method.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (2 marks)
  • There is an environmental gradient (distance from the hedge / changing conditions) so samples must be taken at regular intervals along it to detect the change
  • Random sampling might leave gaps / miss the pattern; systematic sampling avoids bias while covering the gradient
/ 2
06.2 (4 marks)
  • Sample at close intervals (eg every 1 m) first and see at what spacing the variations are still detected without unnecessary data; wider intervals (eg 4 m) may miss peaks and troughs
  • Take increasing numbers of samples at one site and calculate the running mean; the number needed is where the mean stops fluctuating / stabilises
  • Calculate standard deviation to assess scatter: more variable data need more samples
  • Test the method (extraction technique, timing) for reliability before the main study
/ 4
06.3 (4 marks)
  • Remove surface vegetation; pour an irritant solution (dilute mustard / detergent in water) onto the area until saturated (and around it), keep adding as it infiltrates
  • Collect worms that come to the surface for a standardised time; count / identify / weigh
  • Limitations: solution may not percolate impermeable soils or reach deep worms; worms may move sideways or downwards; some may die or not respond; slow-moving worms missed
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 390-393; AQA spec 3.7.1.
Question 710 marks · Fluctuations in supply/demand & energy storage · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 222-224 · EST-05
[2 marks]07.1Explain why compressed air energy storage with heat recovery can achieve over 90% efficiency.
[2 marks]07.2Explain why large-volume hot water stores lose heat more slowly per unit of stored energy than small ones.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why molten salt rather than water is used to store heat from CSP plants.
[2 marks]07.4Explain how high thermal mass building materials reduce energy use.
[2 marks]07.5Describe one other energy storage technology.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (2 marks)
  • Surplus energy drives a pump compressing air stored in underground caverns (salt mines); releasing it drives machinery / turbines
  • Compression produces a lot of heat; storing this heat (hot oil / molten salt) and returning it when the air expands recovers energy that would otherwise be lost
/ 2
07.2 (2 marks)
  • As volume increases the surface area : volume ratio decreases
  • Heat is lost through the surface so there is less surface per unit of stored heat; very large stores can carry summer solar heat into winter (inter-seasonal storage)
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Potassium nitrate has a much higher boiling point so it stores heat at up to 550 °C without pressurisation
  • Water could only reach such temperatures under high pressure, increasing costs; high temperature needed to raise steam for turbines
/ 2
07.4 (2 marks)
  • Materials with high specific heat capacity (concrete, water) warm and cool slowly
  • They absorb heat in hot weather reducing overheating / air conditioning, and release it as the weather cools, delaying the need for heating
/ 2
07.5 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Flywheels: rotating mass stores kinetic energy for short-term smoothing / generation
  • Supercapacitors: electrochemical capacitors with 10-100 times the energy density of conventional capacitors; possible future large-scale electricity storage
  • Thermal storage in molten salt / high-volume tanks; hydrogen / P2G; batteries
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 222-224; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 810 marks · Importance & features of energy resources · spec 3.3.2 · Genn pp. 175-177 · ENF-03
[3 marks]08.1Distinguish between renewable and depletable energy resources, giving an example of a resource that is both renewable and depletable.
[3 marks]08.2Explain why the abundance of an energy resource is not the same as the amount available for use. Give two examples.
[4 marks]08.3Describe the locational constraints of two named renewable energy resources.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Renewable: re-forms naturally relatively quickly so use does not necessarily reduce future availability (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biofuel)
  • Depletable: use can reduce future availability - all non-renewables (fossil fuels, uranium) plus renewables exploited unsustainably
  • Wood / biofuels are renewable but depletable if forests are felled faster than they regrow
/ 3
08.2 (3 marks)
  • Other factors (technology, location, cost) restrict how much can be harnessed
  • Examples: coal too deep to mine; winds high above the ground / far offshore where turbines cannot be placed; waves in deep water where equipment cannot be anchored; low-intensity sunlight that cannot produce high temperatures
  • New technologies or higher prices may make these usable in future
/ 3
08.3 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Wind: strong reliable winds - shallow seas, open plains, uplands; low land-use conflict (not near urban areas, ecologically sensitive or scenic areas); near the grid
  • HEP: high reliable rainfall, large catchment, narrow valley for a small dam with a large basin, impermeable stable bedrock
  • Solar: high light intensity, low cloud cover
  • Wave: coasts with strong reliable prevailing winds and a long fetch
  • Tidal: large tidal range, coastal features (estuaries, channels) that focus the flow
  • Geothermal: hot rocks near the surface / recent volcanic activity
  • Biofuels: nearby farmland / forest / urban waste to avoid transporting bulky low-density fuel
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 175-177; AQA spec 3.3.2.
Question 910 marks · Pesticides · spec 3.4.3.2.8 · Genn pp. 263-265 · PEST-02

Table 1 shows some properties of four groups of insecticide.

Table 1

Organochlorines (eg DDT)HighLowHighHigh
Organophosphates (eg malathion)HighHighLowLow
PyrethroidsHighLowLowLow
NeonicotinoidsHighLowModerateLow (water soluble)
[4 marks]09.1Use Table 1 to explain why organochlorine insecticides caused the decline of birds of prey such as the peregrine falcon in the 1950s and 1960s.
[2 marks]09.2Organophosphates replaced organochlorines for many uses. Use Table 1 to give one advantage and one disadvantage of this change.
[1 mark]09.3Explain why pyrethroids should not be used near rivers or fish farms.
[3 marks]09.4Describe how the properties of neonicotinoids allow them to be used as a seed coating, and explain why this method of application reduces harm to non-target species.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • High persistence means DDT remains in the environment for years / decades, so it has time to disperse and be taken up
  • High liposolubility means it dissolves in fat and is stored in body tissues rather than excreted (bioaccumulation)
  • Concentration increases at each trophic level as predators eat many contaminated prey (biomagnification)
  • Birds of prey are at the top of the food chain so receive the highest concentrations
  • Sub-lethal doses caused thin eggshells that broke / reduced breeding success; higher doses caused sterility or death
/ 4
09.2 (2 marks)
  • Advantage: low persistence / low liposolubility so they do not bioaccumulate or biomagnify / break down quickly
  • Disadvantage: high mammalian toxicity so farm workers / humans are at risk of acute poisoning (neurotoxin inhibiting acetylcholinesterase); chronic effects (memory loss, depression); suspected carcinogens
/ 2
09.3 (1 marks)
  • Pyrethroids are highly toxic to fish (even though mammal toxicity is low)
/ 1
09.4 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Neonicotinoids are water soluble so they are absorbed by the roots (from the soil) and translocated through the plant (systemic)
  • They are moderately persistent so remain in the soil long enough to be absorbed
  • Seed coating avoids spraying so no spray drift / no dust onto flowers or surrounding habitats
  • So bees / pollinators are less exposed than when the crop is sprayed
/ 3

Question 1010 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3 · Genn pp. 130-147 · MIN-10
[9 marks]10.1As high-grade mineral deposits become depleted, discuss the strategies that could be used to secure future supplies of metals.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by the stock (resource base) of a mineral.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Context: Lasky's principle - vast quantities exist at low grade; reserves vs resource; COOG affected by price, technology, energy costs
  • Improved exploration: higher resolution multispectral satellite sensors, IR spectroscopy, gravimetry, magnetometry, seismic, resistivity, portable field analysis, targeted trial drilling
  • Mechanisation allowing deeper underground mines and larger open-cast mines
  • Low-energy extraction of low-grade ores: bioleaching with acidophilic bacteria and fungi, phytomining, iron displacement, bacterial adsorption of rare earths, polymer adsorption from seawater
  • Previously inaccessible deposits: polymetallic nodules, deep sea sediments, Antarctica (currently protected) - environmental and legal issues
  • Extending use: recycling (advantages, difficulties), Cradle to Cradle design, product lifetime extension, substitution of scarce materials, circular economy
  • Evaluation: environmental impacts vs benefits; energy costs; economics; which strategies are most sustainable; conclusion
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • All of the material that exists in the lithosphere, including that which will never be technologically or economically exploitable
/ 1
