Question 110 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.5 · Genn pp. 393-394, 400-402 · MON-01

A conservation team is planning to protect a population of an endangered antelope in a reserve.

[8 marks]01.1Explain why each of the following features of the population should be measured before a conservation strategy is decided.
[2 marks]01.2Describe how the population size of the antelope could be estimated.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (8 marks)
  • Size: shows whether the population is viable / how urgent action is; sets a baseline to judge success; needed to estimate carrying capacity and sustainable off-take
  • Distribution: shows which habitats / areas are used and whether the population is fragmented into non-viable isolated groups needing corridors; identifies key areas to protect
  • Survival rate (mortality): identifies the life stage and causes of death (predation, disease, poaching) so management targets the limiting factor
  • Age structure: many young indicates a growing population; few young / ageing population indicates breeding failure or high juvenile mortality even if adults are numerous; k-selected species need breeding adults protected
/ 8
01.2 (2 marks)
  • Direct counts from vehicles / aircraft / drones along transects, or camera traps with individual recognition
  • Sub-sample counts in known areas scaled up to the whole reserve; mark-release-recapture (Lincoln index) for smaller species
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 393-394, 400-402; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.5.
Question 210 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 69-71 · HAB-12
[4 marks]02.1Describe how each of the following threatens Antarctica.
[6 marks]02.2Describe the main features of the Antarctic Treaty (1959) and the regulations that protect Antarctica.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (4 marks)
  • Warming melts ice shelves that hold back glaciers and lubricates ice flow so land ice reaches the sea faster (Antarctic Peninsula most affected); sea ice loss removes krill habitat and lowers albedo
  • Raised UV harms planktonic algae, krill and fish / sea urchin larvae in surface waters (land animals protected by fur / feathers)
  • Disturbance of wildlife, introduction of non-indigenous species and pathogens, fuel and sewage pollution; visitor numbers rising rapidly at a few landing sites
  • Reserves of coal, oil, gold, uranium, platinum etc; pressure to exploit will rise as reserves elsewhere deplete though currently banned and operations extremely difficult
/ 4
02.2 (6 marks)
  • Treaty signed by 52 countries including all active there; no military activity (except supporting research), no nuclear explosions or radioactive waste dumping
  • No commercial mineral exploitation
  • Fisheries regulated by CCAMLR: krill and some fish exploited, fisheries closed if over-exploited; no commercial exploitation of mammals
  • Tourism control: no accommodation on land (wastes stay on ships), one ship per site with maximum 100 ashore, trained guides, keep 5 m from wildlife, no litter, cleaned protective clothing to prevent introductions
  • Waste management: all wastes removed (fuel containers, batteries, plastics, oil); sewage only discharged where sea currents disperse it; old waste removed unless removal causes more damage
  • Only long-term residents are research scientists and support staff; strict regulation of research impacts
/ 6
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 69-71; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 310 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2.3 · Genn pp. 356-359 · AQUA-03
[8 marks]03.1Explain how each of the following environmental impacts of salmon farming arises and how it can be reduced.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why culling seals and cormorants near fish farms is controversial and give an alternative.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (8 marks)
  • Carnivores fed pellets of sandeels, herring, anchovy; overfishing sandeels reduces puffins and terns; reduced by more plant protein / vegetable oil in pellets or farming herbivores (tilapia)
  • High density spreads lice (which spread to wild fish) and infections; pesticides (organophosphates, pyrethroids, hydrogen peroxide) kill wildlife; antibiotics breed resistant bacteria; reduced by lower densities, single-age tanks with flow from young to old, wrasse as biological control, warm-water washes, circulating flow
  • Escaped farmed fish breed with wild fish introducing disadvantageous genes (bright colour), or non-indigenous escapees colonise as predators / competitors; reduced by better cage design, sterile triploid fish (pressure-treated eggs), species that cannot breed locally (Japanese oysters in UK)
  • Faeces and uneaten food decompose causing deoxygenation under cages; reduced by monitored feeding, siting cages in strong currents, effluent treatment / reed beds for freshwater farms, IMTA with seaweed and shellfish absorbing wastes
/ 8
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Predators are killed under licence to protect stock, reducing wild populations of protected / valued species
  • Better cage designs, netting, fencing, bird scarers exclude predators without killing
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 356-359; AQA spec 3.5.2.3.

  • Both: hardwood timber (teak, mahogany) from clear-felled mixed forest often not replanted; urban expansion

  • Improved access makes it easier to remove timber and farm produce, so clearance spreads along them
/ 2
04.4 (1 marks)
  • Conifers grow quickly so plantations are replanted after harvest and yield a saleable crop within decades; slow-growing hardwoods (teak, mahogany) take too long for commercial plantations so they are taken from natural forest that is clear-felled and not replanted

Question 610 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.3 · Genn pp. 18-19 · BIOV-03
[6 marks]06.1Explain how living organisms provide each of the following ecosystem services.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why Darwin's orchid of Madagascar depends on a single moth species, and why such specialisation is both an advantage and a risk.
[2 marks]06.3Explain why elephants are particularly important seed dispersers.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (6 marks)
  • Photosynthesis and aerobic respiration balance O2 and CO2 in a dynamic equilibrium; evapotranspiration adds water vapour forming clouds and rain; microbes drive the nitrogen and other biogeochemical cycles so wastes do not accumulate and nutrients are recycled
  • Detritivores, fungi and bacteria decompose dead organic matter releasing nutrients and forming humus that binds soil; organic acids weather rock forming new soil; roots bind soil, reducing erosion and regulating water flow / flooding
  • Species provide food, pollination (bees, moths, birds, bats - more reliable than wind, especially in forests), seed dispersal (animals carry larger seeds to suitable sites, faeces as fertiliser, gut passage stimulates germination) and habitat provision (trees as nest sites and canopy microclimate; mollusc shells for hermit crabs)
/ 6
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Its nectar is at the end of a 30 cm tube reached only by the long tongue of the sphinx moth, which carries pollen only between orchids of the same species
  • Advantage: no pollen wasted on other species and the moth has an exclusive food source; risk: if either species declines the other cannot survive
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • They are not ruminants so seeds are not destroyed by repeated chewing / pass through intact; they eat the fruit of most tree species
  • Not territorial so they carry seeds over large distances / areas
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 18-19; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.3.
Question 710 marks · Sampling techniques & measuring abiotic factors · spec 3.7.2.1 · Genn pp. 397-399 · RM-07
[6 marks]07.1Complete Table 1 to show a suitable sampling technique for each group of organisms and one limitation of the technique.
Table 1
OrganismsTechniqueOne limitation
Invertebrates active on the ground surface at night
Invertebrates living in leaf litter
Invertebrates on tree branches
[2 marks]07.2Explain why a light trap cannot be used to prove that a moth species is absent from a site.
[2 marks]07.3Explain how a motor-driven suction sampler can give a population density, whereas a pooter cannot.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Ground surface: pitfall trap (container sunk level with the surface, cover, checked after 24 h) - only mobile surface animals; trapped predators eat others; preservative attracts / repels; escapes
  • Leaf litter: Tullgren funnel (sample on mesh under a light; heat / light drive invertebrates down into a container for a standard time) - only species repelled by heat / light; some die or stop moving; large ones cannot pass the mesh
  • Branches: beating tray (branch beaten a set number of times over a sheet) - hard to standardise vigour; some species not dislodged / fly away; high branches unreachable
/ 6
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Moths only fly as adults for part of the year and activity depends on weather / season, so a species may be present but not caught
  • Brightness / wavelength of the lamp affects attractiveness so catches vary between traps
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • The area of ground covered by the suction pipe is known so invertebrates per m2 can be calculated
  • A pooter is only a handling / collection device for individuals already found, not a sampling method
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 397-399; AQA spec 3.7.2.1.

  • Direct measurements of past conditions cannot be taken, so indirect evidence that is related to the condition must be used

  • Past temperatures
  • Composition of the past atmosphere (eg CO2 / methane concentrations)

Question 1010 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.3-3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 32-49 · CBR-09
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the relative importance of ex-situ and in-situ methods in the conservation of endangered species.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by rewilding.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • In-situ: species remain in natural conditions with the whole community and inter-species relationships; protected areas (SSSI, NNR, SAC, SPA, Ramsar, MCZ), land ownership, agri-environment schemes, habitat creation and design (area, shape, corridors, diversity), management of abiotic and biotic factors, plagioclimax maintenance, population control, reintroductions and rewilding (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish beavers)
  • Limits of in-situ: external threats (pollution, climate change), enforcement, cost, conflicts with land use, small fragmented populations
  • Ex-situ: captive breeding when in-situ is impossible or insufficient; selection criteria; difficulties keeping and breeding (habitat size, food, relationships, breeding triggers, group dynamics, gene pool / inbreeding, hybridisation); techniques (stud books, cryopreservation, AI, embryo transfer, micropropagation, cloning); seed banks; successes (Hawaiian goose, Iberian lynx, red kite, large blue)
  • Limits of ex-situ: expensive, few species, loss of wild behaviour, small gene pools, release requires suitable habitat and post-release support (hard vs soft release, monitoring)
  • Judgement: ex-situ is a temporary safeguard / support that only succeeds if in-situ habitat exists to return to; both needed, in-situ fundamental
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Restoring natural processes / communities to a habitat changed by humans, often by reintroducing missing (keystone) species and removing human management such as grazing
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 32-49; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.3-3.1.2.3.4.
