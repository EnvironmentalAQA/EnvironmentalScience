Question 310 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 52-55 · HAB-03
[4 marks]03.1Explain why tropical rainforests have such high biodiversity.
[2 marks]03.2Explain why rainforest species may be particularly vulnerable to environmental change.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why rainforest plants and animals such as frogs are a rich source of medicines.
[2 marks]03.4Explain the importance of tropical rainforest for the hydrological cycle and for soils.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Stable tropical climate over long periods gave time for species to evolve and adapt to local abiotic and biotic factors; many rare species with small ranges
  • High light, warm temperatures and high rainfall all year allow continuous photosynthesis and growth, supporting rich food webs with no seasonal food shortage (no hibernation / migration)
  • Stratification (emergent, canopy, understorey, shrub, ground layers) and epiphytes create many niches; intense competition for light drives specialisation
  • Biotic pressures (predation, inter-species relationships) drive evolution of specialised pollination / seed dispersal relationships; plants flower and fruit at different times
/ 4
03.2 (2 marks)
  • Adapted to stable conditions so they have narrow ranges of tolerance; a small change can move conditions outside the range
  • Many species are rare with small ranges / small populations
/ 2
03.3 (2 marks)
  • No cold winter kills herbivores so plants defend themselves with toxic chemicals (alkaloids) that are medicinally active in controlled doses (quinine, diosgenin, curare)
  • Animals without teeth / claws (frogs, reptiles) rely on chemical defences or venoms (fer-de-lance venom - captopril)
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Over half the Amazon's rainfall comes from transpiration within the forest; clearance reduces downwind rainfall
  • Rapid decay recycles nutrients so soils are shallow / poor; canopy and roots prevent erosion of the thin soil - clearance leads to rapid erosion and infertility
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 52-55; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.

[4 marks]04.3Describe how the International Whaling Commission regulates whaling.

04.1 (1 marks)
  • (240 000 - 2 000) / 240 000 x 100 = 99.2%
/ 1
04.2 (3 marks)
  • Whales are k-selected: slow growing, late maturing, one calf every few years, so populations recover very slowly
  • Technology (harpoon cannons, steam / factory ships) allowed catches far above the sustainable yield within decades
  • No regulation / open access to the ocean; whales were valuable (oil, spermaceti) and easily found
/ 3
04.3 (4 marks)
  • Moratorium on commercial whaling since 1986 until populations recover
  • Total protection for certain species; whale sanctuaries
  • Limits on numbers and sizes taken; protection of suckling mothers and calves
  • Research into whale biology and threats (ship strikes, net entanglement); exceptions: aboriginal subsistence quotas (eg Inuit bowheads), 'scientific' permits (Japan - ruled not research in 2014), Norway / Iceland set their own quotas
/ 4
04.4 (2 marks)
  • ITTO's 1990 target of trade only from sustainably managed forests by 2000 was not met and little progress since
  • Timber comes from many countries / private land with weak enforcement, high demand and alternative land uses; whaling involves few nations and a single treaty body
/ 2

Question 610 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-05
[8 marks]06.1Explain how each of the following reduces the environmental impact of fishing.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why catch quotas work well for pelagic species such as mackerel but poorly in mixed demersal fisheries.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (8 marks)
  • Normal diagonal mesh closes as the net fills / stretches so small fish cannot escape; square-mesh panels do not close so juveniles escape to grow and breed; TEDs let turtles out; larger mesh sizes generally
  • Minimum size lets fish reach breeding age before capture (eg UK lobster minimum carapace above breeding size, cod 35 cm); maximum size / V-notching egg-carrying females protects large, highly fecund breeding adults (Hebridean lobsters over 145 mm, Nassau grouper)
  • NTZs (Lundy, Lamlash Bay, Chagos, Great Barrier Reef) protect breeding populations and habitats; surplus young spill over and increase surrounding catches; damaged communities recover
  • Closed seasons, ideally in the breeding season, let fish grow and breed; fishermen may oppose because shoaling breeders are easy to catch
/ 8
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Pelagic species form single-species shoals so fishing stops when the quota is reached
  • In mixed fisheries many species are caught together; once one species' quota is reached it must be discarded (dead) while fishing for others continues
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 344-348; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 710 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4 · Genn pp. 47-49 · CBR-08
[4 marks]07.1Describe coppicing and explain how it increases the biodiversity of a woodland.
[4 marks]07.2Explain how the reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park increased biodiversity.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why culling or removing species may be necessary in habitat management.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • Trees (hazel, oak) are cut to ground level every 3-30 years and allowed to regrow from the stump
  • About 0.5 ha cut each year on a rotation (eg 8 years) so the wood is a patchwork of areas of different ages
  • Each age has its own community: newly cut areas get high light for wildflowers and butterflies; older areas give dense cover for nesting birds / dormice
  • Pollarding cuts above browsing height so deer / livestock cannot eat the regrowth
/ 4
07.2 (4 marks)
  • Wolves (top predator, exterminated by 1926) reduced deer populations and changed their behaviour
  • Fewer young trees eaten so trees and riverside vegetation recovered
  • More trees allowed beaver populations to increase; beaver dams increased wetland area
  • Wetland species (birds, fish, invertebrates) became more common; overall biodiversity rose, though wolves cause problems for livestock farmers
/ 4
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Undesirable species (introduced predators / competitors, or natural succession species such as scrub, conifers in the New Forest heathland) reduce survival of the target species
  • Removal restores suitable conditions (eg heathland for sand lizards and smooth snakes) or reduces predation / competition
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 47-49; AQA spec 3.1.3.3-3.1.3.4.
Question 810 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 24-25 · HUM-04
[2 marks]08.1Explain why introduced species often have a greater effect on island communities than on mainland communities.
[6 marks]08.2For each of the following, describe an example of an introduced species and explain its effect on an indigenous species.
[2 marks]08.3Explain how hybridisation with an introduced species threatens the red deer and the Scottish wildcat.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (2 marks)
  • Island communities evolved from few colonising species in isolation, often without mammal predators or large herbivores
  • So indigenous species lack adaptations (eg ground-nesting birds, flightlessness) to survive introduced cats, rats, pigs, goats
/ 2
08.2 (6 marks)
  • Competitor: grey squirrel (North America) out-competes red squirrel - digests acorns, larger, wins nest sites; rhododendron shades ground and releases toxins preventing native regrowth
  • Predator: American mink (escaped fur farms) reduced water vole range; cane toads in Australia; cats / rats / pigs on islands eating ground-nesting birds; Nile perch in Lake Victoria ate cichlids to extinction
  • Pathogen: squirrel pox virus carried by grey squirrels kills red squirrels; crayfish plague carried by signal crayfish kills white-clawed crayfish; Dutch elm disease, ash dieback, sudden oak death imported on plants / soil
/ 6
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Closely related introduced species (sika deer; domestic cats) cross-breed producing fertile hybrids
  • The natural gene pool is changed / diluted with genes it would not naturally contain, so the pure indigenous species may disappear
/ 2

Question 910 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 369, 386-387 · FOR-05

The Rio Bravo Conservation and Management Area in Belize exploits mahogany under a sustainable management plan while conserving a rainforest of over 200 tree, 400 bird and 70 mammal species.

[5 marks]09.1Describe five features of the timber harvesting at Rio Bravo that make it sustainable.
[3 marks]09.2Explain why involving local communities is essential to the success of the reserve.
[2 marks]09.3Explain why selective logging is better for wildlife than clear-felling, and give one disadvantage.
Show mark scheme
09.1 (5 marks)
  • Harvest rate below the maximum sustainable yield; only economically valuable species (mahogany) selectively logged, not clear-felled
  • Trees felled only where other mahoganies stand upwind so seeds recolonise the clearing; large seed trees and small trees left to grow
  • Buffer zones with no harvesting or machinery near rivers / wetlands
  • Restricted machinery and planned track routes re-used to avoid damaging roots of retained / rare / fruit trees
  • Each felled tree tagged and tracked to the retailer for FSC accreditation; rangers control illegal logging; tree nurseries replant over-exploited and ecologically important species
/ 5
09.2 (3 marks)
  • The reserve removed traditional uses (fuelwood, timber, farming clearings, hunting, medicines) so people would otherwise resent / ignore it
  • Employment as rangers, forestry and tourism workers; ecotourists use local restaurants, produce and souvenirs; offcuts given to local carvers
  • Benefits give the community an incentive to protect the forest; carbon sequestration payments from companies fund management
/ 3
09.3 (2 marks)
  • Removing individual trees leaves small clearings so remaining populations survive nearby and the forest structure / microclimate is kept; timber resource used over a longer period
  • Disadvantage: more labour-intensive and expensive; more tracks needed per tree
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 369, 386-387; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.
Question 1010 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.1 · Genn pp. 375-379 · SUS-06
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the extent to which the principles of the circular economy can be applied to three of the following: water supplies, mineral supplies, waste management, pollution control, energy supplies, food production.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by industrial symbiosis.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Water: catchment management, conservation, metering, greywater, re-use of treated effluent, aquifer recharge, sustainable urban drainage - largely cyclical already; limits in arid regions
  • Minerals: exploiting low-grade ores with low-temperature processes (bioleaching), extending product lifespans, repair / re-use / repurpose / recycle, design for disassembly, reduced packaging; limits: dispersal, alloys, energy of collection
  • Waste management: repair, re-use, refurbish, recycle, composting and anaerobic digestion returning nutrients, minimal landfill, energy from residual waste; EU targets on packaging, batteries, WEEE
  • Pollution control: shift from post-production treatment to non-release - fuel desulfurisation not FGD, fuel cells / electric vehicles not combustion, industrial symbiosis (Kalundborg: waste heat, gypsum, fly ash, yeast used by neighbours); limits for legacy pollution
  • Energy: renewables, storage, energy density technologies, low-temperature manufacturing, CHP; limits of intermittency, transport fuels
  • Food: natural processes for nutrients (legumes, organic matter), pest control (IPM), soil biota, agri-environment schemes, IMTA aquaculture; tension with maximum yield for a growing population
  • Land uses supporting ecosystems: urban green infrastructure, gardens, permeable surfaces, habitat in redeveloped docks / lagoons
  • Judgement: high applicability in some sectors (water, waste), partial in others (minerals, energy for transport); needs policy support
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Locating industries so that the wastes of one (heat, gases, by-products) become the raw materials of another, mimicking natural cycles (eg Kalundborg eco-industrial park)
/ 1

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Explain how the physical features of the Earth and the activities of living organisms have combined to create and maintain conditions suitable for life.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Physical features: mass / gravity retaining an atmosphere and giving pressure for liquid water; distance from the Sun and the Goldilocks zone; speed of rotation reducing temperature extremes; tilt producing seasons; magnetic field deflecting the solar wind; the Moon stabilising the orbit and producing tides
  • Importance of liquid water: solvent, transport, temperature control, anomalous expansion, high specific heat capacity, habitats, absorption of UV before the ozone layer
  • Development of life: Archaea at vents; photosynthesis; oxygen reacting with iron then accumulating; ozone layer allowing colonisation of land; aerobic organisms
  • Life changing conditions: carbon sequestration offsetting solar brightening, biogeochemical cycles, transpiration and rainfall, regulation of atmospheric gases
  • Methods of studying past conditions: proxy data, isotope ratios, ice cores, Mg:Ca ratios; understanding of continental drift and ocean currents
  • Links to current threats (climate change, ozone depletion) and a justified conclusion about the interdependence of physical and biological factors
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)

[25 marks]11.2Discuss how an understanding of energy flow and the manipulation of food species can be used to increase food production sustainably.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Energy flow: autotrophs and heterotrophs; losses by reflection, transmission, respiration; 100 000 to 1 000 units; few food chains beyond four levels; plant food most productive; grazing animals where crops impossible; pigs on wastes

  • Sustainability links: energy dependence, trophic level of diet, feeding 12 billion people
  • Justified conclusion on the balance between productivity, efficiency and sustainability
