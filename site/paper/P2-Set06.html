Question 110 marks · Captive breeding, habitat creation & management · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 40-42 · CBR-05

A conservation organisation is converting 700 ha of arable farmland on the coast into a wetland reserve for breeding and wintering birds (as at Wallasea Island).

[4 marks]01.1Explain why habitat area and shape are important in the design of the reserve.
[3 marks]01.2Explain how the reserve could be designed to provide a range of niches.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why some species may need to be deliberately introduced while others colonise naturally.
[1 mark]01.4Explain the value of a biological corridor linking the reserve to other wetlands.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Area must be large enough to support viable populations (breeding numbers high enough, gene pool large enough to avoid inbreeding); species higher up food chains need larger areas (lower density)
  • Large areas contain a full range of resources (eg different fruiting trees / feeding areas through the year)
  • Shape: the perimeter has edge conditions (a mix of neighbouring habitats); a compact shape maximises core habitat for species needing core conditions
  • Some species benefit from edges (higher light) or small isolated habitats (ponds too small for predatory fish suit amphibians)
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Varied topography: banks, lagoons, islands, ditches created with machinery give a range of water depths (emergent, floating-leaved, submerged plant zones)
  • Islands provide roosting / nesting sites safe from terrestrial predators (foxes)
  • Control of salinity (eg intertidal conditions for opossum shrimps feeding dunlin and avocet); scrapes for skylarks; different water depths for waders and ducks
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Mobile species (birds, flying insects) colonise easily
  • Less mobile species (many plants, amphibians, invertebrates) may not reach an isolated site so must be introduced; keystone species especially
/ 2
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Allows dispersal of surplus young, mixing of gene pools (less inbreeding) and recolonisation if a population dies out; avoids hazards (eg rope bridges for howler monkeys over roads)
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 40-42; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 210 marks · Marine productivity & fishing · spec 3.5.2.2 · Genn pp. 344-348 · FISH-06
[3 marks]02.1Describe three methods of reducing seabird by-catch in long-line fisheries.
[3 marks]02.2Explain how restricting fishing effort and restricting fishing methods reduce overfishing.
[2 marks]02.3Describe how captive rearing and release ('population seeding') can increase wild populations of lobsters or oysters.
[2 marks]02.4Explain why collecting data on planktonic fish eggs and larvae helps to prevent population collapse.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Sinkers / weighted lines so hooks sink quickly below the reach of albatrosses
  • Setting lines at night when birds are not feeding
  • Bird-scaring streamers / decoys
  • Curved hooks that catch tuna but not birds
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
  • Effort: limits on boat size, engine power, days at sea, number of licences; decommissioning payments to reduce fleet size where overfishing is unavoidable
  • Methods: bans or length limits on drift nets; bans on demersal trawling over sensitive seabed (Rockall Bank, Hatton Bank deep-water reefs)
  • Shorter trawl tows so turtles / by-catch survive (over 99% survive under 10 minutes)
/ 3
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Eggs / larvae are reared in captivity through the planktonic stage when most die (predation, failure to find habitat)
  • Juveniles are released when survival chances are good, increasing recruitment to the adult population
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
  • Egg / larval survival varies greatly between years so adult numbers do not predict recruitment
  • Several poor years would lower the MSY; egg surveys allow catches to be reduced in advance rather than after a sudden collapse
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 344-348; AQA spec 3.5.2.2.
Question 310 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 61-64 · HAB-08
[2 marks]03.1Explain how overfishing of the giant triton mollusc damaged coral reefs in Australia.
[4 marks]03.2Describe four management practices used in marine protected areas to protect coral reefs.
[2 marks]03.3Explain why deep-water coral reefs recover much more slowly from trawling damage than tropical reefs.
[2 marks]03.4Give two threats to deep-water reefs and one conservation measure.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (2 marks)
  • Giant tritons are predators of the crown-of-thorns starfish which eats coral polyps
  • Removing tritons allowed starfish populations to explode, destroying large areas of reef (food web / trophic cascade)
/ 2
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Fixed mooring buoys so boats do not anchor on coral
  • Education of divers / snorkellers not to touch coral or kick up sediment; bans on spear fishing and souvenir collection
  • Fishing controls: no-take zones (Chagos, Great Barrier Reef, Lundy, Lamlash Bay), seasons, minimum and maximum catch sizes, bans on dynamite / small-mesh nets / trawling
  • Protection of turtle nesting beaches; control of coastal development; protection of mangroves and seagrass; artificial reefs (reef balls, sunk ships / oil rigs)
/ 4
03.3 (2 marks)
  • No sunlight so no symbiotic algae; polyps rely only on captured food so have far less energy and grow very slowly
  • Damage from trawls takes hundreds of years to repair; the slow-growing, low-fecundity fish (orange roughy) they support are easily over-exploited
/ 2
03.4 (2 marks)
  • Deep-water trawling (also by-catch); oil and gas exploration in deeper water; ocean acidification
  • Protected areas banning trawling, eg Darwin Mounds SAC off NW Scotland, Oculina Bank off Florida
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 61-64; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 410 marks · The circular economy, biocapacity & ecological footprints · spec 3.6.4.1 · Genn pp. 374-375 · SUS-05
[6 marks]04.1Describe the main principles of the circular economy.
[2 marks]04.2Explain what is meant by 'optimum production rather than maximum production', using a comparison between a natural ecosystem and an agroecosystem.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why international cooperation (eg through the EU or UN) is needed to implement circular economy initiatives.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (6 marks)
  • Cycling of materials (like biogeochemical cycles) - recycling, re-use
  • Energy from renewable sources
  • Human activities should support ecosystems / conserve biodiversity
  • Separation of technical and biological materials; connected systems where the waste of one process feeds another (industrial symbiosis)
  • Diverse systems are more resistant to change
  • Design of products for extended use and end-of-life re-use; optimum rather than maximum production; technologies that improve efficiency and enable dismantling
/ 6
04.2 (2 marks)
  • Natural systems over-produce so the surplus supports the processes the system relies on: plant products feed pollinators, seed dispersers, decomposers, mycorrhizae
  • Agroecosystems aim for maximum harvested yield, removing the surplus that would maintain soil biota, pollinators and pest predators, so they need artificial inputs
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Costs of environmental measures could put companies in one country at a disadvantage against competitors in countries without them
  • A common approach gives a level playing field, coordinated monitoring and support for less successful countries
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 374-375; AQA spec 3.6.4.1.
Question 510 marks · Impacts of agriculture, social/political factors & sustainable agriculture · spec 3.5.1.3-3.5.1.4 · Genn pp. 329-333 · AGI-02
[3 marks]05.1Describe how agriculture alters the hydrological cycle.
[4 marks]05.2Explain how each of the following consumer choices can reduce the environmental impact of food production.
[3 marks]05.3Explain why post-war European governments introduced grants and guaranteed prices, and describe one environmental consequence.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • Irrigation depletes rivers and aquifers (eg Aral Sea, Ogallala); increases evaporation
  • Soil erosion and compaction reduce water retention and infiltration so runoff and river flow fluctuations increase
  • Evapotranspiration changes: increased in irrigated arid areas, decreased where forests were cleared
/ 3
05.2 (4 marks)
  • Seasonal / local: avoids energy for heating, lighting greenhouses out of season and long-distance transport (food miles) and the pollution this causes
  • Organic: uses natural processes (rotation, legumes, manure, biological control, predator habitats) instead of artificial fertilisers and pesticides, so less pollution, energy use and harm to non-target species; free-range and Fairtrade also accepted for welfare / social reasons
/ 4
05.3 (3 marks)
  • Food shortages after 1945 (dependence on US food aid); farmers could not afford loans to invest so grants funded machinery, drainage, hedgerow removal, liming, improved livestock
  • Guaranteed market / price support: government bought surpluses to hold prices up (and sold stores in poor years) giving farmers security to increase output
  • Consequences: loss of hedgerows and wetlands, intensification, pesticide and fertiliser pollution; later food surpluses (butter mountains) that could not be sold without harming LEDC producers
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 329-333; AQA spec 3.5.1.3-3.5.1.4.

Question 710 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 22-23 · HUM-02
[6 marks]07.1Explain how each of the following changes in abiotic factors caused by human activities can make a habitat unsuitable for species living there.
[2 marks]07.2Explain how increased water turbidity caused by ploughing, mining or dredging harms aquatic organisms.
[2 marks]07.3Explain why species that were already rare are most likely to be lost when abiotic conditions change.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (6 marks)
  • Drainage of wetlands / lowering water tables by groundwater abstraction dries out wetlands; HEP schemes alter river levels flooding sandbank nests of turtles / birds; species with narrow tolerance (specialists) affected first; sundews lost when drained soil becomes aerobic / nitrogen-rich and taller competitors invade
  • Hot water discharges and organic wastes (sewage) reduce dissolved oxygen, killing aerobic organisms such as fish and insect larvae
  • Acid mine drainage and acid rain gases lower pH, denaturing proteins of exposed tissues (fish eggs, gills) and dissolving calcium exoskeletons (crayfish)
/ 6
07.2 (2 marks)
  • Reduces light penetration so submerged plants cannot photosynthesise
  • Blocks the gills of filter feeders such as bivalve molluscs; sediment smothers the bed
/ 2
07.3 (2 marks)
  • Rare species often have very specialised habitat requirements / narrow ranges of tolerance
  • A small change moves conditions outside their range while generalists survive; small populations cannot recover
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 22-23; AQA spec 3.1.2.2.
Question 810 marks · Conservation priorities, legislation & protocols · spec 3.1.2.3.2 · Genn pp. 30-31 · PRI-03
[4 marks]08.1Describe four ways in which the Wildlife and Countryside Act (1981) protects wildlife in the UK.
[4 marks]08.2Explain the difference between CITES Appendix I and Appendix II, giving an example species for each.
[2 marks]08.3Explain why controlling international trade can be more effective than trying to stop collection of a species in its own country.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Designation of protected areas (SSSIs, MCZs)
  • Protection of wild birds and their nests (except some pests / game birds)
  • Protection of named mammals such as otters, dormice, red squirrels, badgers and their setts
  • Uprooting wild plants generally illegal
  • Bats must not be disturbed; roof timber treatments must be non-toxic to bats
/ 4
08.2 (4 marks)
  • Appendix I: species threatened with extinction - all international (commercial) trade banned, except movement for conservation breeding
  • Examples: great apes, big cats, rhinos, blue whale
  • Appendix II: species that may become threatened if trade is not closely controlled - trade permitted under licence from countries where the species is well protected
  • Examples: Honduras mahogany, hippopotamus, green iguana, great white / basking shark, Venus fly trap
/ 4
08.3 (2 marks)
  • Collection may be hard to police in remote areas / poor countries, but if the animals or products cannot be exported / imported there is no market
  • Removing the profit removes the incentive to collect
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 30-31; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.2.

Question 11 — Essay (answer ONE)25 marks
[25 marks]11.1Discuss how an understanding of energy flow and the manipulation of food species can be used to increase food production sustainably.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Energy flow: autotrophs and heterotrophs; losses by reflection, transmission, respiration; 100 000 to 1 000 units; few food chains beyond four levels; plant food most productive; grazing animals where crops impossible; pigs on wastes
  • Energetics: intensive vs extensive, law of diminishing returns, energy subsidies (fertiliser, pesticides, irrigation, machinery, drying, processing, transport), energy ratios, food conversion ratios, dependence on fossil fuels
  • Control of genetics: asexual reproduction / cuttings / cloning, selective breeding and breeds, cross-breeding and hybrid vigour, AI and embryo transfer
  • GM: Roundup Ready soya, Bt crops, golden rice, omega-3 oilseed rape; advantages; risks (gene transfer, antibiotic markers, allergies, costs to LEDC farmers, loss of local diversity)
  • Sustainability links: energy dependence, trophic level of diet, feeding 12 billion people
  • Justified conclusion on the balance between productivity, efficiency and sustainability
