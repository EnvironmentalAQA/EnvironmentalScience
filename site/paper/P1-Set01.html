Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Humans have shifted the carbon cycle by burning fossil fuels and biomass, moving carbon from long-term stores into the atmosphere faster than photosynthesis and ocean uptake remove it, while deforestation reduces photosynthesis, ploughing and drainage speed aerobic decomposition, and paddies, landfills and livestock create new anaerobic sources of methane. The nitrogen cycle has changed as much: the Haber process has roughly doubled fixation, legume crops add more, drainage increases nitrification and reduces denitrification, combustion produces NOx, and leached fertiliser and sewage nitrate cause eutrophication, while nitrous oxide adds to warming.

Carbon can be counteracted by replacing fossil fuels with renewables and nuclear power, energy conservation, afforestation matched to deforestation and timber use, conserving peat bogs and forests, and increasing soil organic matter through low tillage and mulching, with carbon capture and storage as an unproven option. For nitrogen, catalytic converters and urea sprays reduce combustion NOx, biological fixation by legumes and rotation can replace manufactured nitrate, biological wastes can be managed as fertiliser, and low-solubility fertilisers, careful timing and buffer strips reduce leaching.

Some strategies are cheap and established - rotation, buffer strips, fertiliser timing - but others are expensive or untested, and the scale of fossil-fuel dependence, together with feedback and time lags, means the atmosphere will respond slowly. The strategies that mimic natural low-temperature cycling, as in the circular economy, are the most sustainable; the most effective is simply to leave carbon in the ground.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

In the troposphere human activity enhances the greenhouse effect: fossil fuels and deforestation add CO2, rice, livestock and landfill add methane, fertilisers add nitrous oxide, and CFCs and tropospheric ozone are new absorbers. More outgoing infrared is absorbed and re-emitted, warming the surface, while smoke and aerosols scatter sunlight and land-use change alters albedo and evapotranspiration. In the stratosphere, CFCs, halons and HCFCs release chlorine and bromine that catalyse ozone destruction, shifting the O / O2 / O3 equilibrium so that less UV is absorbed there and more reaches the surface, cooling the stratosphere as well.

The consequences run through every system the atmosphere's energy drives: climate, thermohaline circulation, the hydrological cycle, sea level and species distributions, plus UV damage to organisms. Predicting them is hard because the processes are linked - one change alters others - and because feedback can amplify (albedo loss, methane release) or damp (cloud cover, photosynthesis) the effect, sometimes past tipping points. Time lags from ocean heat capacity and the long residence times of CO2 and CFCs, natural variability from solar output, volcanoes and orbital cycles, sparse historical data replaced by proxies, and models built on different assumptions all add uncertainty.

Some things are nevertheless well established: the physics of infrared absorption, the chemistry of ozone loss, and the success of the Montreal Protocol. What is uncertain is the scale and timing of regional impacts, which is exactly why the precautionary principle, not certainty, should guide action.

  • Proxy data and their reliability: dendrochronology, coral growth rings, pollen in dated sediments, ice cores (gas bubbles, isotope ratios, volcanic ash layers, compaction problems)
  • Modern monitoring: long-term data sets (Mauna Loa), electronic equipment, satellites (LEO vs geostationary), Argo floats, remote sensing of ice thickness / area / sea level
  • Feedback mechanisms and tipping points adding uncertainty; ecological uncertainty (species survival, distribution, fragmentation)
  • Computer models: how they are tested and refined; importance of accurate representative data; scenario-based predictions and ranges
  • Uncertainty over ENSO / Conveyor / jet stream responses
  • Evaluation and conclusion: confidence in the overall trend and human cause despite detailed uncertainty; role of peer review and international bodies (IPCC)
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
