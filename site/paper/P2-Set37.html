Question 110 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-60 · HAB-06
[4 marks]01.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows reefs to support a high biomass in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[3 marks]01.2Describe the abiotic conditions needed by reef-building corals and explain why these make reefs vulnerable.
[3 marks]01.3Explain three ways in which coral reefs are important to humans.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
  • Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside polyp cells (up to 30 000 per mm2)
  • They use the polyp's CO2 and excretory wastes (nitrogen, phosphorus) for photosynthesis and growth, so nutrients are recycled efficiently
  • They supply the polyp with glucose and oxygen - over 95% of its food energy; the polyp provides a protected habitat and nutrients
  • Polyps also trap plankton with cilia and stinging nematocysts; surplus CO2 is used to build the calcium carbonate skeleton
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • High light (shallow, clear water) for the algae; warm stable temperatures (about 18-30 °C); low turbidity; constant salinity; well-oxygenated water
  • Found only where all these coincide (about 1% of ocean area)
  • Small changes (warming, sediment, freshwater runoff) move conditions outside the narrow range of tolerance
/ 3
01.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Fisheries: support over a quarter of marine species including fish, lobsters, conch
  • Erosion protection: complex structure dissipates wave energy protecting low coral islands; coral sand builds shores
  • Medicinal discoveries: AZT and Ara-C from sponges, dolastatin from sea hares, bryostatin from Bugula, trabectedin from sea squirts, secosteroids, coral for bone grafts
  • Climate control: carbonate skeletons sequester CO2
  • Tourism (diving, snorkelling)
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 57-60; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 210 marks · How humans influence biodiversity · spec 3.1.2.2 · Genn pp. 23-25 · HUM-03

Figure 1 shows the estimated populations of sea otters, sea urchins and the area of kelp forest along part of the coast of California between 1960 and 2010.

Figure 1
[3 marks]02.1Describe the relationship between the three variables shown in Figure 1.
[3 marks]02.2Explain the relationships shown in Figure 1.
[2 marks]02.3Give two other examples of human over-exploitation of one species affecting the population of another.
[2 marks]02.4Explain why the loss of beavers or forest elephants can cause the decline of many other species.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • As sea otters declined (1960-1980) sea urchins increased (to 350) and kelp area fell (to 25)
  • As otters recovered after 1990 urchins declined and kelp recovered
  • Otter and kelp trends are positively correlated; urchins negatively correlated with both
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
  • Sea otters are predators of sea urchins so fewer otters allowed the urchin population to rise
  • Sea urchins graze kelp so the kelp forest was over-grazed and destroyed
  • Kelp forests are habitat / food for many species including otters (keystone species / trophic cascade); otter recovery restored the balance
/ 3
02.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Over-fishing of sandeels reduced puffins
  • Over-collection of turtle eggs reduced turtles so jellyfish (their food) increased; over-fishing of sardines / herring (competitors) also increased jellyfish
  • Loss of pollinating bees (pesticides, wild flower loss) reduces plant reproduction; loss of elephants / large herbivores reduces seed dispersal of trees
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
  • They control abiotic features: beaver dams create ponds colonised by aquatic species; elephants create clearings and keep water holes open
  • Species that depend on these features (light, water, minerals) cannot survive in their absence
/ 2

Question 310 marks · Forest resources & deforestation · spec 3.5.3.3 · Genn pp. 368-369 · FOR-04
[4 marks]03.1Explain the effects of deforestation on soil.
[4 marks]03.2Explain how deforestation affects hydrology and climate.
[2 marks]03.3Give two ecological impacts of deforestation other than the direct loss of species.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (4 marks)
  • Less dead organic matter / leaf litter so less humus and slower soil formation; fewer nutrients returned
  • Loss of canopy and litter exposes soil to rain splash and wind
  • Decomposing roots no longer bind soil so erosion, gullying and landslides increase (especially on slopes)
  • Reduced organic matter and biota lower infiltration and water retention
/ 4
03.2 (4 marks)
  • Reduced interception and transpiration so less water returned to the atmosphere and less rainfall downwind
  • Increased runoff: more flooding after rain, lower flows in dry periods; sedimentation of rivers
  • Increased albedo of cleared land (reflects more sunlight)
  • Reduced carbon sequestration and loss of the carbon reservoir (CO2 released by burning / decay) contributing to climate change
/ 4
03.3 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Fragmentation of remaining forest into isolated patches with non-viable populations / edge effects
  • Loss of inter-species relationships (pollinators, seed dispersers) causing further declines
  • Loss of genetic resources / potential medicines before they are discovered
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 368-369; AQA spec 3.5.3.3.

[3 marks]05.1Use Table 1 to explain which site is more suitable for maize.
[3 marks]05.2Describe three ways in which temperature can be controlled to increase crop productivity.
[2 marks]05.3Explain why keeping livestock warm increases the amount of food converted to growth.
[2 marks]05.4Explain how day length affects livestock production and how it can be manipulated.
Show mark scheme
05.1 (3 marks)
  • B: frost-free period (150 days) exceeds the 120 needed whereas A (110) is too short - late frosts would kill seedlings
  • B is warmer / longer thermal growing season because south-facing slopes receive more insolation; valley floors are frost pockets where cold dense air collects
  • But B has lower rainfall and a steeper slope (erosion / machinery difficulties) so irrigation / contour cultivation may be needed
/ 3
05.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Greenhouses / polytunnels heated by solar gain or burning gas / paraffin (ventilated to prevent overheating)
  • Woven plastic cloches / fleece over rows to retain warm air near the ground
  • Burning oil / gas or using large fans in orchards to prevent frost damage to blossom
  • Site selection (aspect, avoiding frost pockets); heated buildings and shelter for livestock
/ 3
05.3 (2 marks)
  • Mammals and birds use food energy to maintain a constant body temperature (thermoregulation)
  • Reducing heat loss (shelter, heated buildings) means less food energy is used for heat and more is available for growth
/ 2
05.4 (2 marks)
  • Long days increase milk production and egg laying; poultry grow best with short days; sheep mate as days shorten in autumn
  • Artificial lighting extends the growing season for greenhouse crops or simulates autumn in spring to give sheep a second mating / lambing season
/ 2

07.1 (3 marks)
  • Diversity: HEP (wet winters), solar (summer) and wind peak at different times so supply is more reliable - diverse systems resist change

Question 810 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.2-3.1.1.3 · Genn pp. 8-9 · LIFE-03
[3 marks]08.1Explain how the development of biogeochemical cycles and of transpiration by land plants helped to maintain stable conditions for life.
[3 marks]08.2Describe three limitations of early methods of studying past conditions on Earth.
[4 marks]08.3Describe how each of the following modern techniques provides information about past conditions.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (3 marks)
  • Interconnected biological processes recycle nutrient elements so small amounts support life indefinitely without depletion; waste products of one process are raw materials of another so wastes do not build up
  • Transpiration returned water vapour to the atmosphere increasing rainfall elsewhere
  • Allowing more plant growth, further stabilising climate and soils
/ 3
08.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Lack of ancient historical / instrumental data
  • Reliance on proxy data (tree rings, pollen) of limited reliability for ancient conditions
  • Lack of sophisticated equipment / inability to measure many factors
  • Limited coordination between researchers; lack of data collection in many areas (oceans, poles)
/ 3
08.3 (4 marks)
  • The ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in the ice indicates the temperature when the snow fell
  • Air bubbles trapped in ice give the composition of the past atmosphere (CO2, methane)
  • Ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 dates organic material (eg pollen in sediments)
  • More magnesium is incorporated into calcite at higher temperatures, so the ratio estimates past sea temperature
/ 4

10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Succession: pioneers, seres (hydrosere, lithosere, psammosere), modification of abiotic conditions, climax communities controlled by climate; deflected succession and plagioclimaxes

  • Evaluation: uncertainties (natural fluctuations, unknown carrying capacity); examples (Yellowstone wolves, Scottish deer, chalk grassland); judgement

[25 marks]11.1Using tropical coral reefs as an example, discuss how the ecological features of a habitat determine both its importance and its vulnerability to human activities.
Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
  • Ecological features: cnidarian polyps, cilia, nematocysts, zooxanthellae symbiosis, limestone skeletons building reefs, complex structure and food web; specific abiotic requirements (temperature, light, salinity, low turbidity)
  • Importance: quarter of marine species, fisheries, medicines, carbon sink, erosion protection, tourism
  • Vulnerability: narrow abiotic tolerance means sensitivity to warming, acidification, sedimentation, nutrient pollution, oil, bleaching; slow growth so slow recovery; physical damage; overfishing and food-web effects (crown of thorns); introduced species; dependence on mangroves and seagrass
  • Conservation: MPAs, No Take Zones, fishing controls, tourism management, protection of linked habitats, CITES
  • Comparison with other habitats (rainforests, mangroves, Antarctica) where relevant
  • Evaluation of local vs global threats and justified conclusion

Indicative content
Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
