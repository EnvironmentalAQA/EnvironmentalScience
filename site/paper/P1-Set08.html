Question 210 marks · Renewable energy technologies · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 200-202 · REN-02
[3 marks]02.1Explain how a ground-source heat pump can deliver more heat energy to a building than the electrical energy used to run it.
[3 marks]02.2Explain how Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) with thermal storage overcomes the intermittency of solar power.
[2 marks]02.3Explain why CSP can only be used in areas with little cloud, whereas PV can be used anywhere.
[2 marks]02.4Describe two environmental impacts of solar PV.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • A fluid in a buried ground loop absorbs low-temperature heat from the ground; at the expansion valve the fluid boils at low pressure absorbing this heat
  • A compressor raises the pressure so the gas condenses, releasing heat at a higher temperature inside the building
  • The electricity only moves heat rather than creating it, so up to four times as much heat is delivered as electricity used (less if the source is colder)
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
  • Parabolic reflectors focus sunlight onto tubes of oil, increasing the energy density and reaching high temperatures
  • The heat is transferred to molten salt (potassium nitrate) stored in large insulated tanks at up to 550 °C
  • The stored heat boils water to drive steam turbines whenever electricity is required, including at night
/ 3
02.3 (2 marks)
  • Reflectors can only focus parallel rays of direct sunlight; cloud, dust and smoke scatter light so it cannot be concentrated
  • PV cells convert diffuse as well as direct light, though output is lower
/ 2
02.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Manufacture: extraction / processing of silicon, metals and plastics; toxic wastes (silicon tetrachloride, cadmium)
  • Land take by large solar farms competing with other uses / affecting desert flora and fauna
  • Water for cleaning panels in arid areas
  • Disposal of old panels
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 200-202; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
Question 310 marks · Mineral resources (the lithosphere) · spec 3.2.3.7 · Genn pp. 143-145 · MIN-07
[5 marks]03.1Describe how bioleaching is used to extract copper from low-grade ores or spoil heaps, and explain why it may be more sustainable than smelting.
[3 marks]03.2Describe the process of phytomining.
[2 marks]03.3Explain how polymer adsorption may provide a low-energy source of uranium in the future.
Show mark scheme
03.1 (5 marks)
  • Acidophilic bacteria (eg Acidithiobacillus) oxidise sulfide ores, producing sulfuric acid which dissolves the metal ions
  • Leachate (rainwater / recirculated solution) percolating through the spoil heap is collected; recirculation increases the metal concentration
  • Copper is recovered from solution by electrolysis or by displacement with scrap iron (iron is more reactive so copper is deposited as solid metal)
  • Smelting needs chemical reduction at high temperatures; energy needed rises rapidly as ore grade falls, so low-grade ores are uneconomic
  • Bioleaching works at low temperatures with low energy inputs, so it can exploit low-grade deposits and wastes (increasing reserves) - mimics natural low-temperature processes
/ 5
03.2 (3 marks)
  • Plants (hyperaccumulators) absorb metal ions from soil / water and concentrate them in their leaves
  • The vegetation is harvested and incinerated
  • The concentrated metal in the ash is dissolved in acid and separated by electrolysis; also decontaminates polluted sites
/ 3
03.3 (2 marks)
  • Uranium ions dissolved in seawater adsorb onto the surface of synthetic or natural polymers (lignin from wood, chitin from shrimp shells)
  • The polymers are collected and the uranium recovered, avoiding mining / high-energy extraction
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 143-145; AQA spec 3.2.3.7.
Question 410 marks · Properties of pollutants & environmental factors · spec 3.4.2.2 · Genn pp. 240-242, 255 · PPRO-04
[6 marks]04.1Explain how each of the following environmental factors affects the rate of degradation of pollutants. Give a named example of a pollutant for each factor.
[2 marks]04.2Explain how the presence of adsorbent materials such as clay can reduce the severity of pollution by heavy metals or radioactive caesium-137.
[2 marks]04.3Explain why a fall in pH increases the harm caused by many heavy metals.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (6 marks)
  • Temperature: most chemical / biological reactions are faster at higher temperature so degradable pollutants (eg sewage) break down faster; but faster decomposition may increase deoxygenation
  • Light: provides the activation energy for chemical reactions; photodegradation of pesticides (eg neonicotinoids break down fastest in sunlight); light drives photochemical smog reactions producing secondary pollutants
  • Oxygen: needed for aerobic bacterial decomposition of organic wastes / sewage; oxidation of sulfide ores produces sulfur dioxide / acid mine drainage; oxidation state affects the solubility of metals
/ 6
04.2 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Pollutant ions adsorb onto (the surface of) clay particles / organic matter in soil or sediment
  • So they are immobilised and cannot be absorbed by organisms / leached into water; radiation is absorbed in the ground
  • But they may be released later if conditions change (eg storms disturbing sediment, low pH)
/ 2
04.3 (2 marks)
  • Many heavy metals (eg lead, aluminium) are more soluble at low pH
  • So they are mobilised / leached from soil or sediments and can be absorbed by organisms / enter water bodies
/ 2
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 240-242, 255; AQA spec 3.4.2.2.

Figure 1 shows the concentrations of three pollutants in the air of a large city in a valley during one sunny day.

Figure 1
[4 marks]06.1Use Figure 1 to describe how the ozone concentration changes during the day and explain the pattern.
[2 marks]06.2Explain why the early-morning peak in NO is followed by a peak in NO2.
[2 marks]06.3Name one other toxic secondary pollutant produced in photochemical smogs and give one of its effects on humans.
[2 marks]06.4Explain why cities such as Los Angeles and Mexico City are particularly prone to photochemical smogs.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (4 marks)
  • Ozone is very low overnight / early morning, rises from about 08:00, peaks at about 80 units at 14:00 and falls in the evening
  • Ozone is a secondary pollutant formed by photochemical reactions requiring sunlight (UV), so it peaks in the afternoon after several hours of sunlight
  • NO2 (from morning traffic) is broken down by UV to NO and monatomic oxygen; monatomic oxygen reacts with O2 to form ozone
  • Ozone lags behind the NO / NO2 peaks (which are produced by the morning rush-hour traffic) because the reactions take time; falls at night when there is no sunlight
/ 4
06.2 (2 marks)
  • NO is the primary pollutant produced in engines when nitrogen and oxygen react at high temperature / pressure
  • NO is oxidised in the atmosphere (by oxygen / ozone) to NO2, a secondary pollutant
/ 2
06.3 (2 marks)
  • PANs (peroxyacetyl nitrates)
  • Eye irritation / breathing difficulty / asthma / emphysema / increased risk of heart attacks (toxic at much lower concentrations than NOx)
/ 2
06.4 (2 marks)
Any 2 from:
  • Heavy vehicle use giving high NOx and hydrocarbon emissions
  • Sunny climate providing the UV for photochemical reactions
  • Valley / basin topography and frequent temperature inversions trapping pollutants

Question 1010 marks · Fluctuations in supply/demand & energy storage · spec 3.3.4.1 · Genn pp. 217-224 · EST-06
[9 marks]10.1Explain why the development of energy storage technologies is essential if renewable energy resources are to provide most of a country's electricity.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by peak shaving.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Renewables are intermittent (solar at night, calm days, slack tides) and some unpredictable; demand fluctuates daily, weekly, seasonally and short-term; supply and demand rarely match
  • Without storage, surplus is wasted and shortages must be met by fossil-fuel peaking plants; large-scale electricity storage not yet possible directly so energy must be converted
  • Pumped-storage HEP: proven, rapid response, peak shaving, but limited sites
  • Batteries: lithium-ion for grid and vehicles; factors affecting viability; V2G
  • Hydrogen: electrolysis, storage methods, fuel cells, hydrogen economy, P2G into the gas grid; high energy density enables vehicle use
  • Compressed air with heat recovery; thermal storage (molten salt for CSP, high-volume stores, thermal mass); flywheels; supercapacitors
  • Grid management / ICT and diversity of resources (wind + solar + tidal + biofuel) reduce but do not remove the need for storage; energy conservation reduces peaks
  • Evaluation of efficiency losses, costs, resource limits (lithium), and which technologies suit short-term vs inter-seasonal storage; judgement
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • Storing surplus energy at times of low demand / high supply to meet later peaks in demand, so peak generating capacity can be reduced
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 217-224; AQA spec 3.3.4.1.
