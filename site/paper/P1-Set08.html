Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Deciding how much to control pollution starts with predicting where a pollutant will go. Critical Pathway Analysis uses the state of the effluent, wind and currents, geology, the effect of pH and oxygen on solubility, bioaccumulation and local food sources to predict whether a discharge will disperse harmlessly or concentrate somewhere sensitive; it is mainly used for radioactive discharges and means monitoring can focus on the sites at risk rather than sampling everywhere. Critical Group Monitoring then checks the exposure of the people most at risk from their lifestyle before any health effects occur: if their risk is acceptable everyone else's is lower, and if not, emissions are reduced.

The choice of control is guided by principles. The polluter pays principle gives a financial incentive to prevent damage, but only if penalties are high and polluters cannot escape responsibility. The precautionary principle assumes a waste is harmful until research shows otherwise, so ignorance is no defence. Because the cost of control rises steeply as emissions approach zero, emissions are set As Low As Reasonably Achievable using the Best Available Technology Not Entailing Excessive Cost. Where emission location and timing can be chosen (marine outfalls, downwind of towns, not over aquifers; avoiding temperature inversions) severity is reduced cheaply.

Overall, prevention of production (fuel desulfurisation) or of release (electrostatic precipitators, catalytic converters) is more effective than post-release remediation such as oil-spill clean-up, which never recovers all the pollutant. CPA depends on understanding pathways, and transboundary pollutants need international enforcement, but together these principles give a rational basis for deciding acceptable risk rather than aiming for an unaffordable zero.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Ionising radiation is used because it does things nothing else can: nuclear fission generates low-carbon electricity from fuel of extraordinary energy density; medicine relies on X-rays and CT scans, radiotherapy, tracers and gamma sterilisation of equipment; industry uses thickness gauges, cross-links polymers, tests turbine blades and explores for oil; and agriculture uses irradiation to preserve food and sterilise insect pests. The risks arise because radiation produces free radicals that damage DNA, causing mutation and cancer, with acute and chronic, somatic and gonadic effects; contamination is worse than exposure because the source stays with the person; and activation products, long-lived wastes, accidents at Chernobyl and Fukushima and weapons fallout add environmental risks.

Risk depends on the type of radiation - alpha is barely penetrating but has a weighting factor of 20 - on half-life, on dose in grays and sieverts and on the route of exposure. Weighing risks against benefits is difficult because cancers appear years later with many other possible causes, exposure data are poor, the people who benefit are often not those at risk, and benefits such as avoided surgery are hard to quantify.

Control follows ALARA and BATNEEC: closed sources, remote handling, shielding, distance under the inverse-square law, limiting time, protective clothing, decontamination and potassium iodide against iodine-131. Workers wear dosemeters and film badges and pass contamination monitors; discharges are managed by Critical Pathway Analysis and Critical Group Monitoring; wastes are vitrified, encapsulated or stored at licensed sites. With these controls the benefits generally outweigh the risks - natural radon supplies about half the average dose and the nuclear industry under one per cent - although public perception of risk remains far higher than the measured risk.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

A valid investigation begins with a clear, testable hypothesis and a null hypothesis, informed by existing knowledge, so that the data collected can actually accept or reject it. The first design decision is sample location: random sampling (grid coordinates or numbered sites) or systematic sampling along transects avoids the bias that would come from choosing convenient sites, and a preliminary study shows the interval needed to detect variation without collecting unnecessary data.

Timing matters because many variables change diurnally, seasonally or with weather, so samples on different occasions are needed and the interval must match the rate of change. Sample size and number depend on how variable the population is: a running mean shows when enough samples have been taken and the standard deviation shows the scatter around the mean. Techniques must be standardised so results from different sites, times or researchers are comparable, other variables controlled or monitored, and equipment calibrated so that precise readings are also accurate; anomalies should be repeated rather than ignored.

Finally the analysis must be chosen to suit the data - Spearman's rank for correlation, chi-squared for frequencies, a t-test for two means or Mann-Whitney U for medians - so that significance (p < 0.05) can be assessed, remembering that a significant correlation does not prove causation. Every decision trades reliability against cost, time and disturbance; sub-samples can only estimate the whole, so the most valid study is the one whose sampling design makes its limitations explicit.

Weaker answer · Level 2 (5 marks)

When planning fieldwork you need a hypothesis. You should choose sites randomly so it is not biased, for example using random numbers. You need to take enough samples and repeat them so the results are reliable. The method should be the same each time. You should measure accurately with calibrated equipment. Then you do a statistical test such as a t-test to see if the results are significant. If p is less than 0.05 the result is significant. This makes the conclusions valid.

  • Level 3 covers timing and the choice between statistical tests; Level 2 mentions one test only.
  • The evaluation is missing from Level 2 - there is no discussion of trade-offs or of correlation vs causation, so it cannot reach the top level however accurate it is.

Indicative content - students are not expected to cover all of these:
  • Problematic properties of solar: intermittency, unreliability from cloud, low energy density, changing angle; locational constraints (deserts, latitude)
  • Harnessing solar: photothermal, thermal stores, passive architecture, heat pumps, PV principles and cell efficiencies, solar farms; environmental impacts of manufacture and land use
  • New solar technologies: multijunction, anti-reflective surfaces, CSP with molten salt, PV-thermal hybrids, transparent PV, heliostats, self-cleaning
  • HEP: gravitational potential energy, locational constraints (catchment, rainfall, turbidity, impermeable rock, seismic, topography, land use, distance), impacts on habitats, sediment, methane, communities
  • Energy storage and matching supply to demand: pumped storage, batteries, grid links; combining resources
  • Evaluation and justified conclusion on how far these resources can replace fossil fuels

Indicative content - students are not expected to cover all of these:
  • Attributes: accuracy, precision, representative samples, reliability, anomalies, validity; hypothesis and null hypothesis; preliminary studies
  • Sampling design: random vs systematic location and avoiding bias; timing (diurnal, seasonal, long-term, weather); sample size and number and the running mean; standardisation of technique and control of other variables
  • Techniques for abiotic and biotic factors and their limitations (quadrats, transects, traps, mark-release-recapture assumptions, keys, meters and calibration)
  • Statistics: variability, standard deviation and error bars, significance and p-values, correlation vs causation; choosing Spearman's rank, chi-squared, t-test, Mann-Whitney U
  • Application to Paper 1 topics: monitoring pollution (noise, air quality, water), climate proxies, energy surveys, soil analysis
  • Evaluation of trade-offs (cost, time, disturbance) and justified conclusion
/ 25 (only the higher of the two essays counts)
