Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Sustainability matters because the population may reach 12 billion, land has been degraded since farming began in the fertile crescent, and current yields depend on artificial fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation that draws down groundwater, with little new land to cultivate. Pest control can move from chemicals to cultural methods - weeding, mulching, rotation, barrier and companion crops, habitats for predators - biological control, sterile males and pheromones, resistant varieties and integrated control, with fewer antibiotics in livestock.

Nutrients can come from natural processes: legumes fixing nitrogen, recycling manure and compost, rotation, permaculture and conserving soil biota and mycorrhizae, reducing manufactured nitrate and the non-renewable rock phosphate it accompanies. Energy use falls with low tillage, less machinery, renewable power, low food miles and lower methane from cattle diets and drought-tolerant rice. Water is conserved with low water-use crops, drip irrigation, reservoirs and aquifer recharge and by avoiding saline water; soil by maintaining organic matter and controlling erosion. Retaining hedgerows, ditches, ponds and woodland, conserving crop wild relatives in seed banks and agri-environment schemes protect the biodiversity that supplies pollination and pest control, while Fairtrade, local food and consumer choices address the social side.

The tension is between yield and sustainability: some strategies cost more or yield less in the short term and need policy support to be adopted. But since the unsustainable inputs themselves depend on finite fossil fuels and phosphate, the strategies that use natural processes are the only ones that can feed a larger population indefinitely.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

Wild catches have not increased since about 1990 because most stocks are fully or over-exploited, while aquaculture has grown rapidly and now approaches the wild catch, so the question is how far it can take over. Its advantages are real: limiting factors, breeding, gender and disease can be controlled, yields per area are high, and fish have low food conversion ratios because they need not support their weight or keep warm; herbivores such as tilapia and carp and filter-feeding molluscs need no fishmeal at all.

Its limits are equally real. The carnivorous species consumers prefer - salmon, shrimp - are fed fishmeal from wild forage fish, so their farming still depends on fishing and depletes species such as sandeels on which puffins rely; some species, such as bluefin tuna, will not breed in captivity. Farms destroy mangroves, release organic waste, pesticides and antibiotics that breed resistance, spread lice, allow escapes that contaminate wild gene pools and provoke predator control, though siting, closed systems, polyculture and vaccines can reduce these. Meanwhile fishing itself can be sustainable if quotas based on good stock data, gear design, size and effort limits and no-take zones are enforced.

Aquaculture can therefore supply most of the growth in demand, especially through herbivorous fish and shellfish, and can relieve pressure on wild stocks, but it cannot fully replace fishing while carnivorous species depend on wild fishmeal and some species cannot be farmed. Both must be managed sustainably; neither is a substitute for the other.

Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (8 marks)

Human society depends on other species first as resources: wood, fibres such as cotton, wool and silk, oils, fuels and, above all, food, with wild species such as the morama bean, perennial cereals, eland and bison offering new crops and livestock better adapted to local conditions. Biomimetics copies adaptations into vehicle design, architecture, structures, adhesives, materials and ultrasound; medicines such as taxol, quinine and AZT came from plants and animals, and physiological research on marsupials, hippos and sponges continues to yield medical insights. Wild species are pest-control agents - ladybirds, Encarsia, Cactoblastis, Bacillus thuringiensis - and crop wild relatives in Vavilov centres hold the genes future crops will need.

Less visibly, other species provide the services that keep the planet habitable: regulating atmospheric composition, driving the hydrological and biogeochemical cycles, maintaining soil, pollinating crops, dispersing seeds and providing habitat. Most species have never been investigated, so their value is unknown, and there are ethical, aesthetic and recreational reasons for conservation that owe nothing to usefulness.

Which arguments persuade decision-makers? Economic valuation of resources and services speaks the language of policy and has been most effective, but it undervalues the services that go unnoticed because they operate in dynamic equilibrium and it can never capture the value of species not yet studied. The strongest case combines the precautionary logic of unknown value with the intrinsic argument: conserving biodiversity protects both what we know we need and what we cannot yet know.

Indicative content - students are not expected to cover all of these:
  • Importance: resources (timber, fuel, food, fibres, medicines) and ecosystem services (atmospheric regulation, carbon store, hydrological regulation, climate, soil conservation, habitat, recreation)
  • Causes: agriculture (subsistence and cash crops), mineral extraction, reservoirs, urbanisation, transport, unsustainable timber, fuel and fodder exploitation above MSY; trends by region
  • Consequences: loss of resources, biodiversity and species, fragmentation; hydrological changes; soil impacts; climate impacts (albedo, carbon, rainfall)
  • Productivity vs biodiversity: non-indigenous species, monocultures, close planting, simple age structure, clear-felling
  • Sustainable management: MSY harvest rates, mixed indigenous species, mixed age structure, selective logging, traditional coppicing / pollarding, FSC certification, community involvement, protected areas, debt-for-nature, afforestation programmes (China); Rio Bravo case study
