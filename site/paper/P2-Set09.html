Question 110 marks · Importance of biodiversity: resources & ecosystem services · spec 3.1.2.1.1-3.1.2.1.2 · Genn pp. 10-15 · BIOV-01
[4 marks]01.1Explain what is meant by biomimetics and describe three examples.
[3 marks]01.2Explain why many plants produce chemicals that are useful as human medicines, and give two examples.
[2 marks]01.3Explain why the discovery of medicines is a strong argument for conserving whole habitats rather than individual species.
[1 mark]01.4Give one example of a wildlife species used in physiological research and the human health issue it helps to understand.
Show mark scheme
01.1 (4 marks)
Any 4 from:
  • Copying the structural / functional adaptations of living organisms in the design of manufactured items
  • Aircraft wingtip fins copied from splayed wingtip feathers of soaring birds, reducing turbulence and fuel use
  • Humpback whale flipper tubercles copied on ship rudders and wind turbine blades (tighter turns, rotation at lower wind speeds)
  • Shark skin ridges reducing friction / bacterial adhesion (surface coatings, hospital surfaces)
  • Termite mound convection copied for natural ventilation of buildings; hollow strutted bird bones in lightweight roofs; gecko toe adhesion; burr hooks - Velcro; lotus leaf self-cleaning glass; spider silk for airbags / body armour; bat / dolphin echolocation - ultrasound scanners
/ 4
01.2 (3 marks)
  • Plants produce toxic chemicals (eg alkaloids) to defend themselves against herbivores; in controlled doses these affect human physiology
  • Examples: morphine / codeine from poppies; quinine from cinchona bark (malaria); aspirin from willow bark; taxol from yew (cancers); diosgenin from Mexican yam (contraceptives, cortisone)
  • Animal examples accepted: AZT from a marine sponge (HIV), shark liver lipids (tumours)
/ 3
01.3 (2 marks)
  • Only a very small proportion of species have been studied for medicinal substances so unknown species may contain valuable chemicals
  • Conserving the habitat protects all its species, including those not yet investigated (and their interdependent relationships)
/ 2
01.4 (1 marks)
  • Squid giant nerve cells - nerve function / heart disease, stroke; marsupials (kangaroo, wallaby pouch) - embryo development; hippo skin secretion - sunscreen / burns; sea urchin embryos - teratogen testing; armadillo - leprosy; marine sponge proteins - transplant rejection
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 10-15; AQA spec 3.1.2.1.1-3.1.2.1.2.
Question 210 marks · Conditions for life on Earth · spec 3.1.1.2-3.1.1.3 · Genn pp. 8-9 · LIFE-03
[3 marks]02.1Explain how the development of biogeochemical cycles and of transpiration by land plants helped to maintain stable conditions for life.
[3 marks]02.2Describe three limitations of early methods of studying past conditions on Earth.
[4 marks]02.3Describe how each of the following modern techniques provides information about past conditions.
Show mark scheme
02.1 (3 marks)
  • Interconnected biological processes recycle nutrient elements so small amounts support life indefinitely without depletion; waste products of one process are raw materials of another so wastes do not build up
  • Transpiration returned water vapour to the atmosphere increasing rainfall elsewhere
  • Allowing more plant growth, further stabilising climate and soils
/ 3
02.2 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Lack of ancient historical / instrumental data
  • Reliance on proxy data (tree rings, pollen) of limited reliability for ancient conditions
  • Lack of sophisticated equipment / inability to measure many factors
  • Limited coordination between researchers; lack of data collection in many areas (oceans, poles)
/ 3
02.3 (4 marks)
  • The ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in the ice indicates the temperature when the snow fell
  • Air bubbles trapped in ice give the composition of the past atmosphere (CO2, methane)
  • Ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 dates organic material (eg pollen in sediments)
  • More magnesium is incorporated into calcite at higher temperatures, so the ratio estimates past sea temperature
/ 4
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 8-9; AQA spec 3.1.1.2-3.1.1.3.

Question 410 marks · Impacts of agriculture, social/political factors & sustainable agriculture · spec 3.5.1.5 · Genn pp. 335-337 · AGI-04
[9 marks]04.1Discuss the strategies that can be used to increase the sustainability of agriculture.
[1 mark]04.2State what is meant by permaculture.
Show mark scheme
04.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Why needed: growing population (to 12 billion), land degradation since the fertile crescent, reliance on artificial fertilisers, pesticides and unsustainable irrigation; limits to new land and imports

Question 610 marks · Ecological monitoring & new technologies · spec 3.1.2.3.5 · Genn pp. 393-394, 400-402 · MON-01

A conservation team is planning to protect a population of an endangered antelope in a reserve.

[8 marks]06.1Explain why each of the following features of the population should be measured before a conservation strategy is decided.
[2 marks]06.2Describe how the population size of the antelope could be estimated.
Show mark scheme
06.1 (8 marks)
  • Size: shows whether the population is viable / how urgent action is; sets a baseline to judge success; needed to estimate carrying capacity and sustainable off-take
  • Distribution: shows which habitats / areas are used and whether the population is fragmented into non-viable isolated groups needing corridors; identifies key areas to protect
  • Survival rate (mortality): identifies the life stage and causes of death (predation, disease, poaching) so management targets the limiting factor
  • Age structure: many young indicates a growing population; few young / ageing population indicates breeding failure or high juvenile mortality even if adults are numerous; k-selected species need breeding adults protected
/ 8
06.2 (2 marks)
  • Direct counts from vehicles / aircraft / drones along transects, or camera traps with individual recognition
  • Sub-sample counts in known areas scaled up to the whole reserve; mark-release-recapture (Lincoln index) for smaller species
/ 2

Question 710 marks · Habitat case studies: woodland, rainforest, reefs, islands, mangroves, Antarctica · spec 3.1.2.3.4 · Genn pp. 57-60 · HAB-06
[4 marks]07.1Explain how the relationship between coral polyps and zooxanthellae allows reefs to support a high biomass in nutrient-poor tropical seas.
[3 marks]07.2Describe the abiotic conditions needed by reef-building corals and explain why these make reefs vulnerable.
[3 marks]07.3Explain three ways in which coral reefs are important to humans.
Show mark scheme
07.1 (4 marks)
  • Zooxanthellae are photosynthetic algae living inside polyp cells (up to 30 000 per mm2)
  • They use the polyp's CO2 and excretory wastes (nitrogen, phosphorus) for photosynthesis and growth, so nutrients are recycled efficiently
  • They supply the polyp with glucose and oxygen - over 95% of its food energy; the polyp provides a protected habitat and nutrients
  • Polyps also trap plankton with cilia and stinging nematocysts; surplus CO2 is used to build the calcium carbonate skeleton
/ 4
07.2 (3 marks)
  • High light (shallow, clear water) for the algae; warm stable temperatures (about 18-30 °C); low turbidity; constant salinity; well-oxygenated water
  • Found only where all these coincide (about 1% of ocean area)
  • Small changes (warming, sediment, freshwater runoff) move conditions outside the narrow range of tolerance
/ 3
07.3 (3 marks)
Any 3 from:
  • Fisheries: support over a quarter of marine species including fish, lobsters, conch
  • Erosion protection: complex structure dissipates wave energy protecting low coral islands; coral sand builds shores
  • Medicinal discoveries: AZT and Ara-C from sponges, dolastatin from sea hares, bryostatin from Bugula, trabectedin from sea squirts, secosteroids, coral for bone grafts
  • Climate control: carbonate skeletons sequester CO2
  • Tourism (diving, snorkelling)
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 57-60; AQA spec 3.1.2.3.4.
Question 810 marks · Agroecosystems: abiotic & biotic factors · spec 3.5.1.1 · Genn pp. 314-318 · AGR-04

Table 1 shows the yield of a vegetable crop under different pest control methods over three years on one farm.

Table 1
MethodMean yield / t ha-1Pesticide applications per yearNon-target insect species recorded in field margins
Chemical insecticide only18.568
Crop rotation + beetle banks + hedgerows16.2031
Integrated control18.11-226
[4 marks]08.1Explain how crop rotation and beetle banks reduce pest damage.
[3 marks]08.2Use Table 1 to evaluate integrated control compared with the other two methods.
[3 marks]08.3Describe how the sterile male technique controls insect pests and explain why it is species-specific.
Show mark scheme
08.1 (4 marks)
  • Rotation: each crop has its own pests; growing a different crop each year (4-5 year cycle) means pests left at the end of a season die before that crop returns, so populations cannot build up early
  • Beetle banks / hedgerows provide habitat and overwintering sites for natural predators (ground beetles, ladybirds) that eat aphids
  • Rotation also evens out nutrient demands and includes legumes
/ 4
08.2 (3 marks)
  • Integrated control gives almost the same yield as chemical control (18.1 vs 18.5) with far fewer applications (1-2 vs 6)
  • It retains most of the biodiversity of cultural control (26 vs 31 species) whereas chemical control alone leaves only 8
  • Cultural control alone loses about 12% yield; integrated control combines cultural methods, resistant varieties and carefully timed specific non-persistent pesticides only when essential - best balance of yield, cost and impact
/ 3
08.3 (3 marks)
  • Large numbers of males are sterilised with gamma radiation and released
  • Females of many insects mate only once and store sperm, so mating with a sterile male produces no offspring; enough sterile males reduce fertile matings below replacement level (screw worm fly, Mediterranean fruit fly)
  • Only the target species' females mate with them so non-target species are unaffected; fails if sterilised males behave abnormally or re-colonisation occurs
/ 3
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 314-318; AQA spec 3.5.1.1.

Question 1010 marks · Aquaculture · spec 3.5.2 · Genn pp. 338-359 · AQUA-05
[9 marks]10.1Evaluate the extent to which aquaculture can replace fishing as a source of aquatic food.
[1 mark]10.2State what is meant by a fed aquaculture species.
Show mark scheme
10.1 (9 marks)
Levels of response: L3 7-9 comprehensive, well-structured, applied to context, supported judgement; L2 4-6 sound but incomplete application/evaluation; L1 1-3 basic, limited structure.
Indicative content:
  • Context: wild catches have not increased since about 1990 as stocks are fully or over-exploited; aquaculture output growing rapidly and approaching the wild catch; fish are the last large-scale hunted food
  • Advantages of aquaculture: control of limiting factors, selective breeding, gender control, high yields per area; fish have low FCRs (no thermoregulation / support) compared with livestock; herbivores (tilapia, carp) and molluscs very efficient; polyculture, IMTA, aquaponics, rice-fish
  • Limits: carnivorous species (salmon, shrimp) need fishmeal from wild fish so still depend on fishing and deplete forage fish (sandeels - puffins); some species (bluefin tuna) will not breed in captivity; consumer preference for carnivores
  • Environmental impacts: habitat loss (mangroves), organic pollution, pesticides, antibiotics and resistance, lice, escapes and gene pool contamination, predator control; and how they can be reduced
  • Fishing can be sustainable with quotas, gear design, NTZs, size limits, effort limits; MSY management needs good data
  • Judgement: aquaculture can supply much of the growth in demand, especially herbivores and shellfish, but cannot fully replace fishing; both need sustainable management
/ 9
10.2 (1 marks)
  • A species that is given artificial food (pellets) rather than relying on natural food in the water, eg salmon, shrimp
/ 1
Source: R. Genn, Environmental Science A level AQA (Insight & Perspective, 2018), pp. 338-359; AQA spec 3.5.2.

Levels: L5 21-25 · L4 16-20 · L3 11-15 · L2 6-10 · L1 1-5. Reward breadth across the specification, accurate detail, application to the question and a supported conclusion.
