Model answers: what a Level 3 answer looks like
Model answer · Level 3 (9 marks)

New technologies have transformed what can be monitored and how often. Image databases of unique markings - tiger stripes, whale-shark spots, dolphin fin scars - let individuals be recognised, giving territory size, movements, lifespan and social structure without repeated capture, though only for species with unique features. Motion-sensitive and infrared cameras and CCTV record activity day and night with far less disturbance than observers, especially at nests.

Genetics adds detection where nothing can be seen: eDNA in water reveals great crested newts or invasive carp from shed cells, and DNA databases identify which regional population timber, ivory or fish came from, supporting enforcement. Bat detectors and sonograms give presence, abundance and activity of species heard but not seen. Position monitoring uses radio, GPS and acoustic transmitters, or tiny geolocator tags for birds too small to carry a transmitter, while data recorders on drones, AUVs, balloons, aircraft, satellites or the animals themselves collect abiotic data. Satellites map land use and deforestation in visible light, vegetation density and temperature in infrared, and wind, waves, sea level and oil slicks with radar that works through cloud.

The gains are coverage, continuity and reduced disturbance. The limits are cost, battery life and weight, the need to calibrate sensors and ground-truth remote data, ethical questions about tagging, and the fact that many species still require traditional methods and indirect evidence. New technology therefore complements rather than replaces fieldwork, but for rare, remote or nocturnal species it has made effective conservation possible where it was not before.

Weaker answer · Level 1 (3 marks)

New technologies help monitor wildlife. Cameras can take pictures of animals at night. GPS collars can track where animals go. Satellites can see deforestation from space. DNA can tell which animals are present. Bat detectors can hear bats. These help conservation because we know where animals are and how many there are. They can be expensive though.

What makes the difference
  • Level 1 lists technologies with no detail of how they work or what data they give; Level 3 links each to the information it provides (territory size, presence, activity).
  • Level 3's evaluation considers several limitations (calibration, ground-truthing, ethics, species coverage) and reaches a balanced judgement; Level 1 has one word - 'expensive'.
  • Structure: Level 3 groups by imagery, genetics, sound, position and remote sensing; the list in Level 1 has no organisation.
